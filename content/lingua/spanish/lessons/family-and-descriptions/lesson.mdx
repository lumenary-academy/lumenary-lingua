<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="People you love, in Spanish">

Talking about family is one of the first things people do when they get to know each other, in any language. In this lesson you'll learn the words for your relatives, the verb **tener** in full (you already know *tengo … años* from <LessonLink slug="numbers-and-time">lesson 3</LessonLink>), the words for *my*, *your*, *our*, and a whole set of describing words. At the end, you'll meet Spanish's second verb "to be", **estar**, and see how it differs from **ser**.

</Section>

<Section id="tener" kind="grammar" title="Tener: to have">

| | tener | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yo | <Say lang="es">tengo</Say> | <Say lang="es">Tengo dos hermanas.</Say> |
| tú | <Say lang="es">tienes</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Tienes hijos?</Say> |
| él, ella, usted | <Say lang="es">tiene</Say> | <Say lang="es">Mi abuela tiene un gato.</Say> |
| nosotros, nosotras | <Say lang="es">tenemos</Say> | <Say lang="es">Tenemos un perro.</Say> |
| vosotros, vosotras | <Say lang="es">tenéis</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Tenéis primos aquí?</Say> |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | <Say lang="es">tienen</Say> | <Say lang="es">Ellos tienen tres hijos.</Say> |

(With *vos*: <Say lang="es-AR">tenés</Say>.) Notice the pattern: the stressed *e* in the stem becomes *ie* in four forms (*tienes, tiene, tienen*), but not in *tenemos* and *tenéis*, where the stress falls elsewhere. You'll see this pattern in many verbs in <LessonLink slug="daily-routine">lesson 5</LessonLink>. *Tengo* is simply irregular.

Uses of *tener* so far:

- **having things and people:** <Say lang="es">Tengo un hermano y una hermana.</Say>
- **age:** <Say lang="es">Mi abuelo tiene ochenta años.</Say>
- **physical features:** <Say lang="es">Tiene los ojos verdes y el pelo corto.</Say> (He has green eyes and short hair.) Spanish uses *el, los* here where English uses nothing.

In questions and negatives with *tener*, Spanish often leaves out the article: <Say lang="es">¿Tienes hermanos?</Say> (Do you have any brothers or sisters?), <Say lang="es">No tengo hijos.</Say> (I don't have children.)

**Possession with de.** Spanish has no *'s*. "Ana's mother" is <Say lang="es">la madre de Ana</Say>, literally "the mother of Ana".

</Section>

<Section id="possessives" kind="grammar" title="My, your, his, our: possessive adjectives">

| | One thing | Several things |
| --- | --- | --- |
| my | <Say lang="es">mi</Say> hermano | <Say lang="es">mis</Say> hermanos |
| your (*tú*) | <Say lang="es">tu</Say> casa | <Say lang="es">tus</Say> casas |
| his, her, its, your (*usted*) | <Say lang="es">su</Say> perro | <Say lang="es">sus</Say> perros |
| our | <Say lang="es">nuestro</Say> padre, <Say lang="es">nuestra</Say> madre | <Say lang="es">nuestros</Say> tíos, <Say lang="es">nuestras</Say> tías |
| your (*vosotros*) | <Say lang="es">vuestro</Say> hijo, <Say lang="es">vuestra</Say> hija | <Say lang="es">vuestros</Say> hijos, <Say lang="es">vuestras</Say> hijas |
| their, your (*ustedes*) | <Say lang="es">su</Say> abuela | <Say lang="es">sus</Say> abuelas |

The key idea: **a possessive agrees with the thing possessed, not with the owner.** *Mis* is plural because there are several brothers, even though there is one "me": <Say lang="es">mis hermanos</Say>. *Nuestra* is feminine because *madre* is feminine, whoever "we" are.

*Tu* (your) has no accent; *tú* (you) does: <Say lang="es">Tú tienes tu libro.</Say> (You have your book.)

**Su can mean a lot.** *Su madre* may be his, her, your (*usted*), their or your (*ustedes*) mother. Usually the context makes it clear. When it doesn't, use *de*: <Say lang="es">la madre de él</Say>, <Say lang="es">la madre de ella</Say>, <Say lang="es">la madre de usted</Say>.

</Section>

<Section id="adjectives" kind="grammar" title="Describing: adjectives and colours">

### Agreement

You already know how nationalities agree from <LessonLink slug="who-you-are">lesson 2</LessonLink>. All adjectives work the same way:

| Ending | Forms | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -o | -o, -a, -os, -as | *un chico alto, una chica alta, unos chicos altos, unas chicas altas* |
| -e, -ista | one form, plural -s | *un hombre amable, unas mujeres amables*; *un niño optimista* |
| most consonants | one form, plural -es | *un padre joven, unos padres jóvenes*; *un ojo azul, ojos azules* |
| -or | add -a for feminine | *trabajador, trabajadora, trabajadores, trabajadoras* |

Note the spelling change in *feliz* (happy) → *felices*, like *lápiz → lápices*, and the accent that appears in *jóvenes* to keep the stress on *jo-*.

### Position

Most describing adjectives go **after** the noun, the opposite of English: <Say lang="es">un gato negro</Say> (a black cat), <Say lang="es">una familia grande</Say> (a big family). Words of quantity, like numbers, go before: *dos hermanos*.

A few common adjectives often go **before** the noun, and some shorten there:

- *bueno* → **buen**, *malo* → **mal** before a masculine singular noun: <Say lang="es">un buen amigo</Say> (a good friend), <Say lang="es">un mal día</Say> (a bad day). The feminine doesn't shorten: *una buena amiga*.
- *grande* → **gran** before any singular noun, and the meaning changes: <Say lang="es">una ciudad grande</Say> is a big city; <Say lang="es">una gran ciudad</Say> is a great city.

To strengthen an adjective, put **muy** (very) before it: <Say lang="es">Mi abuela es muy simpática.</Say>

### Colours

| Agree like *alto* | One form, plural -s or -es | Usually don't change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">rojo</Say> red | <Say lang="es">verde</Say> green | <Say lang="es">rosa</Say> pink |
| <Say lang="es">amarillo</Say> yellow | <Say lang="es">azul</Say> blue | <Say lang="es">naranja</Say> orange |
| <Say lang="es">blanco</Say> white | <Say lang="es">gris</Say> grey | <Say lang="es">café</Say> brown (Mexico, Central America) |
| <Say lang="es">negro</Say> black | <Say lang="es">marrón</Say> brown (Spain and elsewhere) | |
| <Say lang="es">morado</Say> purple | | |

So: *una casa blanca, dos casas blancas*; *un coche verde, dos coches verdes* (a green car, two green cars). *Rosa* and *naranja* are also nouns (a rose, an orange) and are often left unchanged: *unas camisetas rosa* (some pink T-shirts), though *rosas* is also heard. For hair, Spanish usually says *castaño* (brown, chestnut) rather than *marrón*: <Say lang="es">Tiene el pelo castaño y los ojos marrones.</Say>

</Section>

<Section id="ser-estar" kind="grammar" title="Ser or estar? A first look">

Spanish has two verbs that translate "to be". You know **ser** from lesson 2. Here is **estar**:

| | estar |
| --- | --- |
| yo | <Say lang="es">estoy</Say> |
| tú | <Say lang="es">estás</Say> |
| él, ella, usted | <Say lang="es">está</Say> |
| nosotros, nosotras | <Say lang="es">estamos</Say> |
| vosotros, vosotras | <Say lang="es">estáis</Say> |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | <Say lang="es">están</Say> |

You've been using it since lesson 1: *¿Cómo estás?* asks how you are *right now*. That is the heart of the difference, at this stage:

- **ser** says what someone or something **is like**: identity, nationality, profession, and characteristics such as personality, appearance, size and colour. <Say lang="es">Mi madre es alta y muy simpática.</Say>
- **estar** says **how** someone or something **is** at the moment: feelings, health, mood, tiredness. <Say lang="es">Hoy mi madre está cansada.</Say>

Some adjectives work with both, and the verb changes the meaning:

| With *ser*: what he is like | With *estar*: how he is now |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">Pablo es serio.</Say> Pablo is a serious person. | <Say lang="es">Pablo está serio.</Say> Pablo is looking serious (today). |
| <Say lang="es">Es aburrido.</Say> He's boring. | <Say lang="es">Está aburrido.</Say> He's bored. |

*Estar* also tells you **where** something is: <Say lang="es">Mi hermana no está en la foto.</Say> (My sister isn't in the photo.) We'll return to location in <LessonLink slug="food-and-places">lesson 6</LessonLink>, and to the finer points of *ser* and *estar* later in the course. For now, the rule "*ser* for what it's like, *estar* for how it is right now" will serve you well.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="A photo on a phone">

Valentina, from Medellín in Colombia, and Min-jun, from South Korea, are classmates at a language school in Bogotá. During a break, Valentina shows him a photo.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Valentina | <Say lang="es">Min-jun, ¿tienes hermanos?</Say> | Min-jun, do you have brothers or sisters? |
| Min-jun | <Say lang="es">Sí, tengo una hermana menor. Es muy simpática. ¿Y tú?</Say> | Yes, I have a younger sister. She's very nice. And you? |
| Valentina | <Say lang="es">Tengo dos hermanos y una hermana. Mira, esta es mi familia.</Say> | I have two brothers and a sister. Look, this is my family. |
| Min-jun | <Say lang="es">¡Qué familia tan grande! ¿Quién es el señor alto?</Say> | What a big family! Who's the tall man? |
| Valentina | <Say lang="es">Es mi papá, Andrés. Es profesor y es muy divertido.</Say> | That's my dad, Andrés. He's a teacher and he's really funny. |
| Min-jun | <Say lang="es">¿Y la señora de pelo rizado?</Say> | And the lady with curly hair? |
| Valentina | <Say lang="es">Es mi mamá. Es médica. En la foto está un poco cansada.</Say> | That's my mum. She's a doctor. In the photo she's a bit tired. |
| Min-jun | <Say lang="es">¿Y los dos chicos son tus hermanos?</Say> | And the two boys are your brothers? |
| Valentina | <Say lang="es">Sí. Mateo es el mayor, tiene veintiocho años. Sebastián tiene quince.</Say> | Yes. Mateo is the eldest, he's twenty-eight. Sebastián is fifteen. |
| Min-jun | <Say lang="es">Y la chica rubia, ¿es tu hermana?</Say> | And the blonde girl, is she your sister? |
| Valentina | <Say lang="es">No, es mi prima Camila. Mi hermana no está en la foto.</Say> | No, that's my cousin Camila. My sister isn't in the photo. |
| Min-jun | <Say lang="es">Tus padres son muy jóvenes.</Say> | Your parents are very young. |
| Valentina | <Say lang="es">¡Gracias! ¿Y tu familia? ¿Cómo es?</Say> | Thanks! And your family? What's it like? |
| Min-jun | <Say lang="es">Es pequeña: mis padres, mi hermana y yo. Y nuestro perro, Kimchi. Es negro y un poco gordo.</Say> | It's small: my parents, my sister and me. And our dog, Kimchi. He's black and a bit fat. |

**Notes.** *Mira* means "look" (you'll learn the form in lesson 11). *¡Qué familia tan grande!* is a handy pattern: *¡Qué* + noun + *tan* + adjective*!*, "What a … !". *¿Cómo es?* asks what something is *like* (characteristics, so *ser*); compare *¿Cómo está?*, how is it now. *Rizado* means curly, and *pequeño* small. *Gordo* (fat) is fine for a dog, but think twice before using it about a person.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Pero and perro, caro and carro">

Family and description words give you plenty of practice with the two Spanish *r* sounds from lesson 1:

- **one tap**: <Say lang="es">pero</Say> (but), <Say lang="es">caro</Say> (expensive), <Say lang="es">moreno</Say>, <Say lang="es">primo</Say>, <Say lang="es">madre</Say>
- **trill**: <Say lang="es">perro</Say> (dog), <Say lang="es">carro</Say> (car, in Latin America), <Say lang="es">pelirrojo</Say>, and every *r* at the start of a word: <Say lang="es">rubio</Say>, <Say lang="es">rojo</Say>, <Say lang="es">rizado</Say>

Also listen for the **soft d** between vowels in *cansado*, *divertido* and *morado*: the tongue barely touches the teeth, close to the *th* of *this*. At the start of a word or after *n* or *l*, the *d* is firm: *divertido* begins with a firm *d* and ends with a soft one.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Family, near and wide">

In much of the Spanish-speaking world, "family" is a wide circle. Grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins are often part of everyday life, and a long Sunday lunch with several generations around the table is a familiar scene in Spain and Latin America alike. It is also common for young adults to live with their parents into their late twenties or beyond, for reasons that include the cost of housing as much as custom.

Godparents matter too. A child's *padrino* (godfather) and *madrina* (godmother) are chosen at baptism, and in Mexico and other countries the parents and godparents call each other *compadre* and *comadre*, "co-father" and "co-mother", a bond that can last a lifetime. You'll also hear family words used for people who aren't relatives: Mexicans may call a friend's child *mijo* or *mija* (from *mi hijo, mi hija*), and in Spain friends call each other *tío* and *tía*.

<Reflection title="Padre nuestro">

The best-known Spanish sentence for many people is the opening of the Lord's Prayer, which in an old public-domain translation reads "*Padre nuestro que estás en los cielos, santificado sea tu nombre*" (Mateo 6:9, Reina-Valera 1909): "Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name". You can now recognise almost every word: *padre*, *nuestro* (here placed after the noun, a pattern you will learn later), *estás* (estar, for *where* someone is), and *tu nombre*. And notice again that the prayer speaks to God as *tú*, with the family form.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"*Mis padres* means 'my fathers'." It means **my parents**. The masculine plural covers a mixed group: *los hermanos* can be brothers and sisters, *los hijos* sons and daughters, *los tíos* aunts and uncles.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"The possessive agrees with the owner." It agrees with the thing owned. A man talking about his sister says *mi hermana*; a woman talking about her two brothers says *mis hermanos*; *nuestra casa* is feminine because *casa* is.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"I'm tired" is *Soy cansado*. Tiredness is a state, so it's **Estoy cansado** (or *cansada*). *Ser* is for what someone is like: *Soy alto, soy trabajadora*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Adjectives go before the noun, as in English." Usually they go after: *una casa blanca*, *ojos verdes*. Only a few, like *buen*, *mal* and *gran*, normally go before.

</Misconception>

</Section>
