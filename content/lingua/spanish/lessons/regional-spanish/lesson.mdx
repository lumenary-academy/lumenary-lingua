<Section id="vocabulary" kind="vocabulary" title="One language, many words">

Throughout this course you have met small notes on how Spanish varies: *móvil* and *celular*, *piso* and *departamento*, the present perfect in Madrid and the preterite in Monterrey. This last B2 lesson looks at that variety directly. None of the varieties below is better or more correct than another: each is the educated standard of its own community, and speakers of all of them understand each other.

<Section id="address" kind="grammar" title="Tú, vos, usted, vosotros, ustedes">

The biggest grammatical difference between varieties lies in how you say *you*.

| | Spain (most) | Most of Latin America | Voseo regions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| one person, informal | *tú* | *tú* | *vos* |
| one person, formal | *usted* | *usted* | *usted* |
| several people, informal | *vosotros, vosotras* | *ustedes* | *ustedes* |
| several people, formal | *ustedes* | *ustedes* | *ustedes* |

### Vosotros

**Vosotros** (feminine *vosotras*) is the informal plural in most of Spain. In Latin America, and in the Canary Islands and parts of Andalusia, *ustedes* covers both formal and informal plural. Latin Americans understand *vosotros* perfectly, from books, films and the Bible, but don't use it in conversation.

| | hablar | comer | vivir |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present | *habláis* | *coméis* | *vivís* |
| Preterite | *hablasteis* | *comisteis* | *vivisteis* |
| Command | *¡hablad!* | *¡comed!* | *¡vivid!* |
| Pronouns | *os* (object), *vuestro, vuestra* (possessive) | | |

With a reflexive command the final *-d* drops: *sentad + os → **sentaos***, *levantaos*. <Say lang="es-ES">¿Vosotros venís a la cena? Os esperamos a las nueve.</Say>

### Voseo

**Vos** replaces *tú* as the informal singular in Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay; in most of Central America (Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Honduras, El Salvador, Guatemala); and in parts of Colombia, Venezuela, Bolivia, Ecuador and southern Mexico. It is not slang: it is the normal, educated form in those places, used by presidents and novelists as well as teenagers.

In the variety of Buenos Aires and Montevideo, and in much of Central America, the present and the command are formed like this:

| | tú | vos |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hablar | *hablas* | *hablás* |
| comer | *comes* | *comés* |
| vivir | *vives* | *vivís* |
| ser | *eres* | *sos* |
| tener | *tienes* | *tenés* |
| querer | *quieres* | *querés* (no stem change) |
| poder | *puedes* | *podés* (no stem change) |
| Command: venir | *¡ven!* | *¡vení!* |
| Command: decir | *¡di!* | *¡decí!* |
| Command: ir | *¡ve!* | *¡andá!* (in Argentina and Uruguay) |

The stress falls on the ending, which is why there is no stem change and why the forms carry accents. Other points:

- After a preposition, use *vos*: *para vos, con vos* (not *contigo*).
- The object and reflexive pronoun is still **te**, and the possessive is still **tu**: <Say lang="es-AR">Vos te llamás Sofía, ¿no? ¿Querés venir con tu hermano?</Say>
- In the preterite, the *tú* form is used: *vos hablaste*.
- In the present subjunctive, the *tú* forms (*que vengas*) are the most common in Argentina; forms like *que vengás* also exist.

In informal Chilean speech you will also hear a "verbal" voseo with *tú* as the pronoun but different verb endings: *¿Cómo estái? ¿Qué querís?*

</Section>

<Section id="leismo" kind="grammar" title="Leísmo">

In the standard system, **lo, la, los, las** are direct object pronouns, and **le, les** are indirect:

- *A Juan **lo** vi ayer.* — I saw Juan yesterday. (direct)
- *A María **la** vi ayer.* (direct)
- ***Le** di el libro a Juan.* — I gave Juan the book. (indirect)

In central and northern Spain many speakers use **le** instead of *lo* when the direct object is a **man or boy**: <Say lang="es-ES">A Juan le vi ayer.</Say> This is called **leísmo**, and the academies accept it for a masculine singular person. Other uses are not accepted as standard:

| Use | Status |
| --- | --- |
| *A Juan le vi.* (masculine person) | Accepted; common in Spain |
| *Le saludo atentamente.* (to *usted*, man or woman) | Accepted everywhere, especially in formal letters |
| *A mis hermanos les vi.* (masculine plural) | Less accepted |
| *A María le vi.* (feminine person) | Not standard |
| *El libro le compré ayer.* (a thing) | Not standard |

You may also hear **laísmo**, *la* for an indirect object with a woman (*La dije que viniera*), in colloquial speech in parts of central Spain. It isn't standard; say ***Le** dije que viniera*.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Seseo, distinción, yeísmo and rehilamiento">

### Seseo, distinción and ceceo

How do you pronounce the *z* in *caza* (hunt) and the *s* in *casa* (house)?

- **Distinción** (most of Spain): *z* and *c* before *e, i* sound like English *th* in *think*; *s* sounds like *s*. *Casa* and *caza* are different: <Say lang="es-ES">casa, caza</Say>.
- **Seseo** (all of Latin America, the Canary Islands and parts of Andalusia): both are pronounced *s*. *Casa* and *caza* sound the same: <Say lang="es-MX">casa, caza</Say>. This is how the great majority of Spanish speakers in the world speak.
- **Ceceo** (parts of Andalusia): both are pronounced with a sound close to *th*.

All three are legitimate. As a learner, choose one and be consistent.

### Yeísmo and rehilamiento

Historically, *ll* (*pollo*, chicken) and *y* (*poyo*, stone bench) were different sounds. Today the vast majority of speakers pronounce them the same, a merger called **yeísmo**: <Say lang="es">pollo, poyo</Say>, <Say lang="es">calló, cayó</Say>. A distinct *ll*, close to the *lli* of English *million*, survives in parts of the Andes, in Paraguay and among some speakers in northern Spain.

In Argentina and Uruguay, *ll* and *y* are pronounced with friction: like the *s* in English *measure*, or, especially among younger speakers in Buenos Aires, like English *sh*. This is called **rehilamiento**: <Say lang="es-AR">Yo me llamo Yolanda y vivo en la calle Mayo.</Say>

### Two more you will hear

- In the Caribbean, Andalusia, the Canary Islands, Argentina, Chile and many coastal areas, an *s* at the end of a syllable is often softened to an *h* sound or dropped: *los amigos* sounds like *loh amigoh*.
- The *j* (and *g* before *e, i*) is a strong, raspy sound in most of Spain and in Mexico, but a soft breath like English *h* in the Caribbean, Colombia, Central America and much of Andalusia: <Say lang="es">jamón, gente</Say>.

</Section>

<Section id="reading" kind="reading" title="Reading: Un idioma, muchas casas">

**A short essay, written for this lesson.**

> <Say lang="es">El español es la lengua materna de unos quinientos millones de personas. La mayoría vive en América, pero el idioma nació en la península ibérica y hoy se habla en cuatro continentes.</Say>
>
> <Say lang="es">En Estados Unidos, decenas de millones de personas hablan español en casa. Hay barrios de Los Ángeles, Miami, Nueva York o San Antonio en los que se puede pasar el día entero sin decir una palabra en inglés. Allí conviven acentos mexicanos, cubanos, puertorriqueños, dominicanos, salvadoreños y de muchos otros países, y el contacto con el inglés ha creado expresiones propias: muchos dicen «te llamo para atrás» por «te devuelvo la llamada», o pasan de una lengua a otra en la misma frase. Algunos lo llaman espanglish; los lingüistas prefieren hablar de contacto de lenguas y de alternancia de códigos, algo normal en cualquier comunidad bilingüe. El país tiene incluso su propia academia, la Academia Norteamericana de la Lengua Española.</Say>
>
> <Say lang="es">En África, Guinea Ecuatorial es el único país en el que el español es lengua oficial. Fue colonia española hasta 1968, y hoy el español convive allí con el francés y el portugués, también oficiales, y con lenguas africanas como el fang y el bubi, que muchas familias hablan en casa. El español ecuatoguineano tiene rasgos propios de pronunciación y de vocabulario, y el país cuenta con su propia academia de la lengua.</Say>
>
> <Say lang="es">¿Cuál es, entonces, el mejor español? La pregunta no tiene respuesta, igual que no la tiene la de cuál es el mejor inglés. Una madrileña, un mexicano, una argentina y un ecuatoguineano pueden hablar durante horas y entenderse sin problemas, aunque cada uno llame de una forma distinta al autobús. Las diferencias no separan el idioma: son la prueba de que está vivo.</Say>

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *la lengua materna* | mother tongue |
| *decenas de millones* | tens of millions |
| *convivir* | to live side by side, to coexist |
| *devolver la llamada* | to call back |
| *el espanglish* | Spanglish (also spelled *spanglish*) |
| *la alternancia de códigos* | code-switching |
| *el fang, el bubi* | two Bantu languages of Equatorial Guinea |
| *ecuatoguineano* | from Equatorial Guinea |
| *el rasgo* | feature, trait |
| *contar con* | to have, to count on |
| *la prueba* | proof |

**Things to notice.** The text uses structures from earlier B2 lessons: impersonal *se* (*se puede pasar el día*, *se habla*), relative clauses (*en los que, que muchas familias hablan*), *aunque* + subjunctive for an irrelevant fact (*aunque cada uno llame…*), and the contrast between *nació* (preterite) and *ha creado* (present perfect).

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Who looks after Spanish?">

The **Real Academia Española** (RAE) was founded in Madrid in 1713. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, academies were created in the other Spanish-speaking countries, and in 1951 they joined in the **Asociación de Academias de la Lengua Española** (ASALE), founded in Mexico. Today ASALE brings together more than twenty academies, from Spain and the Americas to the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea and the United States.

Since the end of the twentieth century the academies have worked together under what they call a **panhispanic** language policy. The major reference works are now joint publications of all of them: the *Diccionario de la lengua española*, the *Nueva gramática de la lengua española*, the *Ortografía* and the *Diccionario panhispánico de dudas*, together with a *Diccionario de americanismos*. Their principle is that the standard has many centres: the educated usage of Buenos Aires, Bogotá, Mexico City or Madrid is equally valid. That's why the dictionary marks words as *Esp.*, *Méx.* or *Arg.*, rather than labelling the Spanish of Spain as the default.

The academies describe and recommend; they don't police everyday speech. Their dictionaries and grammar resources are free online at [rae.es](https://www.rae.es/) and [asale.org](https://www.asale.org/), and they are the best place to settle a doubt about any variety.

Spanish has other homes too: Judeo-Spanish, also called Ladino, carried by the Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain in 1492, is still spoken by small communities, and Spanish left thousands of words in the languages of the Philippines.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mistakes">

<Misconception>

**"The Spanish of Spain is the original, purest form."** Every variety has changed since the fifteenth century, Spain's included. Linguists and the academies treat each country's educated usage as equally valid.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Vos is slang."** In Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay and most of Central America, *vos* is the normal informal *you*, used in literature, the press and government.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Castellano and español are different languages."** They are two names for the same language. *Castellano* is preferred in Spain's constitution and in several Latin American countries; *español* is the more usual name internationally.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I must pick one variety and never mix."** It helps to choose one model for your own speech, especially for *vosotros/ustedes* and pronunciation. But understanding several is a strength, and native speakers who live abroad mix words all the time.

</Misconception>

</Section>
