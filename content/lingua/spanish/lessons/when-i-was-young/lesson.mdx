<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="Another way of looking at the past">

The preterite, which you learned in the <LessonLink slug="what-happened">last</LessonLink> <LessonLink slug="trips-and-irregular-pasts">two lessons</LessonLink>, tells what happened: *I went, she said, we arrived*. But when people talk about their childhood, they mostly describe how things *used to be*: where they lived, what their grandmother was like, what they did every summer. For this Spanish has a second past tense, the **imperfect** (*el pretérito imperfecto*). The good news: it is the most regular tense in the language, with only three irregular verbs.

</Section>

<Section id="imperfect" kind="grammar" title="Forming the imperfect">

Take the stem and add:

| | jugar | tener | vivir |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | jug**aba** | ten**ía** | viv**ía** |
| tú | jug**abas** | ten**ías** | viv**ías** |
| él, ella, usted | jug**aba** | ten**ía** | viv**ía** |
| nosotros, nosotras | jug**ábamos** | ten**íamos** | viv**íamos** |
| vosotros, vosotras | jug**abais** | ten**íais** | viv**íais** |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | jug**aban** | ten**ían** | viv**ían** |

- **-ar verbs** take *-aba-*; **-er and -ir verbs** share *-ía-*.
- **The yo and él forms are identical** (*jugaba, tenía*). Add the subject when it isn't clear: *yo tenía, ella tenía*.
- **No stem changes, no strong stems.** Verbs that are irregular elsewhere are regular here: *quería, podía, dormía, tenía, hacía, decía, venía, ponía, estaba*.
- **Hay** becomes **había** (there was, there were): <Say lang="es">Había un río cerca de mi casa.</Say>

### Only three irregular verbs

| | ser | ir | ver |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | <Say lang="es">era</Say> | <Say lang="es">iba</Say> | <Say lang="es">veía</Say> |
| tú | <Say lang="es">eras</Say> | <Say lang="es">ibas</Say> | <Say lang="es">veías</Say> |
| él, ella, usted | <Say lang="es">era</Say> | <Say lang="es">iba</Say> | <Say lang="es">veía</Say> |
| nosotros, nosotras | <Say lang="es">éramos</Say> | <Say lang="es">íbamos</Say> | <Say lang="es">veíamos</Say> |
| vosotros, vosotras | <Say lang="es">erais</Say> | <Say lang="es">ibais</Say> | <Say lang="es">veíais</Say> |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | <Say lang="es">eran</Say> | <Say lang="es">iban</Say> | <Say lang="es">veían</Say> |

*Ver* is irregular only because it keeps its *e*: *veía*, not *vía*.

</Section>

<Section id="uses" kind="grammar" title="What the imperfect is for">

The imperfect looks at the past from the inside, as a situation that was going on, without focusing on its beginning or end. At this level, use it for four things:

**1. Habits and repeated actions**: what you used to do, or would do. English says "used to", "would", or just the simple past; Spanish uses the imperfect for all three.

- <Say lang="es">De niña, jugaba en la calle con mis vecinos.</Say> As a child, I used to play in the street with my neighbours.
- <Say lang="es">Los domingos comíamos en casa de mis abuelos.</Say> On Sundays we would have lunch at my grandparents'.

**2. Descriptions** of people, places, things and feelings in the past:

- <Say lang="es">Mi abuelo era alto y tenía el pelo blanco.</Say> My grandfather was tall and had white hair.
- <Say lang="es">La casa era pequeña, pero tenía un jardín.</Say>
- <Say lang="es">Estaba muy contenta en aquella escuela.</Say> I was very happy at that school.

**3. Age** in the past: <Say lang="es">Cuando tenía siete años, aprendí a nadar.</Say> (When I was seven, I learned to swim.)

**4. Time of day** in the past: <Say lang="es">Eran las once de la noche.</Say> <Say lang="es">Era la una.</Say>

Typical companions of the imperfect are *antes, de niño, cuando era pequeño, todos los días, siempre, a menudo, los sábados, en aquella época*.

### Soler: "used to"

**Soler** + infinitive means "to usually do". In the present, it's a stem-changing verb (*suelo, sueles, suele, solemos, soléis, suelen*): <Say lang="es">Suelo cenar a las nueve.</Say> (I usually have dinner at nine.) In the imperfect, it's the neatest way to say "used to": <Say lang="es">Solíamos ir al pueblo en verano.</Say> (We used to go to the village in summer.) It's never used alone: you always say what you used to do.

</Section>

<Section id="contrast" kind="grammar" title="Imperfect or preterite? A first look">

Both tenses describe the past, but they answer different questions:

| Imperfect: *what was it like? what used to happen?* | Preterite: *what happened?* |
| --- | --- |
| habits: *Todos los veranos íbamos a la playa.* (Every summer we went to the beach.) | one event: *Un verano fuimos a la montaña.* (One summer we went to the mountains.) |
| background: *Era de noche y no había nadie en la calle.* (It was night and there was nobody in the street.) | an event in that setting: *Llegó un taxi.* (A taxi arrived.) |
| an action in progress: *Leía en mi cuarto…* (I was reading in my room…) | an action that interrupts it: *…cuando sonó el teléfono.* (…when the phone rang.) |
| a description: *Mi primera maestra era muy simpática.* | a finished period seen as a whole: *Fue mi maestra dos años.* (She was my teacher for two years.) |

A useful image: the imperfect paints the **scene**, the preterite moves the **story** forward. Read this short memory and notice how the tenses switch:

> <Say lang="es">Cuando tenía nueve años, vivíamos en un barrio tranquilo. Mi hermano y yo íbamos al colegio a pie. Un día, encontramos un perro pequeño en la calle. Lo llevamos a casa y mi madre dijo que sí.</Say>

The first two sentences set the scene and describe habits (*tenía, vivíamos, íbamos*); then *un día* starts the events (*encontramos, llevamos, dijo*). You'll study this contrast in depth in level B1, in <LessonLink slug="telling-a-story">Telling a story</LessonLink>. For now, if you can ask "was this a habit or a description, or was it a single event?", you'll be right most of the time.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="Grandmother's village">

Sofía grew up in Los Angeles and speaks Spanish at home. She is visiting her grandmother Marta in Guatemala City and asks her about her childhood.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Abuela, ¿cómo era tu vida cuando eras niña?</Say> | Grandma, what was your life like when you were a girl? |
| Abuela | <Say lang="es">Uy, muy diferente. Vivíamos en un pueblo pequeño, cerca de Antigua. No teníamos tele ni teléfono.</Say> | Oh, very different. We lived in a small village near Antigua. We didn't have a TV or a phone. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">¿Y qué hacías todos los días?</Say> | And what did you do every day? |
| Abuela | <Say lang="es">Por la mañana iba a la escuela con mis hermanos. Caminábamos una hora. Por la tarde ayudaba a mi mamá en la cocina.</Say> | In the morning I went to school with my brothers and sisters. We walked for an hour. In the afternoon I helped my mum in the kitchen. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">¿Y no jugabas?</Say> | And didn't you play? |
| Abuela | <Say lang="es">¡Claro que sí! Solíamos jugar en la calle con los vecinos hasta la noche. Tu tío abuelo Tomás era muy travieso.</Say> | Of course! We used to play in the street with the neighbours until dark. Your great-uncle Tomás was very naughty. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">¿Cuántos años tenías cuando fuiste a la capital por primera vez?</Say> | How old were you when you went to the capital for the first time? |
| Abuela | <Say lang="es">Tenía doce años. Fuimos a una boda. Me acuerdo de que había muchísima gente y muchos carros.</Say> | I was twelve. We went to a wedding. I remember there were loads of people and lots of cars. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">¿Te gustó?</Say> | Did you like it? |
| Abuela | <Say lang="es">No mucho. Yo prefería el pueblo. ¿Y tú, mija? ¿Qué hacías cuando eras pequeña?</Say> | Not much. I preferred the village. And you, sweetheart? What did you do when you were little? |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Veía caricaturas todos los sábados y comía cereal en el sofá.</Say> | I watched cartoons every Saturday and ate cereal on the sofa. |
| Abuela | <Say lang="es">¡Ay, los niños de hoy!</Say> | Oh, children today! |

**Notes.** *Ni* means "nor": *No teníamos tele ni teléfono*, "we had neither a TV nor a phone". *Tío abuelo* is a great-uncle. *Por primera vez* = for the first time. *Me acuerdo de que…* = I remember that… *Carro* is "car" in Central America, Mexico, Colombia and Venezuela (*coche* in Spain, *auto* in the Southern Cone). Look at the tenses in the grandmother's answer about the capital: *fuimos* (one event), but *tenía* (age) and *había* (description).

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="The accent that splits ía">

In *-ía* endings, the written accent on the *í* splits the two vowels into separate syllables, as you saw in *día* in lesson 1. So <Say lang="es">tenía</Say> has three syllables, *te-NÍ-a*, and <Say lang="es">vivíamos</Say> has four, *vi-VÍ-a-mos*. Don't run the *i* and *a* together into one syllable.

In -ar verbs, the *nosotros* form has its accent on the first *a* of the ending: <Say lang="es">jugábamos</Say> (ju-GÁ-ba-mos), <Say lang="es">éramos</Say> (É-ra-mos), <Say lang="es">íbamos</Say> (Í-ba-mos). All three are stressed three syllables from the end, so all three need a written accent.

The *b* in *-aba* comes between vowels, so it's the soft *b* from lesson 1, with the lips barely touching: <Say lang="es">jugaba</Say>, <Say lang="es">caminábamos</Say>.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Childhood memories across the Spanish-speaking world">

Ask Spanish speakers about their childhood and certain memories come up again and again. In Spain and in many Latin American countries, the night of 5 January is magical for children: the Three Kings (*los Reyes Magos*) bring presents, in memory of the wise men who visited the infant Jesus. Children leave their shoes out, sometimes with water or grass for the camels, and on the 6th there is a ring-shaped cake, the *roscón* in Spain or *rosca de Reyes* in Mexico, with a small figure hidden inside. At birthday parties in Mexico and Central America, children take turns hitting a *piñata* full of sweets while everyone sings.

Other memories are everyday: the afternoon *merienda*, games in the street or the square until it got dark, summers at a grandparent's village. Many adults also remember the shift from rural to city life in their own families, like Sofía's grandmother.

<Reflection title="When I was a child">

A famous verse about growing up is a perfect lesson in the two past tenses. Saint Paul writes, in an old public-domain Spanish translation: "*Cuando yo era niño, hablaba como niño, pensaba como niño, juzgaba como niño, mas cuando ya fuí hombre hecho, dejé lo que era de niño*" (1 Corintios 13:11, Reina-Valera 1909). Childhood is described in the imperfect, as a state that lasted (*era, hablaba, pensaba, juzgaba*); becoming an adult and leaving childish ways behind is told in the preterite, as a change that happened (*fuí, dejé*; today *fui* is written without an accent). The verse belongs to a chapter about love, where growing up means, above all, learning to love more fully.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"'Used to' is the only English clue for the imperfect." English often uses the simple past where Spanish needs the imperfect: "My grandmother *was* tall" → *era alta*; "Every summer we *went* to the beach" → *íbamos*. Think about the meaning (habit, description), not the English form.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Stem-changing verbs change in the imperfect too: *quiería, puedía*." The imperfect has no stem changes at all: **quería, podía, dormía, prefería**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Iba* is a form of *ver*." It's from **ir**: *iba* = I used to go, I was going. *Ver* gives *veía*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Habían muchos coches.*" You will hear this in many places, but in standard Spanish *había* doesn't change for plural: **había muchos coches**, just as in the present *hay muchos coches*.

</Misconception>
