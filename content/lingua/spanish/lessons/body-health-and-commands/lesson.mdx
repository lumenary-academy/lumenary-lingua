<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="When something hurts">

Few situations make you want the right words as badly as feeling ill in another country. This lesson gives you the body, the symptoms, and the key verb **doler** (to hurt), which you'll find works exactly like *gustar*. Then comes a new kind of verb form: the **imperative**, for telling people what to do. Doctors use it, recipes use it, friends use it, and signs on every street use it. You'll learn the informal *tú* commands, then the polite *usted* ones.

</Section>

<Section id="doler" kind="grammar" title="Doler: what hurts">

*Doler* works like *gustar* (<LessonLink slug="food-and-places">lesson 6</LessonLink>): the part that hurts is the subject, and the person is an indirect object pronoun.

- <Say lang="es">Me duele la cabeza.</Say> My head hurts. / I've got a headache.
- <Say lang="es">Me duelen los pies.</Say> My feet hurt. (plural subject: *duelen*)
- <Say lang="es">¿Te duele algo?</Say> Does anything hurt?
- <Say lang="es">A mi hijo le duele el estómago.</Say> My son has stomach ache.
- <Say lang="es">¿Le duele aquí?</Say> Does it hurt here? (a doctor to a patient, *usted*)

It's a stem-changing verb (*o → ue*), but you'll almost only use *duele* and *duelen*. Notice that Spanish uses **the** with parts of the body, not *my*: the pronoun already says whose head it is. *Me duele **la** cabeza*, never *me duele mi cabeza*.

Other ways to describe symptoms:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">Tengo dolor de garganta.</Say> | I have a sore throat. |
| <Say lang="es">Tengo fiebre y tos.</Say> | I have a fever and a cough. |
| <Say lang="es">Estoy resfriado.</Say> | I have a cold. |
| <Say lang="es">Me siento mal.</Say> / <Say lang="es">Me encuentro mal.</Say> | I feel ill. (*encontrarse* is common in Spain) |
| <Say lang="es">Me corté el dedo.</Say> | I cut my finger. |
| <Say lang="es">Me rompí la pierna.</Say> | I broke my leg. |

The last two use a reflexive verb and *the*, just like *me lavo los dientes*.

</Section>

<Section id="tu-commands" kind="grammar" title="Telling a friend what to do: tú commands">

### Affirmative

For most verbs, the affirmative *tú* command is the same as the **él/ella form of the present**:

| Infinitive | Present (él) | Command |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tomar | toma | <Say lang="es">¡Toma!</Say> Take (it)! |
| beber | bebe | <Say lang="es">Bebe mucha agua.</Say> Drink lots of water. |
| abrir | abre | <Say lang="es">Abre la ventana.</Say> Open the window. |
| dormir | duerme | <Say lang="es">Duerme un poco.</Say> Get some sleep. |
| pedir | pide | <Say lang="es">Pide un taxi.</Say> Order a taxi. |

Stem changes stay (*duerme, pide*). Eight very common verbs have short irregular forms. Learn them as a group:

| decir | hacer | ir | poner | salir | ser | tener | venir |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">di</Say> | <Say lang="es">haz</Say> | <Say lang="es">ve</Say> | <Say lang="es">pon</Say> | <Say lang="es">sal</Say> | <Say lang="es">sé</Say> | <Say lang="es">ten</Say> | <Say lang="es">ven</Say> |

<Say lang="es">Ven aquí.</Say> (Come here.) <Say lang="es">Haz los deberes.</Say> (Do your homework.) <Say lang="es">Ten cuidado.</Say> (Be careful.) <Say lang="es">Sé bueno.</Say> (Be good.) <Say lang="es">Dime la verdad.</Say> (Tell me the truth.)

### Negative

Negative commands use a different form. Take the **yo form of the present**, drop the **-o**, and add the "opposite" ending: **-es** for -ar verbs, **-as** for -er and -ir verbs:

| Infinitive | yo | Negative command |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hablar | habl-o | <Say lang="es">No hables.</Say> |
| comer | com-o | <Say lang="es">No comas.</Say> |
| escribir | escrib-o | <Say lang="es">No escribas.</Say> |
| tener | teng-o | <Say lang="es">No tengas miedo.</Say> Don't be afraid. |
| hacer | hag-o | <Say lang="es">No hagas eso.</Say> Don't do that. |
| decir | dig-o | <Say lang="es">No digas nada.</Say> Don't say anything. |
| salir | salg-o | <Say lang="es">No salgas hoy.</Say> Don't go out today. |
| dormir | duerm-o | <Say lang="es">No duermas en el sofá.</Say> |

Starting from the *yo* form carries over the irregular stems (*tengo → tengas, hago → hagas*) automatically. Three need learning separately: <Say lang="es">no vayas</Say> (*ir*), <Say lang="es">no seas</Say> (*ser*), <Say lang="es">no estés</Say> (*estar*). And the same spelling changes as in the preterite keep the sound: *buscar → no busques*, *llegar → no llegues*, *empezar → no empieces*.

These negative forms are in fact the present **subjunctive**, a mood you'll study properly at level B1. For now, treat them as the negative command.

### Where the pronouns go

- **Affirmative:** attached to the end, written as one word: <Say lang="es">Tómalo.</Say> (Take it.) <Say lang="es">Acuéstate.</Say> (Lie down.) <Say lang="es">Dímelo.</Say> (Tell me it.)
- **Negative:** before the verb, as usual: <Say lang="es">No lo tomes.</Say> <Say lang="es">No te acuestes.</Say> <Say lang="es">No me lo digas.</Say>

Attaching a pronoun adds a syllable, so the stress may need a written accent to stay where it was: *toma → tómalo, acuesta → acuéstate*. One-syllable commands with one pronoun don't need one: *dime, ponlo, hazlo*.

<Note>

**Vos and vosotros.** With *vos*, affirmative commands are stressed on the end and drop the *r* of the infinitive: *tomá, comé, vení, decí*. The negatives are often the same as for *tú*. With *vosotros* (Spain), replace the *-r* of the infinitive with *-d*: *tomad, comed, venid*; reflexive: *sentaos*.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="usted-commands" kind="grammar" title="Polite instructions: usted commands">

For *usted*, there's a single form for affirmative and negative: the negative *tú* form **without the final -s**. For *ustedes*, add **-n**.

| Infinitive | usted | ustedes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tomar | <Say lang="es">tome</Say> | <Say lang="es">tomen</Say> |
| beber | <Say lang="es">beba</Say> | <Say lang="es">beban</Say> |
| abrir | <Say lang="es">abra</Say> | <Say lang="es">abran</Say> |
| decir | <Say lang="es">diga</Say> | <Say lang="es">digan</Say> |
| hacer | <Say lang="es">haga</Say> | <Say lang="es">hagan</Say> |
| ir | <Say lang="es">vaya</Say> | <Say lang="es">vayan</Say> |
| ser | <Say lang="es">sea</Say> | <Say lang="es">sean</Say> |
| estar | <Say lang="es">esté</Say> | <Say lang="es">estén</Say> |
| dar | <Say lang="es">dé</Say> | <Say lang="es">den</Say> |

The pronoun rules are the same: attached in the affirmative (<Say lang="es">Siéntese.</Say> Sit down. <Say lang="es">Tómelo con agua.</Say> Take it with water.), before the verb in the negative (<Say lang="es">No se preocupe.</Say> Don't worry.).

You'll meet *usted* and *ustedes* commands everywhere: from doctors (<Say lang="es">Respire hondo.</Say> Breathe deeply.), in shops (<Say lang="es">Pase, pase.</Say> Come in.), on signs (*No fumen*, No smoking), and in directions, which you first met in the present tense in lesson 6: <Say lang="es">Siga todo recto y gire a la derecha.</Say> (Go straight on and turn right.) In Latin America, where *ustedes* is the only plural "you", the *ustedes* commands are used with friends too.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="A sore throat in Quito">

Thabo, from South Africa, is studying in Quito, Ecuador. He's had a sore throat for days and goes to see a doctor, who uses *usted* with him.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doctora | <Say lang="es">Buenos días. Siéntese, por favor. ¿Qué le pasa?</Say> | Good morning. Sit down, please. What's the matter? |
| Thabo | <Say lang="es">Me duele mucho la garganta y tengo tos.</Say> | My throat hurts a lot and I have a cough. |
| Doctora | <Say lang="es">¿Desde cuándo?</Say> | Since when? |
| Thabo | <Say lang="es">Desde el lunes. Anoche tuve fiebre y casi no dormí.</Say> | Since Monday. Last night I had a temperature and hardly slept. |
| Doctora | <Say lang="es">¿Le duele la cabeza?</Say> | Do you have a headache? |
| Thabo | <Say lang="es">Un poco. Y me duelen los oídos.</Say> | A bit. And my ears hurt. |
| Doctora | <Say lang="es">A ver… Abra la boca y diga «aaa».</Say> | Let's see… Open your mouth and say "aaa". |
| Doctora | <Say lang="es">Tiene la garganta muy roja. Es una infección.</Say> | Your throat is very red. It's an infection. |
| Thabo | <Say lang="es">¿Es grave?</Say> | Is it serious? |
| Doctora | <Say lang="es">No, no se preocupe. Tome estas pastillas tres veces al día, después de comer. Beba mucha agua y descanse.</Say> | No, don't worry. Take these tablets three times a day, after meals. Drink plenty of water and rest. |
| Thabo | <Say lang="es">¿Puedo ir a clase?</Say> | Can I go to class? |
| Doctora | <Say lang="es">Mejor no. Quédese en casa dos o tres días.</Say> | Better not. Stay at home for two or three days. |

Back at the flat, his flatmate Gabriela uses *tú*:

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gabriela | <Say lang="es">¿Qué te dijo la doctora?</Say> | What did the doctor tell you? |
| Thabo | <Say lang="es">Que tengo una infección de garganta.</Say> | That I've got a throat infection. |
| Gabriela | <Say lang="es">¡Pobrecito! Ven, siéntate en el sofá, ponte cómodo y no hables mucho.</Say> | You poor thing! Come on, sit on the sofa, make yourself comfortable and don't talk too much. |
| Thabo | <Say lang="es">Gracias. Eres un ángel.</Say> | Thanks. You're an angel. |
| Gabriela | <Say lang="es">Y apaga el celular. ¡Descansa! ¡Que te mejores!</Say> | And switch off your phone. Rest! Get well soon! |

**Notes.** Compare the two registers: *siéntese* (usted) and *siéntate* (tú) are the same verb, *sentarse*. *A ver* = let's see. *Mejor no* = better not. *Pobrecito* is *pobre* (poor) with the affectionate ending *-ito*. *Celular* is a mobile phone in Latin America (*móvil* in Spain). *Ponte cómodo* (make yourself comfortable) is *pon* + *te*, literally "put yourself comfortable"; to a woman, *ponte cómoda*.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Accents that appear and disappear">

When a pronoun is attached to a command, the stressed syllable doesn't move, but the word gets longer, so the stress rules from lesson 1 may now require a written accent:

| Without pronoun | With pronoun(s) |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">toma</Say> (TO-ma) | <Say lang="es">tómalo</Say> (TÓ-ma-lo) |
| <Say lang="es">siente</Say> | <Say lang="es">siéntese</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">acuesta</Say> | <Say lang="es">acuéstate</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">di</Say> | <Say lang="es">dime</Say>, <Say lang="es">dímelo</Say> |

Listen also for short words that differ only in their accent or context: <Say lang="es">sé</Say> ("be!", or "I know") and *se* (pronoun); <Say lang="es">ve</Say> ("go!", from *ir*, or "he sees", from *ver*). Context always decides: *Ve a la farmacia* can only mean "Go to the pharmacy".

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Pharmacies, remedies and bless you">

In Spain and Latin America, the local *farmacia* (marked in Spain by a green cross) is often the first stop for minor health problems. Pharmacists are trained professionals who will listen to your symptoms and recommend something, or tell you to see a doctor. Which medicines need a prescription varies from country to country, so don't assume the rules are the same as at home. In Spanish cities, one pharmacy in each area stays open at night on a rota (*farmacia de guardia*).

Many families also have their home remedies: chamomile tea (*té de manzanilla*) for an upset stomach, honey and lemon for a sore throat, or chicken soup (*caldo de pollo*) for a cold.

When someone sneezes, people say <Say lang="es">¡Salud!</Say> ("health!") or, especially in Spain and among older speakers, <Say lang="es">¡Jesús!</Say> Some families continue playfully after more sneezes: *¡Jesús, María y José!*

<Reflection title="Danos hoy">

In the Lord's Prayer, as an old public-domain translation puts it, one line reads: "*Danos hoy nuestro pan cotidiano*" (Mateo 6:11, Reina-Valera 1909), "Give us this day our daily bread". *Danos* is the affirmative *tú* command of *dar* with *nos* attached, exactly the pattern of this lesson. It's a reminder that the imperative isn't only for orders. In Spanish it's also the natural form for requests between people who trust each other, and in prayer it becomes the humblest way to ask.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"*Me duele mi cabeza.*" With body parts, Spanish uses the article: **Me duele la cabeza**. The *me* already tells you whose head.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"The negative of *habla* is *no habla*." *No habla* means "he doesn't speak". The negative command has its own form: **No hables**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Commands are rude, so I should avoid them." In Spanish, commands with a friendly tone and *por favor* are normal and polite: *Pásame la sal, por favor*. Doctors, shop assistants and friends use them all the time.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Te sientas* tells someone to sit down." *Te sientas* is a statement: "you sit down". The command attaches the pronoun: **Siéntate** (to a friend), **Siéntese** (to *usted*).

</Misconception>
