<Section id="vocabulary" kind="vocabulary" title="Words for telling stories">

At A2 you learned two past tenses one at a time: the preterite in <LessonLink slug="what-happened">What happened</LessonLink> and <LessonLink slug="trips-and-irregular-pasts">Trips and irregular pasts</LessonLink>, and the imperfect in <LessonLink slug="when-i-was-young">When I was young</LessonLink>. Real stories use both at once, plus a third tense for what had *already* happened, plus a handful of small words that tell the listener where we are in the plot. That is what this lesson is about.

<Section id="background-and-events" kind="grammar" title="Background and events">

When you tell a story, every past verb answers one of two questions:

- **What was the scene like? What was going on?** → **imperfect**. It paints the background.
- **What happened next?** → **preterite**. It moves the plot forward.

| Imperfect: the background | Preterite: the events |
| --- | --- |
| Time: *Eran las once.* | Actions in sequence: *Abrí la puerta, entré y encendí la luz.* |
| Weather: *Llovía. Hacía frío.* | Sudden events: *De repente, sonó el teléfono.* |
| Age: *Tenía doce años.* | A change of state: *Me asusté. Empezó a llover.* |
| People and places: *La casa era vieja.* | An action seen as a closed block: *Viví en Quito tres años.* |
| Feelings and states: *Estaba nerviosa.* | Reactions: *Grité y salí corriendo.* |
| Actions in progress: *Los niños jugaban.* | |
| Habits: *Cada verano íbamos al pueblo.* | |

<Example title="A story, tense by tense">

<Say lang="es">Eran las once de la noche y llovía. Marta volvía a casa a pie; estaba cansada y solo pensaba en dormir. La calle estaba vacía. De repente, un perro enorme salió de un portal. Marta se asustó, dio un paso atrás y se le cayó el paraguas. Entonces el perro se acercó, la miró… y se sentó a sus pies. Llevaba un collar con un nombre: Bombón.</Say>

*It was eleven at night and it was raining. Marta was walking home; she was tired and could only think about sleep. The street was empty. Suddenly an enormous dog came out of a doorway. Marta got scared, stepped back and dropped her umbrella. Then the dog came up to her, looked at her… and sat down at her feet. It was wearing a collar with a name: Bombón.*

The imperfects (*eran, llovía, volvía, estaba, pensaba, estaba, llevaba*) build the scene. The preterites (*salió, se asustó, dio, se cayó, se acercó, miró, se sentó*) are the plot: read only them and you can still follow what happened. Read only the imperfects and you have a stage with nobody acting on it.

</Example>

<Analogy>

Think of a film. The imperfect is the camera moving slowly over the scene: the rain, the empty street, a tired woman walking home. The preterite is the cut to action, the moment something happens and the story moves on.

</Analogy>

### Interrupted actions

The most common pattern in any story: something **was going on** (imperfect) when something else **happened** (preterite).

- <Say lang="es">Leía en el sofá cuando sonó el teléfono.</Say> — I was reading on the sofa when the phone rang.
- <Say lang="es">Estaba duchándome cuando llamaron a la puerta.</Say> — I was in the shower when someone knocked. *Estaba* + gerund stresses "right in the middle of it".
- <Say lang="es">Mientras cocinábamos, se fue la luz.</Say> — While we were cooking, the power went out.

Two things going on at the same time are both background, so both take the imperfect: <Say lang="es">Mientras yo fregaba los platos, mi hermano los secaba.</Say>

Compare these two, which differ by one verb:

- *Cuando salí del metro, **llovía**.* — It was already raining when I came out.
- *Cuando salí del metro, **empezó** a llover.* — It started raining just then.

### Verbs that change meaning

With a few verbs the choice of tense changes the English translation, because the preterite picks out the moment something began or was achieved.

| Verb | Imperfect | Preterite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| conocer | *Conocía a Ana.* I knew Ana. | *Conocí a Ana.* I met Ana (for the first time). |
| saber | *Sabía la verdad.* I knew the truth. | *Supe la verdad.* I found out the truth. |
| querer | *Quería llamarte.* I wanted to call you. | *Quise llamarte.* I tried to call you. *No quise* = I refused. |
| poder | *Podía nadar.* I could swim (in general). | *Pude abrirla.* I managed to open it. *No pude* = I couldn't (and failed). |
| tener miedo | *Tenía miedo.* I was afraid (a state). | *Tuve miedo.* I got scared (at that moment). |

</Section>

<Section id="pluperfect" kind="grammar" title="The pluperfect: what had already happened">

Sometimes a story needs to step **further back** into the past: before the events you are telling, something else had already happened. For that Spanish uses the **pluperfect** (*pretérito pluscuamperfecto*): the imperfect of *haber* plus a past participle.

| | haber (imperfect) | + participle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yo | había | llegado |
| tú | habías | comido |
| él, ella, usted | había | salido |
| nosotros, nosotras | habíamos | visto |
| vosotros, vosotras | habíais | hecho |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | habían | dicho |

Regular participles end in **-ado** (*-ar* verbs) and **-ido** (*-er* and *-ir* verbs): *hablado, comido, vivido*. A few common verbs have irregular ones you need straight away: *hacer → hecho, decir → dicho, ver → visto, poner → puesto, escribir → escrito, volver → vuelto, abrir → abierto, romper → roto, morir → muerto*. You will meet the full list, and the present perfect that uses them, in <LessonLink slug="news-and-experiences">News and experiences</LessonLink>.

Three rules keep it tidy:

1. The participle never changes: *las chicas habían **salido*** (not *salidas*).
2. Object and reflexive pronouns go before *había*: <Say lang="es">Ya me lo había dicho.</Say> — She had already told me.
3. Nothing goes between *había* and the participle: *ya había comido*, never *había ya comido*.

- <Say lang="es">Cuando llegué a la estación, el tren ya había salido.</Say> — When I got to the station, the train had already left. (I missed it.)
- *Cuando llegué a la estación, el tren salió.* — When I got there, the train left. (It left as I arrived.)
- <Say lang="es">Nunca había visto la nieve hasta que fui a Bariloche.</Say> — I had never seen snow until I went to Bariloche.
- <Say lang="es">Me di cuenta de que había olvidado la cartera.</Say> — I realised I had forgotten my wallet.

English often gets away with a simple past here (*I realised I forgot my wallet*). Spanish is stricter: if one past event comes before another and the order matters, use the pluperfect.

### Holding the story together

Connectors are the signposts of a story. A typical shape:

| Stage | Connectors | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | *un día, hace unos años, resulta que* | <Say lang="es">Hace unos años, viajé sola a Cartagena.</Say> |
| Background | *al principio* | <Say lang="es">Al principio todo iba bien.</Say> |
| Turning point | *de repente, de pronto, en ese momento* | <Say lang="es">De pronto, alguien gritó mi nombre.</Say> |
| Development | *entonces, luego, después, al cabo de un rato, mientras* | <Say lang="es">Entonces me di la vuelta.</Say> |
| Ending | *al final, por fin, finalmente* | <Say lang="es">Al final, era mi prima Lucía.</Say> |

Three endings that are easy to mix up: **al final** is how things turned out (*Al final no fuimos*); **por fin** expresses relief after a wait (*¡Por fin llegó el autobús!*); **finalmente** is the last step of a sequence, and a little more formal.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="The night without keys">

**In a café in Valencia.** Lucía, who is from Valencia, meets her friend Andrés, from Valparaíso in Chile, who has been working in Spain for a year. He has a story to tell.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">¿A que no sabes lo que me pasó el sábado?</Say> | I bet you can't guess what happened to me on Saturday. |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">Cuéntame.</Say> | Tell me. |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">Pues resulta que salí a cenar con unos amigos. Era tarde, hacía frío y yo estaba cansadísimo, así que volví a casa en taxi.</Say> | So, I went out for dinner with some friends. It was late, it was cold and I was exhausted, so I went home by taxi. |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">Hasta ahí, todo normal.</Say> | Nothing strange so far. |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">Sí, pero cuando llegué a la puerta, me di cuenta de que no tenía las llaves. Las había dejado en la oficina el viernes.</Say> | Right, but when I got to the door I realised I didn't have my keys. I'd left them at the office on Friday. |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">¡No! ¿Y tu compañero de piso?</Say> | No! What about your flatmate? |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">Estaba de viaje. Había ido a ver a sus padres. Al principio no sabía qué hacer. Llamé a un cerrajero, pero no contestó nadie.</Say> | He was away. He'd gone to see his parents. At first I didn't know what to do. I called a locksmith, but nobody answered. |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">¿Y entonces?</Say> | And then? |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">Entonces me senté en la escalera. Mientras buscaba otro número en el celular, se abrió la puerta de enfrente. Era don Hernán, un señor de ochenta años que vive solo con su gata.</Say> | Then I sat down on the stairs. While I was looking for another number on my phone, the door opposite opened. It was don Hernán, an eighty-year-old man who lives alone with his cat. |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">¿Y te ayudó?</Say> | And did he help you? |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">Mejor todavía. Mi compañero le había dejado una copia de las llaves hacía meses, por si acaso. ¡Y yo no lo sabía!</Say> | Even better. My flatmate had left him a spare set of keys months before, just in case. And I had no idea! |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">¡Qué suerte! ¿Y al final pudiste entrar?</Say> | How lucky! So in the end you managed to get in? |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">Sí, pero antes me invitó a un té y me contó cómo era el barrio cuando él era joven. Estuvimos hablando casi dos horas.</Say> | Yes, but first he invited me in for tea and told me what the neighbourhood was like when he was young. We talked for almost two hours. |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">O sea, que perdiste las llaves y ganaste un amigo.</Say> | So you lost your keys and gained a friend. |
| Andrés | <Say lang="es">Exactamente.</Say> | Exactly. |

Notice how the three tenses share the work. The imperfect sets each scene (*era tarde, hacía frío, estaba de viaje, buscaba*); the preterite carries the plot (*salí, volví, llegué, llamé, se abrió*); the pluperfect explains what lay behind it (*las había dejado, había ido, le había dejado una copia*). Notice also *pudiste*: the preterite of *poder* means he actually managed it.

<Note>

Two words show where each speaker is from. Lucía says *compañero de piso*, the usual term in Spain; in Chile you would more often hear *compañero de departamento*. Andrés says *celular*, the word for a mobile phone across Latin America, where Lucía would say *móvil*. Each understands the other perfectly.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="The accents that change the story">

In storytelling, one written accent can change who did something and when. Spanish marks the stressed syllable whenever it breaks the default rules, and several pairs differ only in stress:

| Present (yo) | Preterite (él, ella, usted) |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">hablo</Say> — I speak | <Say lang="es">habló</Say> — she spoke |
| <Say lang="es">llego</Say> — I arrive | <Say lang="es">llegó</Say> — he arrived |
| <Say lang="es">termino</Say> — I finish | <Say lang="es">terminó</Say> — it finished |
| <Say lang="es">paso</Say> — I pass | <Say lang="es">pasó</Say> — it happened |

Say the first word of each pair with the stress on the first syllable (*HA-blo*) and the second with a clear, strong final syllable (*ha-BLÓ*). If you swap them, you are telling a different story.

The imperfect of *-er* and *-ir* verbs, and the pluperfect auxiliary, carry an accent on the **í**: <Say lang="es">había</Say>, <Say lang="es">tenía</Say>, <Say lang="es">vivía</Say>. The accent splits the *i* from the following *a* into separate syllables: *ha-BÍ-a*, three syllables, not "ha-bya". In the *nosotros* form the stress stays where it was: <Say lang="es">habíamos</Say> (*ha-BÍ-a-mos*).

The *-ar* imperfect is stressed on the syllable just before *-ba*: *ha-BLA-ba, ha-BLA-bas*. In the *nosotros* form that syllable ends up third from last, so it needs a written accent: <Say lang="es">hablábamos</Say>.

Finally, stories have a melody. Spanish speakers tend to pause slightly after a connector that opens a sentence (*De repente, …*; *Al final, …*) and let the voice rise on the background before it falls on the event. Read Marta's story above aloud and exaggerate it a little.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Once upon a time">

Spanish fairy tales open in the imperfect, because the opening is pure background: **Érase una vez…** (very common in Spain) or **Había una vez…** (the usual opening across Latin America, and common in Spain too). Many children's stories end with a rhyme, *Y colorín colorado, este cuento se ha acabado*, or with *…y fueron felices y comieron perdices*, "and they were happy and ate partridges", the Spanish counterpart of "happily ever after".

The most famous opening line in Spanish literature does the same. Miguel de Cervantes begins *Don Quijote* (1605) with background, not action:

> *En un lugar de la Mancha, de cuyo nombre no quiero acordarme, no ha mucho tiempo que vivía un hidalgo…*
> — "In a village of La Mancha, whose name I do not care to recall, not long ago there lived a gentleman…"

Cervantes then spends his first paragraphs on the gentleman's age, meals, clothes and habits, all in the imperfect, before anything happens. The whole novel is free to read online; the Centro Virtual Cervantes, linked below, is a good place to start.

Stories are also part of daily life. In much of the Spanish-speaking world a meal is followed by the **sobremesa**, the time spent chatting at the table after eating, which can last longer than the meal itself. It is where family anecdotes are told and retold, and where you will hear *¿A que no sabes lo que me pasó?* more than anywhere else.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mistakes">

<Misconception>

**"Long actions take the imperfect, short ones the preterite."** Length doesn't decide. *Viví en Quito tres años* is preterite because the three years are seen as a closed block; *vivía en Quito cuando nació mi hija* is imperfect because it is the background to another event. Ask what the verb does in the story, not how long it lasted.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"English simple past = Spanish preterite."** English uses the simple past for both roles: *I knew her*, *I lived near the sea*, *it was cold*. Spanish separates them: *la conocía, vivía cerca del mar, hacía frío* are background; *la conocí* is the moment you met her.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I don't need the pluperfect; the preterite will do."** When one past event comes before another, Spanish marks it: *Cuando llegamos, la película ya había empezado.* With the preterite (*empezó*) it would mean the film began as you arrived.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Realizar means to realise."** It is a false friend: *realizar* means to carry out (*realizar un proyecto*). To realise is **darse cuenta de**: *Me di cuenta de que era tarde.*

</Misconception>

</Section>
