<Section id="near-synonyms" kind="vocabulary" title="Words with the same meaning and different lives">

At C2 you rarely lack a word; the problem is choosing among five. Spanish has few perfect synonyms: words that share a dictionary meaning differ in **register** (who says it, where), **connotation** (what it suggests), **collocation** (what it goes with) and **region**. <Section id="five-registers" kind="reading" title="One message, five registers">

Here is a single message: *Your neighbour's dog barks every night and keeps you awake; you want something done.* All five versions were written for this lesson.

### Coloquial

> Oye, ¿tu perro no se calla nunca o qué? Llevo tres noches sin pegar ojo, de verdad. A ver si haces algo, porfa, que me tiene frito.

### Neutro

> Perdona que te moleste. Quería comentarte que tu perro ladra mucho por la noche y me cuesta dormir. ¿Podrías hacer algo? Te lo agradecería mucho.

### Formal

> Estimado vecino:
>
> Me dirijo a usted para comunicarle que los ladridos de su perro durante la noche me impiden descansar desde hace varios días. Le agradecería que tomara las medidas que considere oportunas.
>
> Atentamente,

### Jurídico-administrativo

> D.ª Lucía Ramos Ibarra, con domicilio en la vivienda 3.º A del inmueble sito en la calle del Olivo, n.º 14,
>
> EXPONE: Que desde el día 2 del corriente se vienen produciendo, en horario nocturno, ladridos reiterados procedentes de la vivienda 2.º B, lo que vulnera lo dispuesto en las normas de convivencia de la comunidad.
>
> SOLICITA: Que se requiera al propietario de dicha vivienda la adopción de las medidas necesarias para el cese de las molestias, a los efectos oportunos.

### Literario

> Cada noche, cuando la calle por fin se rinde al silencio, el perro del vecino lo desgarra. No ladra: interroga. Lanza su pregunta a la oscuridad una y otra vez, y yo, desde mi cama, soy el único que no sabe responderle.

### What changes, and how

| Register | Vocabulary | Grammar and structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colloquial | *pegar ojo*, *porfa*, *me tiene frito* (I'm fed up) | *tú*, questions, *a ver si…*, *que* as loose "because", fillers (*oye*, *de verdad*) |
| Neutral | everyday words, nothing marked | *tú* or *usted*; softeners: *Perdona que…*, *quería*, *¿podrías…?* |
| Formal | *comunicarle*, *impedir*, *tomar las medidas oportunas* | *usted*, letter formulas, imperfect subjunctive of courtesy (*le agradecería que tomara*) |
| Legal-administrative | *sito* (located), *del corriente* (of this month), *vulnerar*, *lo dispuesto*, *dicha*, *el cese*, *a los efectos oportunos* | EXPONE / SOLICITA structure; third person about oneself; nominalisations (*la adopción*, *el cese*); *venir* + gerund; long single sentences |
| Literary | *rendirse al silencio*, *desgarrar*, *interrogar* | metaphor and personification; short sentence for effect (*No ladra: interroga.*); rhythm; first person as experience, not complaint |

The legal-administrative register deserves a note: it is the language of the Spanish *instancia* (a formal application to an authority), of contracts and of court documents. It survives on fixed formulas, and you should use them only when the genre requires it. It even preserves the old **future subjunctive** in laws and regulations — *el que **infringiere** esta norma será sancionado* — a form that has otherwise vanished from Spanish (see <LessonLink slug="golden-age-spanish">The language of the Golden Age</LessonLink>).

</Section>

### Connotation

Each set below shares a meaning; the words carry different judgements.

| Neutral | Positive | Negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *delgado* | *esbelto* (slender) | *escuálido* (scrawny); *flaco* is neutral-colloquial, and an affectionate nickname in the Southern Cone |
| *ahorrador* (thrifty) | *previsor* (far-sighted) | *tacaño*, *agarrado* (stingy), *avaro* (miserly) |
| *perseverante* | *tenaz* (tenacious) | *terco*, *testarudo*, *cabezota* (pig-headed) |
| *mayor* (polite for old) | *veterano* | *viejo* can be warm (*mi viejo* = dad, in the Southern Cone) or rude |

### Collocation

Native speakers recognise words that "go together" (*colocaciones*). Getting them right is one of the clearest marks of C2 Spanish.

| Collocation | Meaning | Not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *cometer un error, un delito* | to make a mistake, commit a crime | *hacer un crimen* |
| *contraer matrimonio, una deuda, una enfermedad* | to marry, incur a debt, catch a disease | |
| *desempeñar un cargo, una función* | to hold a post, perform a role | |
| *entablar una conversación, una relación* | to strike up | |
| *esgrimir un argumento* | to put forward an argument | |
| *zanjar una polémica, una discusión* | to settle a dispute | |
| *conciliar el sueño* | to get to sleep | |
| *presentar la dimisión* | to resign | *dar la dimisión* is also common |
| *formular una pregunta, una queja* | to put a question, lodge a complaint | |
| *prestar atención, ayuda* | to pay attention, give help | |
| *cundir el pánico* | for panic to spread | |

### Register within one sentence

Mixing registers is the commonest stylistic slip at advanced level: *Me dirijo a usted para decirle que su perro me tiene frito* starts formal and collapses into slang. Choose a register and keep to it, unless the mix is deliberate — for humour, or for a quotation.

</Section>

<Section id="emphasis" kind="grammar" title="Word order and emphasis">

Spanish word order is flexible, but not free: each order answers a different question. The rule of thumb is that **new information goes last**, and anything moved to the front becomes the topic or the focus.

### Subject after the verb

- *Ha llamado **tu madre**.* — answers "What happened?" or "Who called?": the subject is new.
- ***Tu madre** ha llamado.* — answers "What about my mother?": she is the topic.

### Fronting an object: topic and focus

- **Topic** with a resumptive pronoun: ***Ese libro** ya **lo** he leído.* · ***A tu hermana la** vi ayer.* When a direct object is moved to the front as a topic, Spanish repeats it with a clitic pronoun (*lo*, *la*, *los*, *las*). Leaving it out sounds wrong.
- **Contrastive focus**, without the pronoun and with strong stress: ***Dinero** no tengo, pero **tiempo** sí.* · ***Mucho trabajo** nos ha costado.*

### Cleft sentences

| Structure | Example |
| --- | --- |
| *Ser … quien / el que* | ***Fue Marta quien** lo dijo.* — It was Marta who said it. |
| *Lo que … es* | ***Lo que necesitamos es** tiempo.* |
| *Es … donde / cuando / como* | ***Es aquí donde** se firmó el tratado.* |
| *Es por eso que* | Very widespread, especially in Latin America; some style guides prefer *por eso* or *es por eso por lo que*. |

In Spain, *sí que* also adds emphasis: *Esto **sí que** es un problema* — Now *this* is a problem.

### Adjective position

Before the noun, many adjectives are subjective or figurative; after it, objective and literal:

| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| *un **pobre** hombre* — a poor (pitiable) man | *un hombre **pobre*** — a poor (penniless) man |
| *un **gran** poeta* — a great poet | *un poeta **grande*** — a big poet |
| *un **viejo** amigo* — an old (long-standing) friend | *un amigo **viejo*** — an elderly friend |
| ***cierta** noticia* — a certain piece of news | *una noticia **cierta*** — a true piece of news |
| *mi **única** oportunidad* — my only chance | *una oportunidad **única*** — a unique opportunity |
| *un **simple** empleado* — a mere employee | *un empleado **simple*** — a simple-minded employee |
| *el **antiguo** director* — the former director | *un mueble **antiguo*** — an antique piece of furniture |
| *el **mismo** director* — the same director | *el director **mismo*** — the director himself |

</Section>

<Section id="brevity" kind="grammar" title="The elegance of brevity">

The great stylists of Spanish prized concision; a well-known saying in Spain credits the Golden Age writer Baltasar Gracián with the idea that *lo bueno, si breve, dos veces bueno* — the good, if brief, is twice as good. Three habits make prose lighter.

**1. Cut pleonasms** (*pleonasmos*), words that repeat what is already said:

| Redundant | Concise |
| --- | --- |
| *subir arriba*, *bajar abajo* | *subir*, *bajar* |
| *volver a repetir* (the first time) | *repetir* |
| *hace dos años atrás* | *hace dos años* or *dos años atrás* |
| *el erario público* | *el erario* (it is public by definition) |
| *prever de antemano* | *prever* |
| *en el día de hoy* | *hoy* |

(Emphatic redundancy is normal in speech: *¡Lo vi con mis propios ojos!* — in writing, keep it for effect.)

**2. Replace wildcard verbs** (*verbos comodín*: *hacer, poner, tener, dar, decir*) with precise ones:

| Vague | Precise |
| --- | --- |
| *hacer un informe* | *redactar un informe* |
| *poner una multa* | *imponer una multa* |
| *tener una enfermedad* | *padecer una enfermedad* |
| *decir un secreto* | *revelar un secreto* |
| *hacer un túnel* | *excavar un túnel* |

**3. Undo empty nominalisations** (see <LessonLink slug="professional-and-academic">Professional and academic Spanish</LessonLink>): *proceder a la apertura* → *abrir*; *realizar una visita* → *visitar*.

<Example title="Before and after">

**Before** (69 words): *En el día de hoy, desde la dirección del centro, queremos poner en conocimiento de todas las familias que se va a proceder a la realización de unas obras de mejora en el edificio, las cuales van a tener lugar durante el periodo de tiempo comprendido entre los días 3 y 14 de julio, motivo por el cual el centro va a permanecer cerrado durante dicho periodo de tiempo.*

**After** (25 words): *La dirección informa a las familias de que el centro permanecerá cerrado del 3 al 14 de julio por obras de mejora en el edificio.*

Gone: *en el día de hoy*, *poner en conocimiento*, *proceder a la realización de*, *periodo de tiempo* (twice), *motivo por el cual*, *dicho*. Nothing of substance was lost.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="address" kind="culture" title="Register begins with how you address people">

In Spanish, register is set before the first full stop — by the choice between *tú*, *usted* and *vos*.

- **Spain** has moved strongly towards *tú* since the late twentieth century: among colleagues, in shops, often with strangers of your own age. *Usted* remains for the elderly, for formal service and for official writing.
- **Much of Latin America** keeps *usted* longer and more widely, especially with older people, superiors and in service encounters.
- **Colombia and Costa Rica** have a notable *ustedeo*: *usted* used even among family and friends, with affection rather than distance. In the Colombian highlands of Boyacá and Cundinamarca you will also hear *sumercé* (from *su merced*), warm and respectful at once.
- **Voseo** regions (Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, much of Central America and parts of other countries) use *vos* as the informal pronoun, and in Argentina and Uruguay it is the standard informal form, heard on television and in advertising as well as at home.

Titles add another layer: *don* and *doña* + first name (*don Ernesto*, *doña Rosa*) show respect with warmth; *licenciado*, *ingeniero* or *doctor* as forms of address are common in Mexico and elsewhere, rarer in Spain.

Email closings mark register too: *Un saludo* and *Saludos cordiales* (neutral to formal), *Atentamente* (formal), *Cordialmente* (very common in Colombia), *Quedo atento / atenta* (common in Latin America: "I await your reply"), *Un abrazo* (friendly).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Formal means long."** The formal version of the dog letter is two sentences. Formality lies in vocabulary, address and formulas, not length. Long, padded prose is not formal; it is *burocratés*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Synonyms are interchangeable."** *Fallecer* in a joke and *estirar la pata* in an obituary are both disasters. Every synonym carries its register, connotation and collocations.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Spanish word order is free."** Grammatically, many orders are possible; communicatively, each answers a different question. *Ese libro lo he leído* and *He leído ese libro* do not mean quite the same — and *Ese libro he leído*, without the clitic, sounds wrong unless *ese libro* carries contrastive stress.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Literary style means rare words."** The literary version above uses everyday words (*calle*, *perro*, *cama*); its effect comes from images, rhythm and a surprising short sentence.

</Misconception>

</Section>
