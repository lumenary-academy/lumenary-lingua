<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="Out into town">

With the present tense from <LessonLink slug="daily-routine">lesson 5</LessonLink>, you can describe your day. Now it's time to go out: to a café, to the market, to the museum on the other side of the square. You'll need to say what you like (with a verb that works backwards compared with English), order something to eat, ask whether there is a pharmacy nearby, and follow the answer. That last part means learning one very irregular and very common verb, **ir** (to go).

</Section>

<Section id="gustar" kind="grammar" title="Gustar: what you like">

English says "I like coffee": *I* is the subject. Spanish turns it round: <Say lang="es">Me gusta el café.</Say>, literally "Coffee is pleasing to me". The thing liked is the subject, so the verb agrees with **it**:

- one thing, or an activity: **gusta**. <Say lang="es">Me gusta el chocolate.</Say> <Say lang="es">Me gusta cocinar.</Say>
- several things: **gustan**. <Say lang="es">Me gustan las manzanas.</Say>

The person who likes is shown with a pronoun before the verb:

| To whom | Pronoun | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (a mí) | <Say lang="es">me</Say> | <Say lang="es">Me gusta el té.</Say> I like tea. |
| (a ti) | <Say lang="es">te</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Te gustan los tacos?</Say> Do you like tacos? |
| (a él, a ella, a usted) | <Say lang="es">le</Say> | <Say lang="es">Le gusta leer.</Say> He/She likes reading; you like reading. |
| (a nosotros, a nosotras) | <Say lang="es">nos</Say> | <Say lang="es">Nos gusta el parque.</Say> We like the park. |
| (a vosotros, a vosotras) | <Say lang="es">os</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Os gusta Madrid?</Say> Do you all like Madrid? |
| (a ellos, a ellas, a ustedes) | <Say lang="es">les</Say> | <Say lang="es">Les gustan los perros.</Say> They like dogs. |

The phrase in brackets (*a mí, a ti, a ella*…) is optional. Add it for emphasis or contrast, or to make *le* and *les* clear: <Say lang="es">A Lucía le gusta el pescado, pero a mí no.</Say> (Lucía likes fish, but I don't.) Notice *a mí* has an accent (*mí* = me), and *a ti* doesn't. When you name the person, you still keep the pronoun: *A Lucía **le** gusta*.

The thing liked usually takes *el, la, los, las*, because you're talking about it in general: *Me gusta **el** café*, not *me gusta café*.

**Encantar** (to love, as in "I love chocolate") works the same way: <Say lang="es">Me encanta el chocolate.</Say> <Say lang="es">Nos encantan los mercados.</Say> Don't add *mucho* to *encantar*; it's already strong.

### Me too, me neither

| Someone says | You agree | You disagree |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">Me gusta el café.</Say> | <Say lang="es">A mí también.</Say> (Me too.) | <Say lang="es">A mí no.</Say> (I don't.) |
| <Say lang="es">No me gusta el café.</Say> | <Say lang="es">A mí tampoco.</Say> (Me neither.) | <Say lang="es">A mí sí.</Say> (I do.) |

With ordinary verbs, use the subject pronoun instead: <Say lang="es">Yo vivo en Quito.</Say> <Say lang="es">Yo también.</Say>

</Section>

<Section id="ordering" kind="grammar" title="Ordering in a café">

Café Spanish is mostly fixed phrases. Learn these as blocks:

| The waiter says | You say |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">¿Qué desea?</Say> / <Say lang="es">¿Qué desean?</Say> What would you like? | <Say lang="es">Para mí, un café con leche, por favor.</Say> For me, a white coffee, please. |
| <Say lang="es">¿Qué le pongo?</Say> (Spain) What can I get you? | <Say lang="es">¿Me pone una tostada?</Say> (Spain) Could I have a slice of toast? |
| <Say lang="es">¿Para tomar?</Say> To drink? | <Say lang="es">¿Me da un jugo de naranja?</Say> (Latin America) Could I have an orange juice? |
| <Say lang="es">¿Algo más?</Say> Anything else? | <Say lang="es">Nada más, gracias.</Say> Nothing else, thanks. |
| <Say lang="es">¿Para aquí o para llevar?</Say> To have here or to take away? | <Say lang="es">Para llevar.</Say> To take away. |
| | <Say lang="es">La cuenta, por favor.</Say> / <Say lang="es">¿Cuánto es?</Say> The bill, please. / How much is it? |

You can also simply say <Say lang="es">Quiero un té, por favor.</Say> With *por favor* and a friendly tone, it's not rude. A softer form you can learn as a phrase is <Say lang="es">Quisiera un té, por favor.</Say> (I'd like a tea, please.)

</Section>

<Section id="hay-estar" kind="grammar" title="Hay or está? Finding places">

Spanish has two ways of answering "is there…?" and "where is…?":

**hay** (there is, there are) says that something **exists** somewhere. It never changes for singular or plural, and it goes with *un, una*, numbers, or no article:

- <Say lang="es">¿Hay una farmacia por aquí?</Say> Is there a pharmacy around here?
- <Say lang="es">En la plaza hay dos restaurantes y una iglesia.</Say>
- <Say lang="es">No hay cajeros en esta calle.</Say> There are no cash machines in this street.

**estar** says **where** a particular, known thing is. It goes with *el, la*, names, and possessives:

- <Say lang="es">¿Dónde está la estación?</Say> Where's the station?
- <Say lang="es">El museo está al lado de la iglesia.</Say>
- <Say lang="es">Mis padres están en el mercado.</Say>

A quick test: if English could say "*a* pharmacy" or "*some* pharmacies", use *hay*; if it says "*the* pharmacy", use *está*.

</Section>

<Section id="ir" kind="grammar" title="Ir: to go, and the contractions al and del">

| | ir |
| --- | --- |
| yo | <Say lang="es">voy</Say> |
| tú | <Say lang="es">vas</Say> |
| él, ella, usted | <Say lang="es">va</Say> |
| nosotros, nosotras | <Say lang="es">vamos</Say> |
| vosotros, vosotras | <Say lang="es">vais</Say> |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | <Say lang="es">van</Say> |

*Ir* looks nothing like its infinitive, so learn it by heart. The place you go to is introduced by **a**: <Say lang="es">Voy a la biblioteca.</Say> To ask where to, use <Say lang="es">¿Adónde vas?</Say> (also written *¿A dónde vas?*). *Vamos* also means "let's go": <Say lang="es">¡Vamos!</Say>

**Two compulsory contractions.** When *a* or *de* meets the article *el*, they merge:

- **a + el = al**: <Say lang="es">Vamos al parque.</Say> (never *a el parque*)
- **de + el = del**: <Say lang="es">El banco está cerca del hotel.</Say> (never *de el hotel*)

Only *el* contracts: *a la plaza, a los museos, de las tiendas* stay as they are. And *él* (he) doesn't contract: *el libro de él*. Many location phrases end in *de*, so you will say *del* a lot: <Say lang="es">al lado del banco</Say>, <Say lang="es">enfrente del museo</Say>, <Say lang="es">lejos del centro</Say>.

### Asking and giving directions

To ask: <Say lang="es">Perdone, ¿dónde está la estación?</Say> or <Say lang="es">¿Cómo llego al museo?</Say> (How do I get to the museum?). *Perdone* is the polite "excuse me" to a stranger (*perdona* with *tú*).

Directions are often given in the present tense, as a description of what you do:

- <Say lang="es">Sigues todo recto.</Say> You go straight on. (*seguir*, e → i: *sigo, sigues, sigue*)
- <Say lang="es">Giras a la derecha.</Say> You turn right. (Latin America also *doblas*)
- <Say lang="es">Cruzas la plaza.</Say> You cross the square.
- <Say lang="es">Tomas la segunda calle a la izquierda.</Say> You take the second street on the left.
- <Say lang="es">Está a cinco minutos andando.</Say> It's five minutes' walk. (Latin America: *caminando*)

To a stranger you'd use the *usted* forms: *sigue, gira, cruza, toma*. Ordinals you'll need: *primera, segunda, tercera* (first, second, third). In <LessonLink slug="body-health-and-commands">lesson 11</LessonLink> you'll learn the command forms, which are also common in directions.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="A café in Salamanca">

Noura, from Jordan, is studying in Salamanca, in Spain. On a Saturday morning she meets her friend Pilar at a café near the Plaza Mayor.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Camarero | <Say lang="es">Buenos días. ¿Qué os pongo?</Say> | Good morning. What can I get you? |
| Pilar | <Say lang="es">Para mí, un café con leche y una tostada con tomate.</Say> | For me, a white coffee and toast with tomato. |
| Noura | <Say lang="es">¿Me pone un té, por favor? Y un zumo de naranja.</Say> | Could I have a tea, please? And an orange juice. |
| Camarero | <Say lang="es">Muy bien. ¿Algo más?</Say> | Very good. Anything else? |
| Noura | <Say lang="es">No, nada más, gracias.</Say> | No, nothing else, thanks. |
| Pilar | <Say lang="es">¿Te gusta el café de aquí?</Say> | Do you like the coffee here? |
| Noura | <Say lang="es">No me gusta mucho el café. Pero me encantan los churros de esta cafetería.</Say> | I don't like coffee much. But I love the churros at this café. |
| Pilar | <Say lang="es">¡A mí también! Oye, ¿adónde vas esta tarde?</Say> | Me too! Hey, where are you going this afternoon? |
| Noura | <Say lang="es">Voy al museo de arte. ¿Está lejos?</Say> | I'm going to the art museum. Is it far? |
| Pilar | <Say lang="es">No, está cerca. Sigues todo recto por esta calle, cruzas la plaza y giras a la derecha. El museo está al lado de una iglesia muy grande.</Say> | No, it's close. You go straight down this street, cross the square and turn right. The museum is next to a very big church. |
| Noura | <Say lang="es">Perfecto. ¿Y hay un cajero por aquí?</Say> | Perfect. And is there a cash machine around here? |
| Pilar | <Say lang="es">Sí, hay uno en la esquina, enfrente del banco.</Say> | Yes, there's one on the corner, opposite the bank. |
| Noura | <Say lang="es">¡Perdone! La cuenta, por favor.</Say> | Excuse me! The bill, please. |
| Camarero | <Say lang="es">Son seis euros con cincuenta.</Say> | That's six euros fifty. |

**Notes.** The waiter uses *os* (*vosotros*): in Spain, waiters often address young customers informally. In Mexico he might ask *¿Qué van a tomar?*, and Noura might order with *¿Me da un té?* or *¿Me trae un jugo de naranja?* *Uno* in *hay uno* stands for *un cajero*: before a noun it's *un*, alone it's *uno*. *Una tostada con tomate* is toast rubbed or spread with tomato and olive oil, a common Spanish breakfast.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Sounds at the table">

- **Silent h before ue.** <Say lang="es">huevo</Say> begins with a *w* sound: "WEH-vo". Many speakers add a faint *g*: "GWEH-vo".
- **ll and y.** <Say lang="es">pollo</Say>, <Say lang="es">tortilla</Say>, <Say lang="es">calle</Say>: for most speakers, *y* as in *yes* (in Argentina and Uruguay, *sh*).
- **j and ge, gi.** <Say lang="es">jamón</Say>, <Say lang="es">naranja</Say>, <Say lang="es">jugo</Say>, and <Say lang="es">gente</Say>: the breathy throat sound from lesson 1.
- **Contractions run together.** <Say lang="es">al lado del banco</Say> is "a-LA-do-del-BAN-co", with no pauses.
- **ce, ci, z** again: <Say lang="es">cerveza</Say>, <Say lang="es">plaza</Say>, <Say lang="es">izquierda</Say>, <Say lang="es">cerca</Say>: *th* in most of Spain, *s* in Latin America.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Tapas, tortillas and buen provecho">

In Spain, going out often means going from bar to bar with friends, sharing small plates called *tapas*, or larger *raciones*. In some cities, including Granada and León, a small tapa traditionally comes free with each drink. People usually pay at the end, not round by round, and to call the waiter you say *¡Perdone!* or *¡Por favor!*, never snap your fingers. Tipping is modest in Spain: leaving some coins or rounding up is common, but not expected the way it is in some countries. In Mexico, a tip of around 10 to 15 per cent in restaurants is customary.

Words for food travel badly. A *tortilla* in Madrid is a thick omelette of eggs, potatoes and often onion; in Mexico City it's the flat round maize bread that holds your tacos. *Zumo* and *jugo*, *bocadillo* and *torta* (a Mexican sandwich) are just as different. When in doubt, ask: <Say lang="es">¿Qué es esto?</Say> (What's this?)

Finally, a custom worth adopting: when you walk past people who are eating, or sit down to eat with others, wish them a good meal: <Say lang="es">¡Buen provecho!</Say> (or, in Spain, <Say lang="es">¡Que aproveche!</Say>). In Mexico it's common to say it even to strangers in a restaurant as you leave.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"I like coffee" is *Yo gusto el café*. *Gustar* means "to be pleasing", so the coffee is the subject: **Me gusta el café**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Gusta* is always singular." It agrees with the thing liked: **Me gustan los tacos** (plural), **Me gusta el taco** (singular), **Me gusta comer** (an activity: singular).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Hay el banco en la plaza.*" *Hay* doesn't go with *the*. Either **Hay un banco en la plaza** (there's a bank) or **El banco está en la plaza** (the bank is in the square).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Voy a el parque* is fine if I say it carefully." *A* + *el* must contract: **Voy al parque**. Likewise **del**, never *de el*.

</Misconception>

</Section>
