<Section id="archaic-words" kind="vocabulary" title="Words and forms of the Golden Age">

The *Siglo de Oro* — roughly from Garcilaso in the early sixteenth century to Calderón's death in 1681 — produced some of the greatest literature in any language. Its Spanish is *early modern* Spanish, not Old Spanish: a C2 reader can follow most of it, as long as they know a few dozen words and forms that have changed. These are the ones you will meet most.

<Section id="address" kind="grammar" title="Tú, vos and vuestra merced">

The biggest change between the Golden Age and today is in **forms of address**, and it explains the modern map of *tú*, *vos* and *usted* that you met in <LessonLink slug="regional-spanish">Regional Spanish</LessonLink>.

| Form | Verb | Used in the Golden Age for | What became of it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *tú* | 2nd singular: *tienes* | intimates, children, servants, inferiors | modern informal *tú* |
| *vos* | 2nd plural endings: *tenéis*, *sois* | originally polite; by 1600 used among equals in intimacy, or to inferiors — and an insult to a superior | lost in Spain; survives as *voseo* in the Americas (*vos tenés*, *vos sos*) |
| *vuestra merced* | 3rd singular: *tiene* | respect: to social superiors, strangers of rank, masters | contracted to *usted* |
| *vosotros* | *tenéis* | plural address of any kind | kept in Spain; replaced by *ustedes* in the Americas |

*Vos* had a curious history: it began as a plural used for a single respected person, wore out through overuse, and slid down the social scale until calling a nobleman *vos* could start a quarrel. As it fell, Spaniards needed a new respectful form and found it in titles: *vuestra merced* (your grace), *vuestra señoría* (your lordship), *vuestra excelencia*. Because they were nouns, they took a third-person verb — which is why *usted* still does. Spoken quickly, *vuestra merced* wore down through forms such as *vuesa merced*, *vuesarced* and *voacé* to **usted**.

In *Don Quijote*, the system is on display: Sancho addresses his master as *vuestra merced*, and Don Quijote mostly answers *tú*. In New Spain, Sor Juana writes to a plural audience with *vosotros* forms (*acusáis*), as a Mexican poet naturally did then; *ustedes* as the only plural is a later development in the Americas.

</Section>

<Section id="grammar-then" kind="grammar" title="Archaic grammar, modernised">

A handful of grammatical habits account for most of the strangeness of Golden Age prose and verse.

| Golden Age | Modern | What is happening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Díjole que se fuese.* | *Le dijo que se fuera.* | Object pronouns could follow a conjugated verb. (Formal Spanish keeps fossils: *véase*, *dícese*.) |
| *Fuese.* | *Se fue.* | Careful: *fuese* may be *se fue* (he left), not the subjunctive. |
| *no ha mucho tiempo* | *no hace mucho tiempo* | *Haber*, not *hacer*, expressed elapsed time. |
| *He de decir…* | *Tengo que decir…* / *Diré…* | *Haber de* + infinitive: obligation or future. Still alive in formal Spanish. |
| *si alguno lo dijere…* | *si alguien lo dice…* | The **future subjunctive** (-*re*): hypothetical future events. |
| *decille, hacello* | *decirle, hacerlo* | The *r* of the infinitive assimilated to *l*, especially in verse for rhyme. |
| *aqueste, deste* | *este, de este* | Old demonstratives and contractions. |

The future subjunctive deserves a note. Golden Age Spanish had three subjunctive tenses where we have two: *diga*, *dijera/dijese* — and *dijere*. The last vanished from speech, but survives in **legal texts** (*el que infringiere esta norma…*, as you saw in <LessonLink slug="style-and-register">Style and register</LessonLink>), in a few fixed phrases (*sea lo que fuere*) and in a proverb everyone still knows:

> *Donde fueres, haz lo que vieres.* — Wherever you go, do as you see done.

</Section>

<Section id="spelling" kind="pronunciation" title="Spelling and sound">

Before the **Real Academia Española** was founded (1713) and published its *Diccionario de autoridades* (1726–1739) and its first *Ortografía* (1741), Spanish spelling was a matter of habit, printer and region. First editions look stranger than the language really is. Modern editions usually update the spelling; facsimiles do not.

| Old spelling | Modern | Sound behind it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *dixo*, *baxo*, *Quixote* | *dijo*, *bajo*, *Quijote* | *x* once spelled a "sh" sound /ʃ/, which became the modern *jota* /x/. |
| *coraçón*, *plaça* | *corazón*, *plaza* | *ç* (c cedilla) spelled a sound like "ts", which became /θ/ or /s/. |
| *passar*, *missa* | *pasar*, *misa* | *ss* was a voiceless /s/, *s* between vowels a voiced /z/. |
| *vno*, *auer*, *diuino* | *uno*, *haber*, *divino* | *u* and *v* were variants of one letter. |
| *ayre*, *reyna*, *Ysabel* | *aire*, *reina*, *Isabel* | *y* and *i* were interchangeable. |
| *quando*, *qual* | *cuando*, *cual* | *qu* before *a* and *o* lasted into the nineteenth century. |
| *theatro*, *philosophía*, *christiano* | *teatro*, *filosofía*, *cristiano* | Learned Greek and Latin spellings. |
| *fazer* (medieval) → *hazer* → *hacer* | *hacer* | Latin *f-* became an *h* that was still pronounced in the sixteenth century in many places. |

Behind the spellings lies a real revolution in pronunciation. Medieval Spanish had six sibilant sounds; during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries they collapsed. The "sh" and "zh" sounds became the guttural **jota**. The "ts" and "dz" sounds became the **/θ/** of *zapato* in northern and central Spain, but merged with **/s/** in Andalusia — and since the ships to the Americas and the Canaries sailed from Seville and Cádiz, that **seseo** became the pronunciation of most of the Spanish-speaking world. That is also why *Don Quixote* is still spelled with an *x* in English, and why some names kept their old letter: **México**, *Oaxaca* and *Texas* preserve the *x* that once spelled "sh", now pronounced as a *jota*.

</Section>

<Section id="four-voices" kind="reading" title="Four voices, a line or two each">

Each quotation below is a line or a sentence, followed by a modern gloss. All four writers are long in the public domain; read their complete works at the [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/) or [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/).

### Garcilaso de la Vega (c. 1500–1536)

A soldier and courtier who, with his friend Juan Boscán, brought the Italian sonnet and the eleven-syllable line into Spanish. He died of wounds received in battle, and his poems were published posthumously in 1543. His Sonnet XXIII is the great Spanish *carpe diem*: enjoy your youth before time takes it. It opens:

> *En tanto que de rosa y azucena*
> *se muestra la color en vuestro gesto*
>
> — Garcilaso de la Vega, Soneto XXIII

**Modern gloss:** *Mientras en tu rostro se ve el color de la rosa y de la azucena…* — "While your face shows the colour of the rose and the lily…" Notice:

- *En tanto que* = *mientras*.
- *la color*: feminine, as often then.
- *gesto* = *rostro*, face — not "gesture".
- *vuestro*: the poet addresses the lady with *vos*, respectful in the 1530s.
- The rose (red) and the lily (white, *azucena*) are the Renaissance colours of a young complexion; the sonnet will end with time changing them.

### Miguel de Cervantes (1547–1616)

*Don Quijote de la Mancha* (1605 and 1615) begins with perhaps the most famous sentence in Spanish:

> *En un lugar de la Mancha, de cuyo nombre no quiero acordarme, no ha mucho tiempo que vivía un hidalgo de los de lanza en astillero, adarga antigua, rocín flaco y galgo corredor.*
>
> — Miguel de Cervantes, *Don Quijote*, I, 1

**Modern gloss:** *En un pueblo de la Mancha, cuyo nombre no quiero recordar, vivía hace poco un hidalgo de esos que tienen una lanza en el soporte, un escudo viejo, un caballo flaco y un galgo de caza.* Notice *lugar* (village), *no ha mucho tiempo* (= *no hace mucho*), *lanza en astillero* (a lance on the rack — unused, like its owner), and the list of four possessions that sketches a poor gentleman.

Later, when the knight charges at windmills, his squire tries to stop him with the respectful form of address:

> *Mire vuestra merced… que aquellos que allí se parecen no son gigantes, sino molinos de viento*
>
> — *Don Quijote*, I, 8

**Modern gloss:** *Mire usted… que aquellos que se ven allí no son gigantes, sino molinos de viento.* *Mire* is third person because *vuestra merced* is grammatically a noun; *se parecen* means "can be seen", an old sense of *parecer*.

### Francisco de Quevedo (1580–1645)

The master of **conceptismo**, a style of dense wit, wordplay and paradox, and a ferocious satirist. One of his satirical sonnets opens with a line that has made Spanish readers laugh for four centuries:

> *Érase un hombre a una nariz pegado*
>
> — Francisco de Quevedo, "A una nariz"

**Modern gloss:** *Había una vez un hombre pegado a una nariz.* The hyperbole inverts the order of things: the man is attached to the nose, not the other way round. *Érase* (= *era* + *se*) is the old fairy-tale opening, still alive in *Érase una vez*.

And in a very different key, the last line of his sonnet on love that outlasts death:

> *polvo serán, mas polvo enamorado.*
>
> — Quevedo, "Amor constante más allá de la muerte"

**Modern gloss:** *Serán polvo, pero polvo enamorado* — "They will be dust, but dust in love." *Mas* = *pero*. The paradox answers the old saying that we are dust and return to dust: yes, says the poet, but the dust will still love.

### Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (1648 or 1651–1695)

A Mexican nun, poet, playwright and scholar, the outstanding writer of colonial New Spain. In her *Respuesta a sor Filotea de la Cruz* (1691) she defended women's right to study. Her best-known satirical poem, in quatrains of eight-syllable lines, begins:

> *Hombres necios que acusáis*
> *a la mujer sin razón*
>
> — Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, Redondillas

**Modern gloss:** *Hombres necios, que acusan a la mujer sin razón…* — "Foolish men, who accuse women without reason…" *Acusáis* is the *vosotros* form, normal in seventeenth-century Mexico; a modern Mexican would say *acusan*. The poem goes on to argue that men blame women for the very faults they themselves provoke.

</Section>

<Section id="siglo-de-oro" kind="culture" title="The Golden Age in context">

The Golden Age coincides with Spain's moment as a world empire, and its literature is as varied as that world.

- **1492**: Antonio de Nebrija publishes his *Gramática de la lengua castellana*, one of the first grammars of a modern European language.
- **Poetry**: Garcilaso and Boscán bring Italian forms (1520s–1530s); the mystics Teresa de Ávila and Juan de la Cruz write of the soul's search for God; Luis de Góngora's dazzling **culteranismo**, full of Latinate syntax and rare words, is answered by Quevedo's **conceptismo**, built on wit and compression.
- **The novel**: the anonymous *Lazarillo de Tormes* (1554) founds the picaresque; Cervantes's *Quijote* invents the modern novel.
- **Theatre**: Lope de Vega writes hundreds of plays and a manifesto, *Arte nuevo de hacer comedias* (1609), for a theatre that mixes tragedy and comedy; Calderón de la Barca gives it philosophy in *La vida es sueño* (1635).
- **The Americas**: the Inca Garcilaso de la Vega's *Comentarios reales* (1609) tells the history of Peru from an Inca and Spanish perspective; Sor Juana writes in New Spain.

### Reading Golden Age texts today

Start with a **modern-spelling edition with notes** (*edición anotada*); Spanish students read the *Quijote* that way, and the Centro Virtual Cervantes offers an annotated version online. Read Golden Age theatre aloud: it was written in verse to be heard. Keep this lesson's table at hand, and remember that when a sentence seems impossible, the culprit is usually one of five things: *vos* or *vuestra merced*, an enclitic pronoun, a future subjunctive, an old meaning (*gesto*, *luego*, *parecer*), or hyperbaton.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Cervantes wrote in Old Spanish."** Old Spanish (*castellano medieval*) is the language of the *Cantar de mio Cid*, around 1200, and it really does need translation. Cervantes wrote early modern Spanish: the grammar and most of the vocabulary are yours already.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"*Vos* was always the informal pronoun."** It began as a respectful form, then slid down the social scale. In Garcilaso it is courtly; by Cervantes's time it could be intimate or insulting depending on who used it to whom.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The *x* in *México* is a mistake."** It is the old spelling of a sound that became the *jota*. Mexico kept it by choice and tradition; the RAE recommends *México* and accepts *Méjico*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"*Luego* means 'later'."** In Golden Age texts it usually means "at once" — *luego, luego* is "immediately". The modern sense came later (though Mexican *luego luego* still means "right away").

</Misconception>

</Section>
