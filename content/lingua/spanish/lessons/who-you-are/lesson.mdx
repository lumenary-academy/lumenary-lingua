<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="Beyond your name">

In <LessonLink slug="sounds-and-greetings">the first lesson</LessonLink> you learned to greet people and give your name with *me llamo*. Now you can say much more about yourself: where you are from, what nationality you are, what you do. All of it rests on one verb, **ser** (to be), and on two ideas that run through the whole language: every noun is masculine or feminine, and words that describe people change their endings to match.

</Section>

<Section id="pronouns" kind="grammar" title="Subject pronouns">

| | Singular | | Plural | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st person | <Say lang="es">yo</Say> | I | <Say lang="es">nosotros</Say>, <Say lang="es">nosotras</Say> | we |
| 2nd person, informal | <Say lang="es">tú</Say> (*vos*) | you | <Say lang="es">vosotros</Say>, <Say lang="es">vosotras</Say> | you all (Spain) |
| 3rd person | <Say lang="es">él</Say>, <Say lang="es">ella</Say> | he, she | <Say lang="es">ellos</Say>, <Say lang="es">ellas</Say> | they |
| "you", formal | <Say lang="es">usted</Say> | you | <Say lang="es">ustedes</Say> | you all |

Four things to notice:

1. **The -as forms are for all-female groups.** *Nosotras* is "we" when every one of us is female; one man in the group and it becomes *nosotros*. The same goes for *vosotras* and *ellas*.
2. **Usted and ustedes take third-person verbs.** They come from an old courtesy title, *vuestra merced*, "your grace", so grammatically you speak *about* the person, like saying "how is Your Majesty?". That is why the formal question is *¿Cómo está usted?* with the same verb as *él* and *ella*.
3. **Vosotros or ustedes?** In most of Spain, *vosotros* is the plural of *tú* (friends, family) and *ustedes* the plural of *usted* (formal). In Latin America (and the Canary Islands and parts of Andalusia) *ustedes* is used for every group, formal or not: a Mexican mother says *ustedes* to her own children. Learn to recognise *vosotros*; use whichever matches the Spanish you hear most.
4. **Spanish usually drops the subject pronoun.** The verb ending already says who is meant: <Say lang="es">soy de Lima</Say> can only mean "I am from Lima". Pronouns are used for contrast or emphasis: <Say lang="es">Yo soy de Lima. ¿Y tú?</Say> (*I*'m from Lima. And you?). And there is no word for "it" as a subject: "It's a book" is just <Say lang="es">Es un libro</Say>.

</Section>

<Section id="ser" kind="grammar" title="Ser: to be">

| Pronoun | ser | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yo | <Say lang="es">soy</Say> | <Say lang="es">Soy de Kenia.</Say> I'm from Kenya. |
| tú | <Say lang="es">eres</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Eres colombiana?</Say> Are you Colombian? |
| él, ella, usted | <Say lang="es">es</Say> | <Say lang="es">Usted es médico, ¿verdad?</Say> You're a doctor, aren't you? |
| nosotros, nosotras | <Say lang="es">somos</Say> | <Say lang="es">Somos estudiantes.</Say> We're students. |
| vosotros, vosotras | <Say lang="es">sois</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Sois de Madrid?</Say> Are you (all) from Madrid? |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | <Say lang="es">son</Say> | <Say lang="es">Ellas son periodistas.</Say> They're journalists. |

With *vos*, the form is <Say lang="es-AR">sos</Say>: *¿Vos sos de Buenos Aires?*

Spanish has two verbs for "to be", *ser* and *estar*. You have already met *estar* in *¿Cómo estás?*; it gets its proper introduction in <LessonLink slug="family-and-descriptions">lesson 4</LessonLink>. For now, use *ser* for these:

- **identity:** <Say lang="es">Soy Ana.</Say> <Say lang="es">Es el profesor.</Say> (He's the teacher.)
- **origin**, with *de*: <Say lang="es">Somos de Bogotá.</Say>
- **nationality:** <Say lang="es">Es japonesa.</Say>
- **profession:** <Say lang="es">Soy enfermero.</Say>
- **what something is:** <Say lang="es">Es una guitarra.</Say>

Notice: **no article before a bare profession.** English says "I'm *a* nurse"; Spanish says <Say lang="es">Soy enfermero</Say>. The article comes back only when you add a description, a point we'll return to with adjectives.

</Section>

<Section id="agreement" kind="grammar" title="Nationalities: matching the person">

A nationality changes its ending to agree with the person it describes, in gender and number. There are three patterns.

| Masculine ends in… | Feminine | Plural | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| -o | -o → -a | add -s | *mexicano, mexicana, mexicanos, mexicanas* |
| a consonant | add -a | add -es / -s | *español, española, españoles, españolas*; *inglés, inglesa, ingleses, inglesas* |
| -e or -í | no change | add -s / -es | *canadiense, canadienses*; *marroquí, marroquíes* |

Look at *inglés → inglesa*: the accent disappears. That is the stress rule from lesson 1 at work. *Inglés* ends in -s but is stressed on the last syllable, so it needs an accent; *inglesa* ends in a vowel and is stressed on the second-to-last syllable, so it doesn't. The same happens with *alemán → alemana* and *japonés → japonesa*.

A group of men and women takes the masculine plural: Tomás and Lucía are *españoles*.

Two spelling points that surprise English speakers: **nationalities and languages are written with a small letter** (*Es inglesa. Son mexicanos. El español es una lengua.*), while countries and cities take a capital (*Inglaterra, Madrid*). And the name of a language is usually the masculine singular nationality: *el español, el inglés, el francés, el chino*.

</Section>

<Section id="nouns" kind="grammar" title="Nouns, gender and articles">

Every Spanish noun is masculine or feminine, even nouns for things. Often the ending tells you:

- **-o is usually masculine:** *el libro* (book), *el vaso* (glass).
- **-a is usually feminine:** *la casa* (house), *la guitarra*.
- **-ción, -sión, -dad, -tad are feminine:** *la nación*, *la televisión*, *la ciudad* (city), *la libertad*.
- **-e and other consonants could be either:** *el nombre* (name), *la noche* (night), *el hotel*, *la flor* (flower). Learn these with their article.

Some common words break the pattern: <Say lang="es">el día</Say> (day), <Say lang="es">el mapa</Say> (map), <Say lang="es">el problema</Say> (problem), <Say lang="es">la mano</Say> (hand), <Say lang="es">la foto</Say> (photo, short for *la fotografía*). For people, the article often does the work: *el estudiante, la estudiante*; *el periodista, la periodista*.

**Plurals.** Add **-s** after a vowel (*libros, casas*), **-es** after a consonant (*ciudades, profesores, hoteles*), and change **-z to -ces** (*el lápiz, los lápices*: pencil). As with *inglesa*, an accent may come or go: *la nación, las naciones*.

**Articles** agree with their noun:

| | Masculine | Feminine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the (singular) | <Say lang="es">el</Say> libro | <Say lang="es">la</Say> casa |
| the (plural) | <Say lang="es">los</Say> libros | <Say lang="es">las</Say> casas |
| a, an | <Say lang="es">un</Say> libro | <Say lang="es">una</Say> casa |
| some | <Say lang="es">unos</Say> libros | <Say lang="es">unas</Say> casas |

</Section>

<Section id="questions" kind="grammar" title="Saying no and asking questions">

**Negation** is easy: put **no** directly before the verb.

- <Say lang="es">No soy de Madrid.</Say> I'm not from Madrid.
- <Say lang="es">Ella no es médica.</Say> She isn't a doctor.

To answer a question with "no", you often say *no* twice: the first is the answer, the second makes the verb negative. <Say lang="es">¿Eres estudiante? No, no soy estudiante.</Say>

**Yes/no questions.** Spanish has no helper word like English *do*. You can keep the word order of a statement and simply raise your voice at the end, or put the subject after the verb:

- <Say lang="es">¿Ella es argentina?</Say> / <Say lang="es">¿Es ella argentina?</Say> / <Say lang="es">¿Es argentina?</Say> Is she Argentinian?
- <Say lang="es">¿Usted es abogado?</Say> / <Say lang="es">¿Es usted abogado?</Say> Are you a lawyer?

Adding <Say lang="es">¿no?</Say> or <Say lang="es">¿verdad?</Say> at the end works like "isn't it?", "aren't you?": <Say lang="es">Eres de Quito, ¿no?</Say>

**Wh- questions** put the question word first, then the verb, then the subject if there is one: <Say lang="es">¿De dónde es Kofi?</Say> (Where is Kofi from?), <Say lang="es">¿Quién es ella?</Say> (Who is she?), <Say lang="es">¿Qué es esto?</Say> (What's this?). Note *¿De dónde…?*: the preposition *de* comes first, never at the end as in English "Where are you from?".

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="At a language exchange in Mexico City">

Every Thursday, a café in Mexico City hosts an *intercambio*, where people meet to practise languages. Ximena is from the city. Priya, from India, is learning Spanish. Then two visitors from Spain arrive.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ximena | <Say lang="es">¡Hola! Soy Ximena. ¿Cómo te llamas?</Say> | Hi! I'm Ximena. What's your name? |
| Priya | <Say lang="es">Hola, me llamo Priya. Mucho gusto.</Say> | Hi, my name's Priya. Nice to meet you. |
| Ximena | <Say lang="es">Igualmente. ¿De dónde eres?</Say> | Likewise. Where are you from? |
| Priya | <Say lang="es">Soy de la India, de Delhi. Y tú eres mexicana, ¿no?</Say> | I'm from India, from Delhi. And you're Mexican, right? |
| Ximena | <Say lang="es">Sí, soy de aquí. ¿Eres estudiante?</Say> | Yes, I'm from here. Are you a student? |
| Priya | <Say lang="es">No, no soy estudiante. Soy enfermera. ¿Y tú? ¿A qué te dedicas?</Say> | No, I'm not a student. I'm a nurse. And you? What do you do? |
| Ximena | <Say lang="es">Soy ingeniera. Ah, ellos son Lucía y Tomás. Son españoles.</Say> | I'm an engineer. Oh, that's Lucía and Tomás. They're Spanish. |
| Lucía | <Say lang="es">¡Hola! ¿Qué tal? ¿Sois de aquí?</Say> | Hi! How's it going? Are you (two) from here? |
| Ximena | <Say lang="es">Yo sí. Ella no, es de la India. ¿Ustedes son de Madrid?</Say> | I am. She isn't, she's from India. Are you (two) from Madrid? |
| Tomás | <Say lang="es">No, somos de Sevilla. Lucía es profesora y yo soy periodista.</Say> | No, we're from Seville. Lucía is a teacher and I'm a journalist. |
| Priya | <Say lang="es">¡Qué bien! Yo también soy de una ciudad grande.</Say> | How nice! I'm from a big city too. |

**Notes.** Lucía, from Spain, asks *¿Sois de aquí?* with *vosotros*; Ximena, from Mexico, asks the same kind of question with *¿Ustedes son…?* Both are speaking informally. *Yo sí* / *Ella no* are short answers: "I am" / "she isn't". *Ximena* is a traditional spelling, pronounced "hee-MEH-nah", with the *x* sounding like *j*, as in *México*. *Grande* (big) is a describing word; you'll learn how these work in lesson 4.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Linking words together">

Spanish speakers run words together when one ends in a vowel and the next begins with one. This is called *sinalefa*, and it's one reason Spanish sounds fast. Practise saying these as one smooth stream:

- <Say lang="es">¿De dónde eres?</Say> sounds like "de-DÓN-de-E-res", with *de* and *eres* joined.
- <Say lang="es">Soy de Estados Unidos.</Say> sounds like "soy-des-TA-do-su-NI-dos".
- <Say lang="es">Es enfermera.</Say> sounds like "e-sen-fer-ME-ra": the final *s* slides onto the next word.

Also practise stress in the masculine/feminine pairs, where the written accent comes and goes: <Say lang="es">inglés</Say>, <Say lang="es">inglesa</Say>; <Say lang="es">alemán</Say>, <Say lang="es">alemana</Say>. The stressed syllable is the same (*-GLÉS*, *-GLE-*); only the spelling rule changes. And take long words slowly, syllable by syllable: *es-ta-dou-ni-DEN-se*.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Two surnames, and who counts as 'American'">

**Two surnames.** In most Spanish-speaking countries, people have two surnames: the father's first surname followed by the mother's first surname. If Ximena's father is Jorge Torres Ruiz and her mother is Elena Galindo Paz, she is **Ximena Torres Galindo**. In everyday life she may go by *Ximena Torres*, but official documents use both. In many countries women traditionally keep their own surnames when they marry, and in Spain parents can now choose which surname goes first. When a form asks for *primer apellido* and *segundo apellido*, it means the first and second surname.

**"Americano".** For many Latin Americans, *América* is the whole continent from Alaska to Patagonia, so everyone from Mexico to Chile is *americano*. To refer to people from the United States, *estadounidense* is the precise word; *norteamericano* and *americano* are also heard, especially in Spain, but some people find them imprecise. Using *estadounidense* is never wrong.

**Spanish across the world.** Spanish is an official language in Spain, eighteen countries of the Americas, and Equatorial Guinea in Africa, and tens of millions of people speak it in the United States. You'll meet speakers of all these places in this course.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"I'm a teacher" is *Soy un profesor*. Not quite: with a bare profession after *ser*, Spanish leaves out the article: **Soy profesor** (a man), **Soy profesora** (a woman).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Nationalities need a capital letter, as in English." In Spanish they don't: *Es inglesa, son mexicanos, el español*. Countries and cities are capitalised; nationalities and languages are not.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Ustedes* is only for formal situations." That is true only in most of Spain. Across Latin America, *ustedes* is the one word for "you all", whether you're speaking to your friends or to a room of officials.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"You always need *yo*, *tú* and so on, as in English." You don't. *Soy de Perú* is complete; *Yo soy de Perú* adds emphasis ("*I*'m from Peru"). Overusing pronouns makes your Spanish sound heavy.

</Misconception>

</Section>
