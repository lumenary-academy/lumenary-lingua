<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="Numbers everywhere">

Numbers are some of the most useful words you will ever learn: you need them for prices, phone numbers, addresses, times, dates and ages, and you need to *hear* them quickly, when someone at a ticket office or a market stall says a price once and waits. This lesson builds them up in order, then puts them to work with the clock, the calendar and the shop counter.

</Section>

<Section id="numbers" kind="grammar" title="Numbers from 0 to 1,000">

### 0 to 30

| | | | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 <Say lang="es">cero</Say> | 1 <Say lang="es">uno</Say> | 2 <Say lang="es">dos</Say> | 3 <Say lang="es">tres</Say> | 4 <Say lang="es">cuatro</Say> | 5 <Say lang="es">cinco</Say> |
| 6 <Say lang="es">seis</Say> | 7 <Say lang="es">siete</Say> | 8 <Say lang="es">ocho</Say> | 9 <Say lang="es">nueve</Say> | 10 <Say lang="es">diez</Say> | 11 <Say lang="es">once</Say> |
| 12 <Say lang="es">doce</Say> | 13 <Say lang="es">trece</Say> | 14 <Say lang="es">catorce</Say> | 15 <Say lang="es">quince</Say> | 16 <Say lang="es">dieciséis</Say> | 17 <Say lang="es">diecisiete</Say> |
| 18 <Say lang="es">dieciocho</Say> | 19 <Say lang="es">diecinueve</Say> | 20 <Say lang="es">veinte</Say> | 21 <Say lang="es">veintiuno</Say> | 22 <Say lang="es">veintidós</Say> | 23 <Say lang="es">veintitrés</Say> |
| 24 <Say lang="es">veinticuatro</Say> | 25 <Say lang="es">veinticinco</Say> | 26 <Say lang="es">veintiséis</Say> | 27 <Say lang="es">veintisiete</Say> | 28 <Say lang="es">veintiocho</Say> | 29 <Say lang="es">veintinueve</Say> |

11 to 15 must simply be learned. From 16 to 29, numbers are one word, built from *diez y…* and *veinte y…* squeezed together: *dieci-séis*, *veinti-dós*. The accents on *dieciséis, veintidós, veintitrés, veintiséis* follow the stress rule from lesson 1: they end in -s and are stressed on the last syllable.

### 30 to 100

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30 <Say lang="es">treinta</Say> | 40 <Say lang="es">cuarenta</Say> | 50 <Say lang="es">cincuenta</Say> | 60 <Say lang="es">sesenta</Say> |
| 70 <Say lang="es">setenta</Say> | 80 <Say lang="es">ochenta</Say> | 90 <Say lang="es">noventa</Say> | 100 <Say lang="es">cien</Say> |

From 31 on, numbers are three words joined by **y** (and): <Say lang="es">treinta y uno</Say> (31), <Say lang="es">cuarenta y cinco</Say> (45), <Say lang="es">noventa y nueve</Say> (99). Careful with 60 and 70: *sesenta* (s) and *setenta* (t).

### Hundreds to a thousand

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 <Say lang="es">cien</Say> | 200 <Say lang="es">doscientos</Say> | 300 <Say lang="es">trescientos</Say> |
| 400 <Say lang="es">cuatrocientos</Say> | 500 <Say lang="es">quinientos</Say> | 600 <Say lang="es">seiscientos</Say> |
| 700 <Say lang="es">setecientos</Say> | 800 <Say lang="es">ochocientos</Say> | 900 <Say lang="es">novecientos</Say> |
| 1,000 <Say lang="es">mil</Say> | | |

Watch the three irregular ones: **quinientos** (500), **setecientos** (700), **novecientos** (900).

- **Cien or ciento?** Exactly 100 is *cien*: *cien pesos*, *cien personas*. From 101 to 199 it becomes *ciento*: <Say lang="es">ciento uno</Say> (101), <Say lang="es">ciento cincuenta</Say> (150).
- **No y after the hundreds.** *Y* only joins tens and units: <Say lang="es">doscientos treinta y cuatro</Say> (234), never *doscientos y treinta*.
- **Mil**, not *un mil*: <Say lang="es">mil euros</Say>. Years are read as ordinary numbers: 2026 is <Say lang="es">dos mil veintiséis</Say>.

### Numbers that agree

Most numbers never change, but two kinds do:

1. **Uno** shortens to **un** before a masculine noun and becomes **una** before a feminine one: <Say lang="es">un euro</Say>, <Say lang="es">una hora</Say>; also <Say lang="es">veintiún años</Say> (21 years), <Say lang="es">treinta y una personas</Say> (31 people). Counting by itself, it stays *uno*.
2. **The hundreds** have a feminine form: <Say lang="es">doscientos pesos</Say> but <Say lang="es">doscientas personas</Say>.

<Section id="age" kind="grammar" title="Age: tener … años">

In Spanish you don't *are* an age, you *have* it. The verb is **tener** (to have). We'll learn all of it in the <LessonLink slug="family-and-descriptions">next lesson</LessonLink>; for now, three forms are enough:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">Tengo diecinueve años.</Say> | I'm nineteen. |
| <Say lang="es">¿Cuántos años tienes?</Say> | How old are you? (*tú*) |
| <Say lang="es">Kofi tiene veintiún años.</Say> | Kofi is twenty-one. |
| <Say lang="es">¿Cuántos años tiene usted?</Say> | How old are you? (*usted*) |

*Tengo* goes with *yo*, *tienes* with *tú*, and *tiene* with *él, ella* and *usted*. Don't leave out *años*: *tengo veinte* on its own is understood in conversation, but the full phrase is *tengo veinte años*.

</Section>

<Section id="time" kind="grammar" title="Telling the time">

To ask the time: <Say lang="es">¿Qué hora es?</Say> The answer uses *ser* and the feminine article, because *hora* (hour) is feminine and understood:

- <Say lang="es">Es la una.</Say> It's one o'clock. (singular: *una hora*)
- <Say lang="es">Son las cuatro.</Say> It's four o'clock. (plural: *las cuatro horas*)

Minutes **past** the hour are added with **y**; minutes **to** the next hour are subtracted with **menos**:

| Time | Spanish |
| --- | --- |
| 4:05 | <Say lang="es">Son las cuatro y cinco.</Say> |
| 4:15 | <Say lang="es">Son las cuatro y cuarto.</Say> |
| 4:30 | <Say lang="es">Son las cuatro y media.</Say> |
| 4:40 | <Say lang="es">Son las cinco menos veinte.</Say> |
| 4:45 | <Say lang="es">Son las cinco menos cuarto.</Say> |
| 1:50 | <Say lang="es">Son las dos menos diez.</Say> |
| 12:55 | <Say lang="es">Es la una menos cinco.</Say> |

Notice the last line: before one o'clock it's still singular, *es la una menos cinco*, because the hour named is one.

In much of Latin America you will also hear the "to" times said with **para**: <Say lang="es">Son veinte para las cinco</Say> or <Say lang="es">Es un cuarto para las cinco</Say> (4:40, 4:45). Both systems are understood everywhere.

To say **at** what time something happens, use **a la** / **a las**: <Say lang="es">La clase es a las nueve.</Say> (The class is at nine.) <Say lang="es">¿A qué hora es el concierto? A las ocho de la noche.</Say>

**The 24-hour clock.** Timetables, tickets and official announcements use it, and it is read as plain numbers: 18:30 is <Say lang="es">las dieciocho treinta</Say>. In conversation, people usually say <Say lang="es">las seis y media de la tarde</Say>.

</Section>

<Section id="calendar" kind="grammar" title="Days, months and dates">

| Days | | Months | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">lunes</Say> Monday | <Say lang="es">viernes</Say> Friday | <Say lang="es">enero</Say> | <Say lang="es">mayo</Say> | <Say lang="es">septiembre</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">martes</Say> Tuesday | <Say lang="es">sábado</Say> Saturday | <Say lang="es">febrero</Say> | <Say lang="es">junio</Say> | <Say lang="es">octubre</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">miércoles</Say> Wednesday | <Say lang="es">domingo</Say> Sunday | <Say lang="es">marzo</Say> | <Say lang="es">julio</Say> | <Say lang="es">noviembre</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">jueves</Say> Thursday | | <Say lang="es">abril</Say> | <Say lang="es">agosto</Say> | <Say lang="es">diciembre</Say> |

- **Small letters.** Days and months are written in lower case, unlike English.
- **The week starts on Monday** on Spanish calendars.
- **Days are masculine and need no preposition.** "On Saturday" is <Say lang="es">el sábado</Say>; "on Saturdays" is <Say lang="es">los sábados</Say>: <Say lang="es">El concierto es el sábado.</Say> Days ending in -s don't change in the plural: *el lunes, los lunes*.
- *Septiembre* is also spelled *setiembre*, especially in some Latin American countries.

**Dates** are said as *el* + number + *de* + month (+ *de* + year): <Say lang="es">el cinco de mayo</Say> (May 5th), <Say lang="es">el doce de octubre de dos mil veintiséis</Say>. Spanish uses ordinary numbers, not "fifth" or "twelfth". The one exception is the first of the month, often <Say lang="es">el primero de mayo</Say> in Latin America and <Say lang="es">el uno de mayo</Say> in Spain; both are correct.

To say today's date: <Say lang="es">Hoy es martes, tres de marzo.</Say> Written, the day always comes first: 3/3/2026, and 5/4 is the 5th of April, not May 4th.

</Section>

<Section id="prices" kind="grammar" title="How much is it?">

Use **cuesta** for one thing and **cuestan** for several:

- <Say lang="es">¿Cuánto cuesta el libro?</Say> <Say lang="es">Cuesta quince euros.</Say>
- <Say lang="es">¿Cuánto cuestan las naranjas?</Say> <Say lang="es">Cuestan treinta pesos el kilo.</Say> (30 pesos a kilo; note *el kilo*, "the kilo", where English says "a kilo".)
- At the till, for the total: <Say lang="es">¿Cuánto es?</Say> <Say lang="es">Son veinte euros.</Say>

**Euros and cents.** Spain and most of Europe write a decimal comma: 3,50 € is three euros fifty. Say <Say lang="es">tres euros con cincuenta</Say> or simply <Say lang="es">tres cincuenta</Say>.

**Pesos.** Mexico, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Uruguay, Cuba and the Dominican Republic all use pesos, each country its own, and they are usually written with the same sign as the dollar: \$40 on a Mexican market stall means *cuarenta pesos*. Mexico writes a decimal point, as in English: \$12.50 is <Say lang="es">doce pesos con cincuenta centavos</Say>. Other countries have other currencies: the *sol* in Peru, the *quetzal* in Guatemala, and the US dollar in Ecuador, for example.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="At the market in Oaxaca">

Mei, from Singapore, is spending a month in Oaxaca, in southern Mexico. On Saturday morning she goes to the market with her friend Diego.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mei | <Say lang="es">Buenos días, señora. ¿Cuánto cuestan los mangos?</Say> | Good morning. How much are the mangoes? |
| Doña Rosa | <Say lang="es">Buenos días. Cuarenta pesos el kilo.</Say> | Good morning. Forty pesos a kilo. |
| Mei | <Say lang="es">¿Y las naranjas?</Say> | And the oranges? |
| Doña Rosa | <Say lang="es">Treinta. Son muy dulces.</Say> | Thirty. They're very sweet. |
| Mei | <Say lang="es">Un kilo de mangos y un kilo de naranjas, por favor. ¿Cuánto es?</Say> | A kilo of mangoes and a kilo of oranges, please. How much is it? |
| Doña Rosa | <Say lang="es">Son setenta pesos.</Say> | That's seventy pesos. |
| Mei | <Say lang="es">Aquí tiene cien.</Say> | Here's a hundred. |
| Doña Rosa | <Say lang="es">Y treinta de cambio. Gracias, señorita.</Say> | And thirty change. Thank you, miss. |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">Oye, Mei, ¿qué hora es?</Say> | Hey, Mei, what time is it? |
| Mei | <Say lang="es">Son las once menos cuarto. ¿A qué hora es el concierto?</Say> | It's a quarter to eleven. What time is the concert? |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">A las doce, en la plaza. Es gratis.</Say> | At twelve, in the square. It's free. |
| Mei | <Say lang="es">¡Qué bien! Diego, ¿cuándo es tu cumpleaños?</Say> | Great! Diego, when's your birthday? |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">Es el doce de octubre. Tengo veintiún años. ¿Y tú?</Say> | It's on the twelfth of October. I'm twenty-one. And you? |
| Mei | <Say lang="es">Mi cumpleaños es el primero de mayo. Tengo veinticuatro años.</Say> | My birthday is on the first of May. I'm twenty-four. |

**Notes.** *Doña* is a respectful title used before a woman's first name, especially for older women; the masculine is *don*. *Oye* ("hey", literally "listen") is a friendly way to get someone's attention. Mei says *el primero de mayo*, as people in Mexico do; in Spain you would more often hear *el uno de mayo*. *Dulces* (sweet) and *gratis* (free of charge) are new words; *dulces* is plural to agree with *naranjas*.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Saying numbers clearly">

- **c and z in numbers.** *Cinco, once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, cien, doscientos, diez* all have the *c/z* sound from lesson 1: *th* in most of Spain, *s* in Latin America. Listen to <Say lang="es-ES">doce</Say> and <Say lang="es-MX">doce</Say>.
- **qu is k.** <Say lang="es">quince</Say> is "KEEN-seh" and <Say lang="es">quinientos</Say> is "kee-NYEN-tos".
- **Glide the vowels.** *seis* (one syllable, "SAYS"), *veinte* ("BAYN-teh"), *nueve* ("NWEH-beh"), *diez* ("DYES").
- **Link the y.** In *treinta y uno*, the three words run together: "TRAYN-tai-OO-no". Practise with <Say lang="es">cuarenta y ocho</Say> and <Say lang="es">sesenta y siete</Say>.
- **Seis or siete?** *Seis* (6) is one syllable; *siete* (7) is two, "SYEH-teh". In a noisy market, people often repeat prices to make sure.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="A different rhythm to the day">

In Spain the day runs later than in most of Europe and North America. Lunch, the main meal for many people, is often eaten between 2 and 3 p.m., and dinner around 9 or even 10 p.m. That stretches *la tarde*: in Spain you may hear *buenas tardes* at 7 p.m. while it is still light. One reason often mentioned is that Spain has used Central European Time since 1940, although much of the country lies as far west as Britain; but working hours and customs matter too. In Mexico, Colombia or Argentina, mealtimes differ again, and *la comida* (lunch) is also often eaten in the early afternoon.

Two practical tips. First, times on tickets and timetables use the 24-hour clock, so if your bus leaves *a las 19:15*, that's a quarter past seven in the evening. Second, dates are always day first: if a form asks for your *fecha de nacimiento* (date of birth), write the day, then the month, then the year.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"I'm twenty" is *Soy veinte* or *Estoy veinte*. Not in Spanish: age is something you *have*. Say **Tengo veinte años**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Ciento* means one hundred." Only in compounds (*ciento uno*, *ciento veinte*). On its own, and before a noun, 100 is **cien**: *cien euros*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"It's one o'clock" is *Son la una*. One o'clock is singular: **Es la una**. All the other hours are plural: *son las dos, son las doce*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"On Monday" needs a word for "on", as in English. Spanish uses only the article: **el lunes** (on Monday), **los lunes** (on Mondays). Never *en lunes*.

</Misconception>

</Section>
