<Section id="vocabulary" kind="vocabulary" title="Words for opinions and debate">

At B1 you can start to discuss things, not only describe them. That takes two skills: building longer sentences that hold several ideas together, which is what **relative clauses** do, and a set of phrases for **giving opinions, agreeing and disagreeing**, ideally without offending anyone. This lesson gives you both, and ends with a neighbourhood meeting.

<Section id="relatives" kind="grammar" title="Relative clauses">

A relative clause describes a noun with a whole clause: *the neighbour **who lives upstairs***, *the book **that you lent me***. English can often leave out *that*; Spanish never leaves out its relative.

### Que: the all-purpose relative

**Que** works for people and things, as subject or object. It is by far the most common.

- <Say lang="es">La vecina que vive arriba es médica.</Say> — The neighbour who lives upstairs is a doctor.
- <Say lang="es">El libro que me prestaste es buenísimo.</Say> — The book (that) you lent me is great.

### Quien, quienes: people, especially after prepositions

**Quien** (plural **quienes**) refers only to people. Use it after a preposition, or between commas:

- <Say lang="es">La amiga con quien viajé vive en Quito.</Say> — The friend I travelled with lives in Quito.
- *Mi tía, quien vive en Lima, viene en julio.* (Here *que* is more common in speech: *Mi tía, que vive en Lima…*)

Don't use *quien* as the subject of a clause without commas: *la mujer **que** vive aquí*, not *la mujer quien vive aquí*.

### El que, la que, los que, las que

After a preposition, Spanish very often uses an article + *que*, agreeing with the noun it refers to:

- <Say lang="es">La empresa para la que trabajo es pequeña.</Say> — The company I work for is small.
- <Say lang="es">El parque en el que jugábamos ya no existe.</Say> — The park we used to play in no longer exists.
- <Say lang="es">Los amigos con los que salgo son del barrio.</Say> — The friends I go out with are from the neighbourhood.

Without a noun before it, *el que* means "the one who", "those who": <Say lang="es">Los que quieran hablar, que levanten la mano.</Say> (Those who want to speak, raise your hand.)

Notice where the preposition goes: always **before** the relative. English can leave it at the end (*the company I work for*); Spanish can't.

### Lo que: "what", "which"

**Lo que** refers to an idea rather than a noun. It translates English *what* (= the thing that) and *which* referring to a whole sentence:

- <Say lang="es">Lo que más me preocupa es el ruido.</Say> — What worries me most is the noise.
- <Say lang="es">No entiendo lo que dices.</Say> — I don't understand what you're saying.
- <Say lang="es">Llegó tarde, lo que molestó a todos.</Say> — He arrived late, which annoyed everyone.

### Donde: places

**Donde** (no accent) introduces a clause about a place. It can be replaced by *en el que, en la que*:

- <Say lang="es">El barrio donde crecí ha cambiado mucho.</Say> — The neighbourhood where I grew up has changed a lot.

### Cuyo: "whose"

**Cuyo, cuya, cuyos, cuyas** means "whose". It agrees with the **thing possessed**, not with the owner:

- <Say lang="es">La escritora cuyos libros leí vive en Montevideo.</Say> — *cuyos* agrees with *libros*.
- <Say lang="es">Tengo un vecino cuya perra ladra toda la noche.</Say> — *cuya* agrees with *perra*.

*Cuyo* is more common in writing than in conversation, but it appears in the most famous sentence in Spanish literature, the opening of *Don Quijote*: *En un lugar de la Mancha, **de cuyo nombre** no quiero acordarme…* (whose name I do not care to recall).

### Indicative or subjunctive in a relative clause?

If the noun refers to something **known to exist**, the relative clause takes the indicative. If it's something you are looking for, or that may not exist, it takes the **subjunctive**:

- <Say lang="es">Busco el piso que tiene terraza.</Say> — I'm looking for the flat that has a terrace (a particular one I know).
- <Say lang="es">Busco un piso que tenga terraza.</Say> — I'm looking for a flat with a terrace (any one, if there is one).
- <Say lang="es">No conozco a nadie que hable japonés.</Say> — I don't know anyone who speaks Japanese.

<Section id="opinions" kind="grammar" title="Giving opinions, agreeing and disagreeing">

### Giving an opinion

| Expression | Mood after it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Creo que / Pienso que / Me parece que* | indicative | *Creo que es una buena idea.* |
| *No creo que / No me parece que* | subjunctive | *No creo que sea buena idea.* |
| *En mi opinión, / Para mí, / Desde mi punto de vista,* | (a full sentence) | *Para mí, el problema es el tráfico.* |
| *Me parece bien / mal / injusto que* | subjunctive | *Me parece injusto que no podamos votar.* |

The last line is subtle. *Me parece que* + indicative gives an opinion about what is true; *me parece bien que* + subjunctive judges something, like the emotional expressions in <LessonLink slug="feelings-and-doubt">Feelings and doubt</LessonLink>.

### Agreeing, disagreeing and conceding

| To… | Say |
| --- | --- |
| Agree fully | <Say lang="es">Estoy totalmente de acuerdo.</Say> <Say lang="es">Tienes toda la razón.</Say> |
| Agree in part | <Say lang="es">Estoy de acuerdo hasta cierto punto.</Say> <Say lang="es">No estoy del todo de acuerdo.</Say> |
| Disagree politely | <Say lang="es">Entiendo tu punto de vista, pero…</Say> <Say lang="es">Yo no lo veo así.</Say> <Say lang="es">Yo no diría eso.</Say> |
| Concede a point | <Say lang="es">En eso tienes razón, pero…</Say> <Say lang="es">Es verdad que…, pero…</Say> |
| Ask for an opinion | <Say lang="es">¿Tú qué opinas?</Say> <Say lang="es">¿Qué te parece?</Say> |
| Take the floor | <Say lang="es">Si me permites…</Say> <Say lang="es">Perdona que te interrumpa, pero…</Say> |

The pattern "concede, then disagree" (*Es verdad que…, pero…*) is the backbone of a polite argument in Spanish, as in English.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="A neighbourhood meeting">

**In a neighbourhood of Arequipa, Peru.** The local council has proposed closing calle Las Flores to cars at weekends. Elena chairs the residents' meeting; don Rafael owns a hardware shop on the street; Valeria is a student; Óscar is a father of two. In a meeting like this they use *usted* with each other. Notice the Peruvian words: *carro* for car, *colegio* for school, *estacionar* for to park.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elena | <Say lang="es">Buenas tardes a todos. Hoy hablamos de la propuesta de la municipalidad, que quiere cerrar la calle Las Flores a los carros los fines de semana. ¿Quién empieza?</Say> | Good afternoon, everyone. Today we're discussing the council's proposal, which wants to close calle Las Flores to cars at weekends. Who'll start? |
| Don Rafael | <Say lang="es">Yo, si me permiten. Tengo una ferretería en esa calle desde hace treinta años. Lo que me preocupa es que mis clientes no tengan dónde estacionar. Muchos de los que compran en mi tienda vienen de lejos.</Say> | Me, if I may. I've had a hardware shop on that street for thirty years. What worries me is that my customers won't have anywhere to park. Many of the people who shop in my store come from far away. |
| Valeria | <Say lang="es">Entiendo su punto de vista, don Rafael, pero no estoy de acuerdo. En las ciudades donde han cerrado calles al tráfico, muchas tiendas venden más. La gente camina tranquila y entra a mirar.</Say> | I understand your point of view, don Rafael, but I don't agree. In cities where they've closed streets to traffic, many shops sell more. People walk calmly and go in to look around. |
| Don Rafael | <Say lang="es">Eso es lo que dicen, pero no creo que sea igual en todas partes.</Say> | That's what they say, but I don't think it's the same everywhere. |
| Óscar | <Say lang="es">Para mí, lo más importante es la seguridad. Mis hijos van a un colegio que está justo en esa calle, y todas las mañanas veo carros que pasan a toda velocidad.</Say> | For me, safety is the most important thing. My children go to a school that's right on that street, and every morning I see cars going past at full speed. |
| Elena | <Say lang="es">Pero la propuesta es solo para los fines de semana, ¿no?</Say> | But the proposal is only for weekends, isn't it? |
| Óscar | <Say lang="es">Sí, y por eso me parece un buen comienzo. Es verdad que no lo resuelve todo, pero es un paso.</Say> | Yes, and that's why it seems a good start to me. It's true that it doesn't solve everything, but it's a step. |
| Don Rafael | <Say lang="es">En eso tiene razón. Yo propondría una prueba de tres meses. Si las ventas bajan, se vuelve a abrir la calle.</Say> | You're right about that. I'd propose a three-month trial. If sales go down, the street is reopened. |
| Valeria | <Say lang="es">Me parece justo. Y hace falta que haya un estacionamiento cerca para la gente que viene de lejos.</Say> | That seems fair. And there needs to be a car park nearby for people who come from far away. |
| Óscar | <Say lang="es">Una cosa más: lo que no queremos es que los carros pasen por las calles de al lado, donde también viven familias.</Say> | One more thing: what we don't want is for cars to go through the side streets, where families live too. |
| Elena | <Say lang="es">Muy bien, lo anoto. Entonces, ¿estamos de acuerdo en pedir una prueba de tres meses, con un estacionamiento cerca y control en las calles de al lado?</Say> | Very good, I'll note that down. So, do we agree to ask for a three-month trial, with a car park nearby and traffic control in the side streets? |
| Todos | <Say lang="es">De acuerdo.</Say> | Agreed. |

Find the relatives: *la municipalidad, que quiere*; *lo que me preocupa*; *los que compran*; *las ciudades donde*; *lo que dicen*; *un colegio que está*; *carros que pasan*; *la gente que viene*; *lo que no queremos*; *las calles de al lado, donde*. Then the debating moves: Valeria disagrees politely (*Entiendo su punto de vista, pero…*), Óscar concedes and argues (*Es verdad que…, pero…*), and don Rafael concedes a point and proposes a compromise (*En eso tiene razón. Yo propondría…*).

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Que or qué? Stress you can hear">

The relatives *que, quien, donde, cuando, como* are **unstressed**: they lean on the next word and are said quickly. The question and exclamation words *qué, quién, dónde, cuándo, cómo* are **stressed**, and that stress is why they carry a written accent. You can hear the difference:

| Unstressed (relative) | Stressed (question word) |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">Lo que dices es verdad.</Say> | <Say lang="es">No sé qué dices.</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">El barrio donde vivo</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Dónde vives?</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">La chica con quien hablé</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Con quién hablaste?</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">Cuando llego, ceno.</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Cuándo llegas?</Say> |

The accent stays in indirect questions, because the word is still a question word and still stressed: *No sé **dónde** vive*, *Me preguntó **qué** quería*. Read each pair aloud and let the relative almost disappear into the next word, while the question word stands out.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Talking things through">

Spanish-speaking countries have long traditions of public discussion. The **tertulia**, a regular gathering of friends to talk about politics, literature or anything else, was a fixture of cafés in Spain and Latin America in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and many radio and television programmes still bring together *tertulianos* to debate the news. Further back, the **cabildo abierto**, an open meeting of a town's residents, played a part in the independence movements of several Latin American countries; the one held in Buenos Aires in May 1810 is the best known.

In everyday conversation, norms vary by country, region and situation. In many informal settings, and in Spain especially, people often overlap, finish each other's sentences and speak with energy, and this is usually a sign of interest rather than rudeness. In more formal settings, and in many Latin American contexts, softening phrases (*si me permite, con todo respeto, perdone que le interrumpa*) are valued. Listen first, and when in doubt, concede before you disagree.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mistakes">

<Misconception>

**"El libro me prestaste."** Spanish never drops the relative: ***El libro que** me prestaste.*

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"La empresa que trabajo para."** The preposition goes before the relative: ***La empresa para la que** trabajo* (or *para la cual*).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Soy de acuerdo" / "Estás correcto".** Agreement uses *estar*: ***Estoy de acuerdo***. Being right is *tener razón*: ***Tienes razón***.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Cuyo agrees with the owner."** It agrees with what is owned: *el vecino **cuya casa**…*, *la vecina **cuyo perro**…*. Avoid replacing it with *que su* (*el vecino que su casa…*), which is common in speech but not considered standard.

</Misconception>
