<Section id="vocabulary" kind="vocabulary" title="Words for wishes and advice">

Until now, almost every Spanish verb you have used has stated something: *vivo en Lima, llovía, el tren había salido*. This lesson opens a door to a new set of forms, the **subjunctive**, which Spanish uses when you are not stating a fact but wanting it, hoping for it or recommending it. It is the most famous feature of Spanish grammar, and it is far friendlier than its reputation.

<Section id="forming-the-subjunctive" kind="grammar" title="Forming the present subjunctive">

### Two moods

Spanish verbs come in **moods**. The **indicative**, which you know, presents something as information: *Ana viene mañana*. The **subjunctive** is used mainly after *que*, when the first part of the sentence does something other than assert: it wants, hopes, recommends, feels or doubts. Compare:

- <Say lang="es">Sé que Ana viene mañana.</Say> — I know Ana is coming tomorrow. (information → indicative)
- <Say lang="es">Quiero que Ana venga mañana.</Say> — I want Ana to come tomorrow. (a wish → subjunctive)

The good news is that you already know some subjunctive forms: the *usted* commands from <LessonLink slug="body-health-and-commands">Body, health and commands</LessonLink> (*hable, coma, venga, vaya*) are borrowed from it.

### Regular verbs: the "opposite vowel"

Take the **yo** form of the present indicative, drop the **-o**, and add the endings of the *other* conjugation: *-ar* verbs take endings with **e**, *-er* and *-ir* verbs take endings with **a**.

| | hablar → hablo | comer → como | vivir → vivo |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | hable | coma | viva |
| tú | hables | comas | vivas |
| él, ella, usted | hable | coma | viva |
| nosotros, nosotras | hablemos | comamos | vivamos |
| vosotros, vosotras | habléis | comáis | viváis |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | hablen | coman | vivan |

The *yo* and *él/ella/usted* forms are identical; context or a subject pronoun tells them apart.

Because you start from the *yo* form, its irregularities come along for free:

| Infinitive | yo (present) | Subjunctive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tener | tengo | tenga, tengas, tengamos… |
| hacer | hago | haga, hagas, hagamos… |
| decir | digo | diga, digas, digamos… |
| poner | pongo | ponga… |
| salir | salgo | salga… |
| venir | vengo | venga… |
| traer | traigo | traiga… |
| conocer | conozco | conozca… |
| ver | veo | vea… |

Some verbs change their spelling to keep the same sound before *e*: *buscar → busque*, *llegar → llegue*, *empezar → empiece*, *escoger → escoja*, *seguir → siga*.

### Stem-changing verbs

The *-ar* and *-er* stem-changers you met in <LessonLink slug="daily-routine">Daily routine</LessonLink> keep the same "boot" pattern: the stem changes everywhere except *nosotros* and *vosotros*.

- *pensar*: piense, pienses, piense, **pensemos**, **penséis**, piensen
- *volver*: vuelva, vuelvas, vuelva, **volvamos**, **volváis**, vuelvan

The *-ir* stem-changers have one extra twist: in *nosotros* and *vosotros* they change too, *e → i* and *o → u*.

- *pedir*: pida, pidas, pida, **pidamos**, **pidáis**, pidan
- *sentir*: sienta, sientas, sienta, **sintamos**, **sintáis**, sientan
- *dormir*: duerma, duermas, duerma, **durmamos**, **durmáis**, duerman

### The six true irregulars

Six verbs don't follow the *yo* rule. Some learners remember them as **DISHES**: *dar, ir, ser, haber, estar, saber*.

| | dar | ir | ser | haber | estar | saber |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | dé | vaya | sea | haya | esté | sepa |
| tú | des | vayas | seas | hayas | estés | sepas |
| él, ella, usted | dé | vaya | sea | haya | esté | sepa |
| nosotros | demos | vayamos | seamos | hayamos | estemos | sepamos |
| vosotros | deis | vayáis | seáis | hayáis | estéis | sepáis |
| ellos, ustedes | den | vayan | sean | hayan | estén | sepan |

Watch the accents: **dé** has one to tell it apart from the preposition *de*, and every form of *estar* except *estemos* is stressed on its last syllable (*esté, estés, estén*).

</Section>

<Section id="wishes-and-influence" kind="grammar" title="Wanting, hoping and recommending">

The pattern for this lesson has three parts:

| Main clause: a wish or influence | que | A different subject + subjunctive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quiero | que | vengas a mi fiesta. |
| Mis padres esperan | que | encuentre trabajo pronto. |
| Te recomiendo | que | pruebes el ceviche. |
| Es importante | que | durmáis bien antes del examen. |

The verbs and expressions that trigger it are those of the vocabulary list: *querer, desear, preferir, esperar, pedir, recomendar, aconsejar, sugerir, necesitar, insistir en, permitir, prohibir*, and impersonal expressions such as *es importante, es necesario, es mejor, es fundamental, hace falta, conviene*.

- <Say lang="es">Mi madre quiere que la llame todos los domingos.</Say> — My mother wants me to call her every Sunday.
- <Say lang="es">Prefiero que vayamos en tren.</Say> — I'd rather we went by train.
- <Say lang="es">Te pido que no se lo digas a nadie.</Say> — I'm asking you not to tell anyone.
- <Say lang="es">El médico me aconseja que camine media hora al día.</Say> — The doctor advises me to walk half an hour a day.
- <Say lang="es">Hace falta que alguien traiga hielo.</Say> — Someone needs to bring ice.
- <Say lang="es">No es necesario que vengas temprano.</Say> — You don't need to come early.

### Same subject? Use the infinitive

The subjunctive appears only when **someone else** is involved. If the person wishing and the person doing are the same, Spanish uses the infinitive, with no *que*:

| Same subject: infinitive | Different subject: que + subjunctive |
| --- | --- |
| *Quiero viajar.* I want to travel. | *Quiero que viajes.* I want you to travel. |
| *Espero aprobar.* I hope to pass. | *Espero que apruebes.* I hope you pass. |
| *Es importante dormir bien.* (in general) | *Es importante que duermas bien.* (you, specifically) |

With *recomendar, aconsejar, pedir, permitir* and *prohibir* you can also use an indirect object and an infinitive: *Te recomiendo descansar* means the same as *Te recomiendo que descanses*.

### One verb, two meanings: decir que

*Decir que* reports information with the indicative, but gives an instruction with the subjunctive:

- <Say lang="es">Marta dice que llega tarde.</Say> — Marta says she's arriving late. (information)
- <Say lang="es">Marta dice que llegues pronto.</Say> — Marta says you should arrive early. (an instruction)

### Wishing people well

A *que* clause on its own expresses a wish, with *I hope* understood: <Say lang="es">¡Que te mejores!</Say>, <Say lang="es">¡Que tengas suerte!</Say>, <Say lang="es">¡Que descanses!</Say>, <Say lang="es">¡Que te vaya bien en la entrevista!</Say> You will hear these every day.

### Giving advice at the right strength

| Strength | Expression | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gentle | *¿Por qué no…?* + present | <Say lang="es">¿Por qué no hablas con ella?</Say> |
| Gentle | *Te recomiendo / te aconsejo que…* | <Say lang="es">Te aconsejo que duermas un poco.</Say> |
| Neutral | *Es mejor que…* | <Say lang="es">Es mejor que lo dejes para mañana.</Say> |
| Neutral | *Deberías* + infinitive | <Say lang="es">Deberías descansar.</Say> |
| Firm | *Tienes que* + infinitive | <Say lang="es">Tienes que ir al médico.</Say> |
| Firm | A command | <Say lang="es">¡Ve al médico ya!</Say> |

*Deberías* is the conditional, which you will study properly in <LessonLink slug="work-and-hypotheses">Work and hypotheses</LessonLink>; for now treat it as a fixed phrase meaning "you should".

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="Advice before a big move">

**In Guadalajara, Mexico.** Sofía has accepted a job in Madrid and asks her aunt Teresa, who lived in Spain for years, for advice. They use *tú*.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Tía, ¿tienes un momento? Necesito que me des unos consejos.</Say> | Auntie, have you got a moment? I need you to give me some advice. |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">Claro, mi niña. ¿Qué pasa?</Say> | Of course, sweetheart. What's up? |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Ya sabes que en enero me voy a Madrid. Estoy feliz, pero también un poco nerviosa.</Say> | You know I'm off to Madrid in January. I'm happy, but a bit nervous too. |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">Es normal. ¿Ya tienes dónde vivir?</Say> | That's normal. Have you got somewhere to live yet? |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Todavía no. Mi jefa me recomienda que busque algo cerca de la oficina, pero allí todo es carísimo.</Say> | Not yet. My boss recommends I look for something near the office, but everything there is really expensive. |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">Entonces te aconsejo que compartas departamento… bueno, allá dicen piso… con otras personas. Así ahorras y conoces gente.</Say> | Then I'd advise you to share an apartment… well, over there they say *piso*… with other people. That way you save money and meet people. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Buena idea. ¿Qué más?</Say> | Good idea. What else? |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">Es importante que tengas todos tus papeles en orden antes de salir. No dejes nada para el último día.</Say> | It's important that you have all your papers in order before you leave. Don't leave anything for the last day. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Sí, ya lo sé. Y mi mamá quiere que la llame todos los días.</Say> | Yes, I know. And my mum wants me to call her every day. |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">¡Todos los días! Bueno, es mejor que la llames seguido al principio. Ya sabes cómo es tu mamá.</Say> | Every day! Well, it's best to call her often at first. You know what your mum is like. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">¿Y qué me recomiendas para no sentirme sola?</Say> | And what do you recommend so I don't feel lonely? |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">Que salgas, que camines mucho y que digas que sí a todas las invitaciones, por lo menos el primer mes. Y no hace falta que hables como los madrileños, ¿eh? Habla como tú.</Say> | Go out, walk a lot and say yes to every invitation, at least for the first month. And you don't need to talk like people from Madrid, OK? Talk like yourself. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">¡Ja! Lo intentaré. Espero que vengas a visitarme.</Say> | Ha! I'll try. I hope you come and visit me. |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">Claro que sí. Quiero que me enseñes tu barrio y que vayamos juntas al Prado.</Say> | Of course. I want you to show me your neighbourhood and for us to go to the Prado together. |
| Sofía | <Say lang="es">Hecho. Gracias, tía.</Say> | Deal. Thanks, Auntie. |
| Teresa | <Say lang="es">De nada, mi amor. Y que te vaya muy bien.</Say> | You're welcome, love. And all the very best. |

Every subjunctive in the dialogue follows a wish or a piece of advice: *necesito que me des, me recomienda que busque, te aconsejo que compartas, es importante que tengas, quiere que la llame, no hace falta que hables, espero que vengas, quiero que me enseñes y que vayamos, que te vaya bien*. Teresa's answer *Que salgas, que camines…* leaves *te recomiendo* understood.

<Note>

Two touches of Mexican Spanish: *mamá* is the everyday word for "mum" (in Spain *mamá* is used too, alongside *madre* and *mi madre*), and *seguido* is commonly used in Mexico to mean "often" (*la llamo seguido*). In Spain you would hear *a menudo*.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Same sound, new spelling">

Spanish spelling is designed so that each verb keeps its **sound** when the endings change. That is why some subjunctives look odd on paper but sound perfectly regular:

| Infinitive | Subjunctive | What the spelling protects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">buscar</Say> | <Say lang="es">busque</Say> | The hard /k/ sound: *ce* would sound like /se/ or /θe/ |
| <Say lang="es">llegar</Say> | <Say lang="es">llegue</Say> | The hard /g/: the *u* is silent, it's there to keep *g* hard |
| <Say lang="es">empezar</Say> | <Say lang="es">empiece</Say> | Spanish writes *ce*, not *ze* |
| <Say lang="es">escoger</Say> | <Say lang="es">escoja</Say> | The throaty /x/ of *ge*, which *ga* would lose |

In *empiece*, most of Spain pronounces the *c* like English *th* in *think*, while Latin America, the Canary Islands and parts of southern Spain pronounce it as /s/. Both are standard; you will learn more in <LessonLink slug="regional-spanish">Spanish around the world</LessonLink>.

Accents matter too. Say <Say lang="es">dé</Say> (give) with a full stress, unlike the unstressed preposition *de*. Stress the last syllable of <Say lang="es">esté</Say> and <Say lang="es">estén</Say>; *este* without the accent is "this".

A curious trio for most speakers: <Say lang="es">vaya</Say> (subjunctive of *ir*), *valla* (fence) and *baya* (berry) sound exactly the same, because *b* and *v* are pronounced alike and so, for the great majority of speakers, are *y* and *ll*. Only spelling and context tell them apart.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Wishes as everyday courtesy">

Spanish is full of small wishes built on *que* + subjunctive, and using them is one of the easiest ways to sound warm and natural. When someone is about to eat, you say *¡Que aproveche!* (very common in Spain) or *¡Buen provecho!* (common everywhere). When someone leaves for an exam, a trip or an interview: *¡Que te vaya bien!* or simply *¡Suerte!* At night: *¡Que descanses!* When someone is ill: *¡Que te mejores!*

Many everyday expressions also carry a religious heritage. *Si Dios quiere* ("God willing") and *Dios mediante* are heard across the Spanish-speaking world when people talk about plans, from deeply devout speakers and from people who use them simply as set phrases. *Que Dios te bendiga* ("God bless you") is a common farewell from grandparents in many countries. You don't need to use them yourself, but you will understand them when you hear them.

Finally, a note on directness. Among friends and family in many Spanish-speaking places, advice is often given plainly, with a command or *tienes que*: *¡Ve al médico!* or *Tienes que descansar*. That usually sounds caring, not bossy. The very cautious English style (*you might perhaps want to consider…*) can sound distant when translated word for word. Norms vary by country and situation, so listen to how people around you do it.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mistakes">

<Misconception>

**"I want you to come" = *Quiero tú venir*.** English uses an object and an infinitive; Spanish uses *que* and a full verb in the subjunctive: **Quiero que vengas.** The *que* can never be left out.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Quiero que yo vaya."** When the subject is the same, use the infinitive: **Quiero ir.** The subjunctive appears only when one person wants something of another.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The subjunctive means maybe."** Uncertainty is only one of its uses. In this lesson it marks influence: *Te recomiendo que lo pruebes* is not uncertain at all. Think of the subjunctive as "not asserted as information", rather than "unsure".

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Espero que dé" and "espero que de" are the same.** The accent on **dé** (from *dar*) is not optional: without it, the word is the preposition *of, from*.

</Misconception>

</Section>
