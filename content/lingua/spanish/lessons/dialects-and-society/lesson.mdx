<Section id="us-spanish" kind="culture" title="Spanish and English in the United States">

The United States has one of the largest Spanish-speaking populations in the world, made up of speakers of Mexican, Puerto Rican, Cuban, Salvadoran, Dominican and many other varieties, and of their children and grandchildren. Three phenomena define it.

**Heritage speakers.** Many US Latinos grew up speaking Spanish at home and studying in English. They typically have native pronunciation and comprehension, and sometimes less practice with formal registers and writing. Universities now offer "Spanish for heritage speakers" courses that build on what they know rather than treating them as beginners.

**Code-switching.** Moving between languages within a conversation — *Le dije a mi mamá que I'd be home late, pero ya sabes cómo es* — is not a sign of knowing neither language well. Linguists have shown that fluent bilinguals switch at points where the two grammars fit together, and that doing it smoothly requires command of both. Shana Poplack's classic 1980 study made the point in its own title: *Sometimes I'll start a sentence in Spanish y termino en español*.

**Loans and calques.** US Spanish borrows freely, adapting English words to Spanish sound and grammar:

| US Spanish | General Spanish | From |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *la troca* | *la camioneta* | truck |
| *parquear* | *aparcar*, *estacionar* | to park (also Caribbean) |
| *el lonche* | *el almuerzo* | lunch (also Mexico) |
| *la marqueta* | *el mercado* | market |
| *la aseguranza* | *el seguro* | insurance |
| *llamar para atrás* | *devolver la llamada* | to call back |

The traffic runs both ways: English owes Spanish *canyon*, *rodeo*, *patio*, *plaza*, *ranch*, *lasso*, *mosquito*, *alligator* (*el lagarto*), and, via Spanish from Taíno, *hurricane* and *barbecue*. Since 1973 the US has had its own academy, the Academia Norteamericana de la Lengua Española, a member of ASALE.

<Section id="contact" kind="culture" title="Spanish in contact: Spain and Latin America">

### Catalan, Basque and Galician

Spain's 1978 Constitution makes Castilian Spanish the official language of the state and allows other languages to be co-official in their regions: **Catalan** in Catalonia and the Balearic Islands, and as **Valencian** in the Valencian Community; **Galician** in Galicia; **Basque** (*euskera*), a language unrelated to any other, in the Basque Country and part of Navarre; and **Aranese**, a variety of Occitan, in Catalonia's Val d'Aran. Millions of Spaniards are bilingual, and the Spanish spoken in these regions shows it.

| Region | Feature of local Spanish | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Catalonia | final *-d* pronounced [t]; a "dark" *l*; calques from Catalan | *Madrit*; *hacer tarde* (to be late); *plegar* (to finish work) |
| Basque Country and neighbouring areas | conditional in *si*-clauses | *Si tendría tiempo, iría* (standard: *si tuviera*) |
| Galicia (and Asturias, León) | simple past for recent events | *Hoy comí con mi madre* (central Spain: *he comido*) |
| Galicia | the diminutive *-iño* | *un cafeíño* |

Spanish has also borrowed from them: from Catalan, *paella*, *capicúa* (a palindromic number, from *cap i cua*, "head and tail") and *butifarra*; from Basque, *chabola* (shack), *aquelarre* (witches' sabbath) and *órdago* (an all-in bet, from *hor dago*, "there it is"); from Galician, *morriña* (homesick longing) and the *grelos* and *cachelos* of Galician cooking.

### Guaraní in Paraguay

Paraguay's 1992 Constitution makes **Guaraní** and Spanish its official languages. Guaraní is unusual among the indigenous languages of the Americas: it is spoken by most of the population, including people of no indigenous identity, and many Paraguayans move between the two languages all day. Their everyday mix has a name, **jopara** ("mixture"). Paraguayan Spanish is full of Guaraní words — the cold *mate* called *tereré*, the cheese bread called *chipa* — and Guaraní and the related Tupí gave Spanish (and English) *jaguar*, *tapir*, *ñandú*, *mandioca*, *tapioca* and the River Plate word for pineapple, *ananá*. Even the Argentine *che* has been traced by some to Guaraní, though its origin is debated.

### Quechua and Aymara in the Andes

**Quechua**, the language of the Inca state, has several million speakers in Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador (as Kichwa) and neighbouring countries, and is among the most widely spoken indigenous language families of the Americas. **Aymara** is spoken around Lake Titicaca, in Bolivia, Peru and Chile. Both are official in Bolivia, and Quechua and Aymara are official in Peru where they predominate. Andean Spanish shows their influence:

- **The pluperfect of surprise or report**: *¡Había sido tu hermano!* — "So it was your brother!" (a discovery, not a past-before-past).
- **The double possessive**: *su casa de mi tía* — my aunt's house.
- **Softeners**: *nomás* (*Pase nomás*, do come in), frequent diminutives, *pues* at the end (*Ya, pues*).

Quechua words are part of everyday Spanish, many far beyond the Andes: *papa* (potato), *cancha* (pitch, court), *choclo* (corn on the cob), *palta* (avocado, in the Southern Cone and Peru), *poroto* (bean), *carpa* (tent), *chacra* (small farm), *guagua* (baby, in the Andes), *pampa*, *llama*, *cóndor* and *puma*. *Alpaca* comes from Aymara.

### Nahuatl in Mexico

Nahuatl, the language of the Mexica (Aztecs), is still spoken by more than a million Mexicans; a 2003 Mexican law recognises the country's indigenous languages as national languages alongside Spanish. Through Spanish, Nahuatl gave the world *chocolate*, *tomate*, *aguacate*, *chile*, *cacao*, *chicle* and *coyote*. Mexican Spanish keeps many more: *cuate* (buddy, from "twin"), *guajolote* (turkey), *elote* (corn on the cob), *popote* (drinking straw), *papalote* (kite), *tianguis* (street market), *escuincle* (kid) and *apapachar* (to cuddle). Nahuatl place names explain the *tl* and *x* of Mexican spelling: *Popocatépetl*, *Tlaxcala*, *Xochimilco*.

<Section id="three-voices" kind="reading" title="Reading: three voices">

Three short portraits, written for this lesson. The people are invented; their situations are common.

> **Ramona, 68, Asunción.** En casa siempre hablamos guaraní; el castellano era para la escuela y para el banco. Mis nietos hablan las dos cosas mezcladas, el jopara, y antes eso me molestaba. Ahora no. Cuando la más chiquita me dice *che jarýi* —«abuelita mía»— en medio de una frase en castellano, pienso que el idioma de mi madre todavía tiene casa.
>
> **Aitor, 24, Bilbao.** Estudié en euskera desde los tres años, porque mis padres, que no lo hablaban, quisieron que yo lo tuviera. Ahora trabajo en castellano, sueño en las dos lenguas y hago las cuentas en euskera. Mi abuela de Burgos se ríe de mí porque digo *si tendría tiempo* en vez de *si tuviera*. Yo le digo que es mi acento, no un error. Ella dice que es las dos cosas.
>
> **Daniela, 17, Los Ángeles.** Mi abuela me corrige el español y mis amigos me corrigen el inglés, así que nunca gano. Con mis primas hablamos en las dos lenguas a la vez, y nadie se pierde. Este año empecé una clase de español para hablantes de herencia y descubrí que sé muchísimo más de lo que creía. Lo que me falta es escribirlo. Lo que me sobran son ganas.

| Expression | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *che jarýi* | (Guaraní) my grandmother |
| *tener casa* | here: to have a home, to survive |
| *hacer las cuentas* | to do sums |
| *nunca gano* | I can never win |
| *nadie se pierde* | nobody gets lost (everyone follows) |
| *lo que me sobran son ganas* | what I have plenty of is enthusiasm |

Each voice illustrates a theme of this lesson: diglossia and its slow change (Ramona), schooling in a co-official language and a contact feature that is both regional and non-standard (Aitor), heritage bilingualism and code-switching (Daniela).

</Section>

<Section id="variation-norm" kind="grammar" title="Variation, prestige and the norm">

Linguists describe variation along three axes: **where** you are from (*variación diatópica*), **who** you are socially (*diastrática*) and **what situation** you are in (*diafásica*). A single speaker moves along the third axis many times a day.

Some features carry prestige; others are stigmatised, and the reasons are social, not logical. Here is how the current norm treats some common forms:

| Form | How common | What the norm says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *seseo* (*casa* and *caza* alike) | most of the Spanish-speaking world | fully standard |
| *ustedes* for all plural "you" | all of Latin America, the Canaries, western Andalusia | fully standard |
| *vos tenés* | Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, Central America | standard in those regions |
| *habían muchas personas* | very widespread in Latin America and parts of Spain | existential *haber* should stay singular: *había* |
| *dijistes* | widespread in informal speech | non-standard: *dijiste* |
| *haiga* | rural and popular speech | non-standard: *haya* |

### The academies

The **Real Academia Española** was founded in 1713, with the motto *Limpia, fija y da esplendor*. Today it works with 22 other academies — across Latin America, in the Philippines, in Equatorial Guinea and in the US — in the **Asociación de Academias de la Lengua Española (ASALE)**, founded in 1951. Since the 1990s their reference works have been jointly produced and explicitly **pan-Hispanic**: the *Diccionario panhispánico de dudas* (2005), the *Nueva gramática de la lengua española* (2009), the *Ortografía* (2010) and the *Diccionario de la lengua española* (23rd edition, 2014, continuously updated online).

Their model is a **polycentric norm**: correct Spanish is the educated usage of each region, so a Mexican, a Madrilenian and a Buenos Aires professional can all speak "correctly" and differently. What the academies do *not* do is legislate. They have no power to fine anyone, and their dictionary records words because people use them — the old joke that "the RAE has approved a word" misunderstands its role. Their recommendations matter because schools, publishers, courts and the media choose to follow them.

</Section>

<Section id="inclusive-language" kind="culture" title="The debate on inclusive language">

Few language questions have been as hotly debated in the Spanish-speaking world since the 2010s as *lenguaje inclusivo*. This section sets out the proposals, the main arguments on each side, and the academies' position. It takes no side: you will meet educated, thoughtful speakers on both.

### What is proposed

| Proposal | Example | Where you will see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doubling (*desdoblamiento*) | *los ciudadanos y las ciudadanas*; *todos y todas* | speeches, official greetings |
| Collective or generic nouns | *la ciudadanía*, *el alumnado*, *el profesorado*, *las personas* | administrative and educational style guides |
| Graphic symbols | *tod@s*, *todxs* | informal writing, activist texts |
| A new ending *-e* | *todes*, *les alumnes*, the pronoun *elle* | some groups in Argentina, Chile, Spain and elsewhere, especially to include non-binary people |

### Arguments in favour

- Language reflects and shapes how we see the world; if women are always included under a masculine form, they can become less visible.
- Proponents cite psycholinguistic experiments suggesting that generic masculines often call up male images first.
- Non-binary people ask to be named in a way that fits them, and *-e* offers a pronounceable form.
- Languages change; many changes now considered normal began as deliberate innovations by a minority.

### Arguments against

- In Spanish grammar, the masculine is the unmarked gender: *los alumnos* already includes everyone, and gender in grammar is not the same as sex.
- Doubling makes texts long and agreement chains cumbersome (*los y las representantes elegidos y elegidas…*).
- *@* and *x* cannot be pronounced, and they cause problems for screen readers and for people learning to read.
- The *-e* ending has not been adopted by most speakers; critics argue that change should come from general usage, not from institutions or groups.

### The academies' position

The RAE's 2020 report on inclusive language, prepared at the request of the Spanish government, maintains that the generic masculine is inclusive and grammatically correct. It accepts doubling as legitimate in some contexts, such as greetings (*señoras y señores*), but considers it unnecessary when the masculine is clearly generic; it accepts collective nouns where they are natural and do not change the meaning; and it considers *@*, *x* and *-e* foreign to the morphological system of Spanish. Some governments, universities and companies have published guides that favour doubling and collective nouns; others have restricted *-e* or *x* in schools or official documents. As with all usage, what happens next will be decided by speakers.

**For your own Spanish:** in exams and formal writing, the generic masculine, collective nouns and the occasional greeting doublet are all accepted. Use *-e* forms only if you understand the context and intend the statement they make.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Spanglish is broken Spanish."** It is a set of contact practices — loans, calques, code-switching — used by bilinguals with command of two languages. Whether it suits a given situation is a question of register, not of intelligence.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The purest Spanish is spoken in Spain"** — or in Valladolid, or Bogotá, or wherever local pride puts it. Every variety is a development of the same Castilian; none is the original. The academies' own norm is polycentric.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The RAE makes the rules."** It describes educated usage and makes recommendations, together with the other 22 academies. It cannot ban a word, and it adds words to the dictionary because they are already in use.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Contact features are mistakes."** *Hoy comí* in Galicia and *su casa de mi tía* in the Andes are regular features of those varieties. Some contact features are also non-standard — *si tendría* is one — and a C2 speaker knows both facts.

</Misconception>

</Section>

<Reflection title="Each in their own language">

In the account of Pentecost, the crowd in Jerusalem is amazed not that everyone suddenly speaks one language, but that each hears the apostles "in his own language" (Acts 2:6). Babel's confusion is healed without erasing difference. Whatever one thinks of any particular debate in this lesson, there is something worth honouring in a grandmother's Guaraní, a student's Basque and a teenager's two-language sentences: each is a way that people have found to be at home.

</Reflection>
