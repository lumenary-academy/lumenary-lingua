<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="Looking ahead">

You can now tell what happened (<LessonLink slug="trips-and-irregular-pasts">preterite</LessonLink>) and what used to be (<LessonLink slug="when-i-was-young">imperfect</LessonLink>). This lesson turns to what's coming. Spanish has two main ways of talking about the future, one of which you can already almost use, since it's built from *ir*. You'll also learn to talk about the weather, the first thing anyone checks before making plans, to invite people out, and to say who you're giving, sending or telling something *to*.

</Section>

<Section id="ir-a" kind="grammar" title="Ir a + infinitive: plans and intentions">

The simplest way to talk about the future is **ir** (in the present) + **a** + **infinitive**, just like English "going to":

- <Say lang="es">Voy a visitar a mis abuelos.</Say> I'm going to visit my grandparents.
- <Say lang="es">¿Qué vas a hacer este fin de semana?</Say> What are you going to do this weekend?
- <Say lang="es">Vamos a cenar fuera.</Say> We're going to eat out. (*Vamos a…* can also mean "Let's…")
- <Say lang="es">Va a llover.</Say> It's going to rain.

Use it for **plans and intentions** and for things that are **clearly about to happen**. It's the most common way of talking about the future in conversation, especially in Latin America. Object and reflexive pronouns go before *ir* or on the end of the infinitive: <Say lang="es">Me voy a levantar temprano.</Say> = <Say lang="es">Voy a levantarme temprano.</Say>

</Section>

<Section id="future" kind="grammar" title="The simple future">

The future tense is the easiest tense to form: take the **whole infinitive** and add one set of endings, the same for -ar, -er and -ir verbs:

| | viajar | volver | ir |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | viajar**é** | volver**é** | ir**é** |
| tú | viajar**ás** | volver**ás** | ir**ás** |
| él, ella, usted | viajar**á** | volver**á** | ir**á** |
| nosotros, nosotras | viajar**emos** | volver**emos** | ir**emos** |
| vosotros, vosotras | viajar**éis** | volver**éis** | ir**éis** |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | viajar**án** | volver**án** | ir**án** |

Every form except *nosotros* has a written accent. Even *ir* and *ser* are regular: *iré, seré*.

### Irregular stems

A few common verbs change their stem, but keep the same endings:

| Drop the vowel, add **d** | Drop the vowel | Shorter stem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tener → **tendr-**é | poder → **podr-**é | hacer → **har-**é |
| poner → **pondr-**é | saber → **sabr-**é | decir → **dir-**é |
| salir → **saldr-**é | querer → **querr-**é | |
| venir → **vendr-**é | haber → **habr-**á (*habrá* = there will be) | |

- <Say lang="es">El año que viene tendré más tiempo.</Say> Next year I'll have more time.
- <Say lang="es">¿Podrás venir a la fiesta?</Say> Will you be able to come to the party?
- <Say lang="es">Mañana hará buen tiempo.</Say> Tomorrow the weather will be good.
- <Say lang="es">Habrá mucha gente.</Say> There will be a lot of people.

### Ir a or the future?

Both refer to the future, and often either works. As a guide:

- **ir a** for plans already decided and for things you can see coming: *Voy a estudiar medicina* (I've decided).
- **the future** for predictions, forecasts and promises, and in more formal or written language: *Mañana lloverá en el norte* (a forecast). <Say lang="es">Te llamaré.</Say> (I'll call you: a promise.)

<Note>

The future also expresses **guesses about the present**: *¿Dónde estará Ana?* means "Where can Ana be?", *Tendrá unos treinta años* "He must be about thirty". You'll meet this use more later; for now, just recognise it.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="weather" kind="grammar" title="Talking about the weather">

Spanish describes the weather in three ways, and English "it's" can match any of them:

1. **hacer** + noun: <Say lang="es">Hace calor.</Say> <Say lang="es">Hace mucho frío.</Say> (It's very cold: *mucho*, because *frío* is a noun here.) <Say lang="es">Hace sol.</Say> <Say lang="es">Hace buen tiempo.</Say>
2. **a verb of its own**: <Say lang="es">Llueve.</Say> (It rains / it's raining.) <Say lang="es">Nieva.</Say> In the future: *lloverá, nevará*; with *ir a*: *va a llover*.
3. **estar** or **hay**: <Say lang="es">Está nublado.</Say> <Say lang="es">Hay niebla.</Say>

Temperature: <Say lang="es">Hace veinte grados.</Say> or, in Spain, <Say lang="es">Estamos a veinte grados.</Say> Most Spanish-speaking countries use degrees Celsius.

Keep three "cold"s apart:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">Hace frío.</Say> | It's cold (the weather). |
| <Say lang="es">Tengo frío.</Say> | I'm cold (I feel cold). The same with *tengo calor*. |
| <Say lang="es">La sopa está fría.</Say> | The soup is cold (a state of a thing). |

</Section>

<Section id="invitations" kind="grammar" title="Inviting, accepting, declining">

| To invite | To accept | To decline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">¿Quieres venir al cine?</Say> | <Say lang="es">¡Claro!</Say> / <Say lang="es">¡Me encantaría!</Say> | <Say lang="es">Lo siento, no puedo. Tengo que estudiar.</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">¿Te apetece ir a la playa?</Say> (Spain) | <Say lang="es">¡Vale!</Say> (Spain) / <Say lang="es">¡Dale!</Say> | <Say lang="es">¡Qué pena! Ese día voy a estar fuera.</Say> |
| <Say lang="es">¿Qué tal si cenamos juntos?</Say> | <Say lang="es">¡Perfecto!</Say> / <Say lang="es">¡Buena idea!</Say> | <Say lang="es">Gracias, pero otro día.</Say> |

Then arrange the details: <Say lang="es">¿A qué hora quedamos?</Say> (Spain) or <Say lang="es">¿A qué hora nos vemos?</Say>, and <Say lang="es">¿Dónde quedamos?</Say> / <Say lang="es">¿Dónde nos vemos?</Say> In Spain, *quedar* means "to arrange to meet": *Quedamos a las ocho en la plaza.* When you decline, it's polite to give a reason with **tener que** + infinitive (*tengo que trabajar*) and suggest another time.

</Section>

<Section id="indirect" kind="grammar" title="To me, to you, to her: indirect object pronouns">

You already know these pronouns from *gustar* (<LessonLink slug="food-and-places">lesson 6</LessonLink>): *me gusta* = "it is pleasing **to me**". An **indirect object** is the person *to* or *for* whom something is done: I give the book **to Ana**, I buy a present **for my father**.

| | to/for |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">me</Say> | me |
| <Say lang="es">te</Say> | you (*tú*) |
| <Say lang="es">le</Say> | him, her, you (*usted*) |
| <Say lang="es">nos</Say> | us |
| <Say lang="es">os</Say> | you all (*vosotros*) |
| <Say lang="es">les</Say> | them, you all (*ustedes*) |

They go in the same places as *lo* and *la*: before a conjugated verb, or attached to an infinitive.

- <Say lang="es">Te escribo mañana.</Say> I'll write to you tomorrow.
- <Say lang="es">Mi madre me regaló un reloj.</Say> My mother gave me a watch (as a present).
- <Say lang="es">Les voy a mandar una foto.</Say> I'm going to send them a photo.

Very often Spanish uses **both** the pronoun and the named person, which sounds redundant to English ears but is normal and usually expected: <Say lang="es">Le di el regalo a Ana.</Say> (I gave the present to Ana.) <Say lang="es">¿Les explicas el plan a tus padres?</Say>

**Dar** (to give) is the verb you'll use most with these pronouns. In the present it's regular except for *yo*: *doy, das, da, damos, dais, dan*.

### A first look at double pronouns: se lo

When an indirect and a direct pronoun come together, the **indirect one goes first**: <Say lang="es">¿El libro? Te lo doy mañana.</Say> (The book? I'll give it to you tomorrow.) <Say lang="es">Me lo prometiste.</Say> (You promised it to me.)

But Spanish never says *le lo* or *les la*. When **le** or **les** meets **lo, la, los, las**, it becomes **se**:

- <Say lang="es">Le doy el libro a Ana.</Say> → <Say lang="es">Se lo doy.</Say> I give it to her.
- <Say lang="es">Les mandé las fotos a mis primos.</Say> → <Say lang="es">Se las mandé.</Say> I sent them to them.

This *se* is not reflexive; it's just *le/les* in disguise. If it's unclear who *se* is, add *a él, a ella, a usted, a ellos…*: *Se lo doy a ella.* You'll practise combinations like these much more later; for now, learn to recognise *se lo* and use *me lo, te lo*.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="Saturday in the mountains">

Carmen is from Madrid. Yusuf, from Morocco, is studying there for a year. On Wednesday, after class:

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Oye, Yusuf, ¿qué vas a hacer este fin de semana?</Say> | Hey, Yusuf, what are you doing this weekend? |
| Yusuf | <Say lang="es">Todavía no lo sé. ¿Por qué?</Say> | I don't know yet. Why? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Vamos a ir a la sierra el sábado con unos amigos. ¿Te apetece venir?</Say> | We're going to the mountains on Saturday with some friends. Do you fancy coming? |
| Yusuf | <Say lang="es">¡Me encantaría! Pero ¿qué tiempo va a hacer?</Say> | I'd love to! But what's the weather going to be like? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Según el pronóstico, por la mañana hará sol, pero por la tarde lloverá.</Say> | According to the forecast, it'll be sunny in the morning, but it'll rain in the afternoon. |
| Yusuf | <Say lang="es">Entonces saldremos temprano, ¿no?</Say> | Then we'll leave early, right? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Sí. ¿Quedamos a las siete y media en la estación?</Say> | Yes. Shall we meet at half past seven at the station? |
| Yusuf | <Say lang="es">¿A las siete y media? Uf… Vale, estaré allí.</Say> | At half past seven? Phew… OK, I'll be there. |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Otra cosa: el domingo es el cumpleaños de Elena. ¿Le compramos algo juntos?</Say> | Another thing: Sunday is Elena's birthday. Shall we buy her something together? |
| Yusuf | <Say lang="es">Buena idea. ¿Qué le podemos regalar?</Say> | Good idea. What can we give her? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Le encantan los libros de viajes. Hay una librería muy buena cerca de Sol.</Say> | She loves travel books. There's a very good bookshop near Sol. |
| Yusuf | <Say lang="es">Perfecto. Lo compramos el viernes y se lo damos el domingo.</Say> | Perfect. We'll buy it on Friday and give it to her on Sunday. |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">¿Y le escribimos una tarjeta?</Say> | And shall we write her a card? |
| Yusuf | <Say lang="es">Sí, pero la escribes tú. ¡Mi letra es horrible!</Say> | Yes, but you write it. My handwriting is terrible! |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Vale, te lo prometo. ¡Nos vemos el sábado!</Say> | OK, I promise. See you on Saturday! |

**Notes.** *La sierra* here means the mountains near Madrid. *Según* = according to. *Allí* = there. *Sol* is the Puerta del Sol, a square in central Madrid. Notice how often Spanish uses the present for arrangements (*¿Quedamos…?, Lo compramos el viernes*), like English "Shall we…?" and "We'll…". In *se lo damos*, *se* stands for *le* (to Elena) and *lo* for the book. In Mexico, Carmen might ask *¿Se te antoja venir?* or *¿Quieres venir?*, and Yusuf might answer *¡Claro!* rather than *¡Vale!*

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Stressing the future">

Future forms are stressed on the ending, and all but *nosotros* show it with an accent: <Say lang="es">hablaré</Say> (ha-bla-RÉ), <Say lang="es">tendrás</Say> (ten-DRÁS), <Say lang="es">lloverá</Say> (llo-ve-RÁ), <Say lang="es">saldremos</Say> (sal-DRE-mos). Don't stress the stem, or the word may sound like another tense.

The irregular stems put two consonants together: *tendré* (-ndr-), *saldré* (-ldr-), *podré* (-dr-). Make the *r* a single quick tap after the *d*, as in *madre*.

Weather words give you practice with diphthongs: <Say lang="es">llueve</Say> (LLUE-ve, two syllables), <Say lang="es">nieva</Say> (NIE-va), <Say lang="es">viento</Say> (VIEN-to), <Say lang="es">tiempo</Say> (TIEM-po).

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Seasons upside down, and 'ahorita'">

If you plan a trip to the Spanish-speaking world, remember that the seasons depend on the hemisphere. In Argentina, Chile, Uruguay and Paraguay, Christmas falls in summer, and families may celebrate with a barbecue in the heat, while July is mid-winter. Near the equator, in Colombia, Ecuador or Central America, the year is divided less into four seasons than into rainy and dry periods, and the climate depends mostly on altitude: Bogotá, at over 2,500 metres, is cool all year round.

Plans also come with their own vocabulary of time. In Mexico, *ahorita* (literally "little now") can mean "right now" or "in a little while", depending on tone and context, and *ya voy* ("I'm already going") can mean "I'm on my way" or "I'll be there soon". Expectations about punctuality vary by context everywhere: business meetings usually start on time, while arriving exactly at the stated hour for a party at someone's home can, in many places, mean arriving before the hosts are ready.

<Reflection title="Si Dios quiere">

Many Spanish speakers, when they talk about plans, add *si Dios quiere* ("God willing"): *Nos vemos el lunes, si Dios quiere.* It's an echo of an old idea, found in the Letter of James, that our plans for tomorrow are held in God's hands. The word *ojalá* ("I hope so, if only") comes from Andalusian Arabic and originally meant something close to "if God wills". Yusuf, from Morocco, would recognise the phrase behind it. Whether or not people say it with faith, the expression carries a certain humility about the future that is worth noticing.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"*Voy a viajo.*" After *ir a*, the verb stays in the infinitive: **Voy a viajar**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Saliré, teneré, haceré.*" These verbs have irregular future stems: **saldré, tendré, haré**. The endings, though, are always the regular ones.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Es frío* means it's cold today." Weather uses *hacer*: **Hace frío**. A person who feels cold says **Tengo frío**; a thing that is cold *está fría*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Le lo doy.*" Spanish never puts *le* or *les* before *lo, la, los, las*. They become *se*: **Se lo doy**.

</Misconception>
