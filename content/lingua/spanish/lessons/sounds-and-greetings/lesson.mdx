<Section id="welcome" kind="motivation" title="Spanish sounds the way it is written">

Here is good news to start with: Spanish spelling is close to phonetic. Once you know how each letter sounds, you can read aloud almost any word you meet, even one you have never seen, and you can usually spell a word you have only heard. English has *though*, *through*, *tough* and *cough*; Spanish has nothing like that.

This lesson gives you the whole sound system in one go, then the rules for stress and written accents, and then the words you need for your first conversation: hello, goodbye, how are you, what is your name, nice to meet you. Take your time with the sounds. Everything later in the course is built on them, and a good accent is mostly a matter of a few habits learned early.

Wherever you see a speaker icon next to a Spanish word, your browser can read it aloud if it has a Spanish voice. Listen, then say it yourself, out loud.

</Section>

<Section id="vowels" kind="pronunciation" title="Five vowels, always the same">

English has around a dozen vowel sounds (more, depending on the accent) and writes them with five letters in confusing ways. Spanish has exactly five vowel sounds, one for each letter, and they never change.

| Letter | Sound (approximately) | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a | the *a* in *father*, but shorter | <Say lang="es">casa</Say>, <Say lang="es">mapa</Say> |
| e | the *e* in *bed* | <Say lang="es">mesa</Say>, <Say lang="es">tres</Say> |
| i | the *ee* in *see*, but shorter | <Say lang="es">sí</Say>, <Say lang="es">libro</Say> |
| o | the *o* in *for*, with rounded lips, no glide | <Say lang="es">no</Say>, <Say lang="es">solo</Say> |
| u | the *oo* in *food*, but shorter | <Say lang="es">uno</Say>, <Say lang="es">mucho</Say> |

Two habits make the difference:

1. **Keep vowels short and pure.** English speakers tend to slide *o* into *ow* and *e* into *ay*. Spanish <Say lang="es">no</Say> is a single, clean sound, not "noh-oo".
2. **Never weaken an unstressed vowel.** In English *banana*, only the middle *a* is a real *a*; the others become a lazy "uh". In Spanish <Say lang="es">banana</Say>, all three are full, clear *a*s.

When two vowels meet, they often glide into one syllable: <Say lang="es">bueno</Say> (good), <Say lang="es">bien</Say> (well), <Say lang="es">seis</Say> (six), <Say lang="es">aire</Say> (air). Each vowel still keeps its own sound; you just say them quickly together.

</Section>

<Section id="consonants" kind="pronunciation" title="Consonants that differ from English">

Most consonants sound roughly as in English. These are the ones to learn carefully.

| Spelling | How it sounds | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ñ | *ny* as in *canyon*; a letter of its own | <Say lang="es">España</Say>, <Say lang="es">mañana</Say> |
| ll and y | for most speakers, *y* as in *yes* | <Say lang="es">llamo</Say>, <Say lang="es">yo</Say> |
| r (between vowels, after a consonant) | a single quick tap of the tongue, like the *tt* in American *butter* | <Say lang="es">pero</Say>, <Say lang="es">tres</Say> |
| rr, and r at the start of a word | a rolled trill: several taps | <Say lang="es">perro</Say>, <Say lang="es">Roma</Say> |
| j, and g before e or i | a breathy sound from the back of the throat, like Scottish *loch*; softer, close to English *h*, in much of Latin America | <Say lang="es">jamón</Say>, <Say lang="es">gente</Say> |
| g before a, o, u | hard, as in *go* | <Say lang="es">gato</Say>, <Say lang="es">gusto</Say> |
| gue, gui | hard *g*; the u is silent | <Say lang="es">guitarra</Say> |
| h | always silent | <Say lang="es">hola</Say>, <Say lang="es">hasta</Say> |
| c before e or i, and z | *th* as in *think* in most of Spain; *s* in Latin America, the Canary Islands and parts of Andalusia | <Say lang="es">gracias</Say>, <Say lang="es">cinco</Say>, <Say lang="es">zapato</Say> |
| c before a, o, u, and qu | *k* (the u of qu is silent) | <Say lang="es">casa</Say>, <Say lang="es">queso</Say> |
| b and v | the same sound: a *b* at the start or after m or n, and a softer *b* with the lips barely touching between vowels | <Say lang="es">vaso</Say>, <Say lang="es">bebe</Say>, <Say lang="es">uva</Say> |
| d between vowels | soft, like the *th* in *this* | <Say lang="es">nada</Say>, <Say lang="es">todo</Say> |
| ch | as in *church* | <Say lang="es">chocolate</Say> |

**Seseo and distinción.** In most of Spain, <Say lang="es-ES">gracias</Say> is said "GRAH-thyahs" and *casa* (house) and *caza* (hunting) sound different. In Latin America it is "GRAH-syahs", and *casa* and *caza* sound alike. The first is called *distinción*, the second *seseo*. Both are correct, educated Spanish; neither is a lisp. Pick one and use it consistently. If you hear more of one variety, that is the natural choice.

**ll and y across the world.** Almost everyone today pronounces *ll* and *y* the same (this is called *yeísmo*). In Argentina and Uruguay both sound like the *s* in *measure* or the *sh* in *show*: <Say lang="es-AR">me llamo</Say>, "meh SHAH-moh".

**Tapping and trilling.** If the rolled *rr* does not come yet, don't worry: it is the sound learners take longest to get, and people will understand you. Practise with *pero* (but) and *perro* (dog). The difference between one tap and a trill is the difference between those two words.

<Note>

**Spelling your name.** The letters are called: a, be, ce, de, e, efe, ge, hache, i, jota, ka, ele, eme, ene, eñe, o, pe, cu, erre, ese, te, u, uve, uve doble, equis, ye, zeta. In much of Latin America, *v* is often called *ve* or *ve corta* and *w* *doble ve*; *y* is also called *i griega*. To spell *Kofi*, say "ka, o, efe, i".

</Note>

<Section id="stress" kind="grammar" title="Stress, written accents, ¿ and ¡">

Every Spanish word of two or more syllables has one stressed syllable, and two rules tell you which one it is.

1. **A word ending in a vowel, in -n or in -s is stressed on the second-to-last syllable:** <Say lang="es">casa</Say> (CA-sa), <Say lang="es">hablan</Say> (HA-blan, they speak), <Say lang="es">buenos</Say> (BUE-nos).
2. **A word ending in any other consonant is stressed on the last syllable:** <Say lang="es">hablar</Say> (ha-BLAR, to speak), <Say lang="es">ciudad</Say> (ciu-DAD, city), <Say lang="es">español</Say> (es-pa-ÑOL).
3. **A word that breaks these rules shows its stress with a written accent** (*tilde*) on the stressed vowel: <Say lang="es">café</Say> (ends in a vowel but stressed on the last syllable), <Say lang="es">inglés</Say>, <Say lang="es">lápiz</Say> (ends in z but stressed on the first), <Say lang="es">música</Say>, <Say lang="es">teléfono</Say>.

So when you see an accent, stress that vowel; when you don't, apply rules 1 and 2. A few more things about accents:

- The accent never changes the sound of the vowel, only where the stress falls. *é* is still the *e* of *bed*.
- Words stressed three syllables from the end always carry an accent: *música*, *teléfono*, *América*.
- An accent on *i* or *u* next to another vowel splits them into two syllables: <Say lang="es">día</Say> is *DÍ-a*, not one syllable.
- Some short words take an accent only to tell them apart from a twin: <Say lang="es">tú</Say> (you) and *tu* (your), <Say lang="es">sí</Say> (yes) and *si* (if), <Say lang="es">él</Say> (he) and *el* (the).
- Question words carry an accent: <Say lang="es">¿cómo?</Say> (how?), <Say lang="es">¿qué?</Say> (what?).

Since a missing accent can change a word (*papá*, dad; *papa*, potato, or the Pope), treat accents as part of the spelling. On a phone, hold down a vowel to get its accented form.

**Upside-down marks.** Spanish opens questions and exclamations with ¿ and ¡, so a reader knows from the start how to say the sentence. They go where the question or exclamation begins, which is not always the start of the sentence: <Say lang="es">Y tú, ¿cómo te llamas?</Say> (And you, what's your name?).

</Section>

<Section id="greetings" kind="grammar" title="Greeting by the clock, and tú or usted">

### Which greeting when

Spanish greetings follow the clock and the light more than an exact time. Say <Say lang="es">buenos días</Say> in the morning, <Say lang="es">buenas tardes</Say> from lunch until it gets dark, and <Say lang="es">buenas noches</Say> after dark. *Buenas noches* is both "good evening" when you arrive and "good night" when you leave. Among friends you will often hear just <Say lang="es">¡Buenas!</Say> at any time.

<Say lang="es">Hola</Say> works everywhere and is often combined: <Say lang="es">Hola, buenos días.</Say>

### Two ways to say "you"

Spanish has an informal and a formal word for "you" when talking to one person.

| | Informal: **tú** | Formal: **usted** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use it with | friends, family, children, classmates, people your age in relaxed settings | people you don't know, older people, officials, customers, anyone you want to show respect to |
| How are you? | <Say lang="es">¿Cómo estás?</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Cómo está usted?</Say> |
| What's your name? | <Say lang="es">¿Cómo te llamas?</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Cómo se llama usted?</Say> |
| And you? | <Say lang="es">¿Y tú?</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Y usted?</Say> |

Notice that the verb changes too: *estás* with *tú*, *está* with *usted*. You will see why in the <LessonLink slug="who-you-are">next lesson</LessonLink>, when we learn the verb forms properly. For now, learn each question as a whole phrase.

*Usted* is often written *Ud.* or *Vd.* It is not cold or unfriendly; it is courteous. If someone wants you to use *tú*, they will tell you: <Say lang="es">puedes tutearme</Say> ("you can call me *tú*").

**Vos.** In Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay, and widely in Central America, people use *vos* instead of *tú*, with slightly different verb forms: <Say lang="es-AR">¿Cómo te llamás?</Say>, <Say lang="es-AR">¿Y vos?</Say>. This course teaches *tú* forms, but you will meet *vos* in films, songs and conversations, and now you will recognise it.

### Names

To give your name, say <Say lang="es">Me llamo Ana</Say>. You can also say <Say lang="es">Soy Ana</Say> (I am Ana), which is very natural when introducing yourself. When you meet someone, say <Say lang="es">mucho gusto</Say> or <Say lang="es">encantado</Say> / <Say lang="es">encantada</Say>. *Encantado* agrees with the person speaking: Kofi says *encantado*, Nuria says *encantada*, whoever they are talking to. The reply is <Say lang="es">igualmente</Say> (likewise) or another *mucho gusto*.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="Two first meetings">

**At a university in Valencia, Spain.** Nuria, a student, meets Kofi, who has just arrived from Ghana for a semester. They are young and in a relaxed setting, so they use *tú*.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nuria | <Say lang="es">¡Hola! ¿Qué tal?</Say> | Hi! How's it going? |
| Kofi | <Say lang="es">Hola. Bien, gracias. ¿Y tú?</Say> | Hi. Fine, thanks. And you? |
| Nuria | <Say lang="es">Muy bien. Me llamo Nuria. ¿Cómo te llamas?</Say> | Very well. My name's Nuria. What's your name? |
| Kofi | <Say lang="es">Me llamo Kofi.</Say> | My name's Kofi. |
| Nuria | <Say lang="es">Perdón, ¿cómo?</Say> | Sorry, what? |
| Kofi | <Say lang="es">Kofi: ka, o, efe, i.</Say> | Kofi: K, O, F, I. |
| Nuria | <Say lang="es">¡Ah, Kofi! Mucho gusto.</Say> | Ah, Kofi! Nice to meet you. |
| Kofi | <Say lang="es">Encantado, Nuria.</Say> | Pleased to meet you, Nuria. |
| Nuria | <Say lang="es">Bueno, hasta luego.</Say> | Well, see you later. |
| Kofi | <Say lang="es">¡Chao! Hasta mañana.</Say> | Bye! See you tomorrow. |

**At a hotel in Lima, Peru, in the evening.** Julián works at the reception desk. The guest, Elena Ríos, is older and a customer, so both use *usted*.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Julián | <Say lang="es">Buenas noches, señora. Bienvenida.</Say> | Good evening, madam. Welcome. |
| Sra. Ríos | <Say lang="es">Buenas noches. Me llamo Elena Ríos.</Say> | Good evening. My name is Elena Ríos. |
| Julián | <Say lang="es">Mucho gusto, señora Ríos. Me llamo Julián.</Say> | Nice to meet you, Mrs Ríos. My name is Julián. |
| Sra. Ríos | <Say lang="es">Encantada, Julián.</Say> | Pleased to meet you, Julián. |
| Julián | <Say lang="es">¿Cómo está usted?</Say> | How are you? |
| Sra. Ríos | <Say lang="es">Más o menos, gracias. ¿Y usted?</Say> | So-so, thanks. And you? |
| Julián | <Say lang="es">Muy bien, gracias. Aquí tiene la llave.</Say> | Very well, thank you. Here is the key. |
| Sra. Ríos | <Say lang="es">Muchas gracias.</Say> | Thank you very much. |
| Julián | <Say lang="es">De nada. Buenas noches.</Say> | You're welcome. Good night. |

Notice *bienvenida* (welcome): like *encantada*, it ends in *-a* because it describes a woman. To a man, Julián would say *bienvenido*. *Aquí tiene* (here you are) is a handy fixed phrase for handing something over.

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Kisses, handshakes and saying hello to the room">

How people greet with their bodies varies from place to place, so watch what others do and follow.

- **In Spain**, friends and new acquaintances in social settings usually greet with *dos besos*: a light touch of cheeks, right then left, with a kissing sound rather than an actual kiss. This is usual between women and between a man and a woman; men usually shake hands, though family members and close friends may kiss.
- **In much of Latin America**, one cheek kiss is the norm in the same situations, and in Argentina and Uruguay men who are friends or relatives often greet each other with a kiss as well.
- **At work and in formal settings**, a handshake is the safe choice everywhere.

Greeting is also a social duty in a way English speakers sometimes find surprising. In many places it is polite to say <Say lang="es">buenos días</Say> to everyone when you walk into a small shop, a waiting room or a lift, and <Say lang="es">hasta luego</Say> as you leave. Skipping it can seem cold.

The choice between *tú* and *usted* also varies. In Spain, *tú* is widespread, even between strangers of similar age. In much of Latin America *usted* is used more, and in parts of Colombia and Costa Rica people use *usted* even with family and close friends. A safe rule anywhere: begin with *usted* with older people and in formal settings, and switch when you are invited to.

<Reflection title="adiós">

*Adiós* began as *a Dios*, "to God": a way of commending someone to God's care as they leave, much like the English *goodbye*, which grew out of "God be with you". Most speakers today don't think of the origin when they say it, just as English speakers don't hear "God" in *goodbye*. Spanish speakers who pray also notice something about *tú*: prayers address God as *tú*, not *usted*. The intimate form, the one used with family, is the one used with God.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"*Buenas noches* only means good night, at bedtime." Not so: it is also the normal greeting after dark. Walking into a restaurant at 9 p.m., you say *buenas noches*, not *buenas tardes*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"The accent mark changes how the vowel sounds." It doesn't. *á* sounds exactly like *a*; the accent only shows which syllable is stressed (or tells apart pairs like *tú* and *tu*).

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Spanish speakers in Spain lisp." Saying *c* and *z* as *th* is *distinción*, the standard pronunciation of most of Spain, and it applies only to those letters: an *s* is still an *s*. It is no more a lisp than the Latin American *s* is a mistake.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Encantado* agrees with the person I'm talking to." It agrees with the speaker. A woman always says *encantada*, whether she meets a man or a woman.

</Misconception>
