<Section id="connectors" kind="vocabulary" title="The connectors of a C1 argument">

At B1 you learned to say *pero*, *porque* and *por eso* (see <LessonLink slug="opinions-and-debate">Opinions and debate</LessonLink>). Those words still work at C1, but a page of formal Spanish built only on them sounds like a school composition. An educated writer has a wider palette, and each connector carries a small instruction for the reader: *what follows contradicts*, *what follows is a consequence*, *what follows is a condition*. Learn them in families, with their register and the mood that follows.

| Connector | Meaning | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |

<Section id="families" kind="grammar" title="Three families: contrast, cause and consequence, condition">

### Contrast

*Sin embargo*, *no obstante*, *con todo* and *aun así* all introduce an idea that goes against what the reader expected. They are **sentence connectors**, not conjunctions, so they behave like adverbs and are fenced off with punctuation:

- At the start of a sentence, followed by a comma: *El plan es ambicioso. **No obstante,** carece de financiación.*
- After a semicolon, when the two ideas are close: *El plan es ambicioso; **sin embargo,** carece de financiación.*
- In the middle of a clause, between commas: *El plan, **sin embargo,** carece de financiación.*

Never join two full sentences with a bare comma before *sin embargo*. In English *The plan is ambitious, however it lacks funding* is already a slip; in Spanish the equivalent is just as clumsy.

*Ahora bien* is special: it signals that what follows is the point that matters. *El argumento es elegante. **Ahora bien,** ¿se sostiene con datos?* — "That said, does it hold up?" *En cambio* compares two subjects (*Chile exporta cobre; Colombia, **en cambio,** exporta café*), while *por el contrario* reverses a claim (*No mejoró; **por el contrario,** empeoró*).

### Cause and consequence

Causal connectors take the **indicative**, because they present the cause as a fact:

- ***Dado que** el plazo vence mañana, debemos decidir hoy.*
- *No firmaremos, **puesto que** las condiciones han cambiado.*

*A raíz de* and *debido a* take a noun or infinitive, not a clause: *Cerraron la fábrica **a raíz de** las inundaciones.* (Avoid *a raíz de que* in careful writing; say *porque* or *dado que* instead.)

For consequences, *por consiguiente*, *por ende* and *así pues* draw a conclusion in formal prose; *así que* and *entonces* do the same in conversation. *De ahí que* is the interesting one: the consequence it introduces is normally presented as something the reader can observe, and Spanish puts it in the **subjunctive**:

- *Llovió toda la noche; **de ahí que** el partido se suspendiera.*

(You will see in <LessonLink slug="nuances-of-mood-and-aspect">Nuances of mood and aspect</LessonLink> why a known fact can take the subjunctive.)

*De modo que* and *de manera que* change meaning with mood: + indicative is a **result** (*Llegó tarde, de modo que perdió el tren* — so he missed the train), + subjunctive is a **purpose** (*Habla despacio, de modo que todos te entiendan* — so that everyone understands).

### Condition

Beyond *si*, formal Spanish uses conditional conjunctions that always take the **subjunctive**, because the condition has not yet been met:

| Conjunction | Example |
| --- | --- |
| *siempre y cuando* | *Aprobaremos el presupuesto **siempre y cuando** se reduzcan los gastos.* |
| *siempre que* | *Puedes venir **siempre que** avises antes.* |
| *con tal de que* | *Firmo lo que sea **con tal de que** acabemos hoy.* |
| *a menos que* / *a no ser que* | *No saldremos **a menos que** pare la lluvia.* |
| *en caso de que* | ***En caso de que** haya dudas, consulte el anexo.* |

Watch *siempre que*: with the indicative it means "whenever" (*Siempre que viene, trae flores*), with the subjunctive "provided that" (*siempre que venga* = provided he comes). *Siempre y cuando* only has the second meaning, which is why careful writers prefer it in contracts and essays.

</Section>

<Section id="concession" kind="grammar" title="Conceding gracefully">

A strong argument admits what is true in the other side and then shows why it does not change the conclusion. Spanish has a family of **concessive** structures for this, and the choice of mood is where a C1 writer shows control.

### *Por mucho que*, *por más que*, *por muy … que*

These mean "however much / no matter how":

- + **subjunctive**: the speaker does not assert the fact, or treats it as irrelevant. ***Por mucho que** insistas, no cambiaré de opinión.* (Insist all you like.)
- + **indicative**: the fact is real and the speaker asserts it. ***Por mucho que** estudia, no aprueba.* (He does study a lot, yet he fails.)
- With an adjective or adverb, use *por muy … que* + subjunctive: ***Por muy** cansada **que** estés, termina el informe.*

### *Aun cuando*

*Aun cuando* is a formal "even if / even though". With the subjunctive it is hypothetical (***Aun cuando** llueva, saldremos* — even if it rains); with the indicative it presents a fact (***Aun cuando** tenía razón, nadie la escuchó* — even though she was right).

### *Si bien*

*Si bien* means "although, while it is true that", and it takes **only the indicative**, because what it concedes is admitted as true. It belongs to formal writing and pairs with a classic formula of the Spanish essay:

> ***Si bien es cierto que** la medida es costosa, **no lo es menos que** sus beneficios son duraderos.*
>
> While it is true that the measure is costly, it is no less true that its benefits are lasting.

### *Pese a que*, *a pesar de que*, *y eso que*

*Pese a que* and *a pesar de que* work like *aunque*: indicative for facts, subjunctive for hypotheses or for facts the speaker treats as known and unimportant. In conversation, *y eso que* + indicative adds the concession as an afterthought: *Se resfrió, **y eso que** iba bien abrigado.* — "and that's despite being wrapped up warm."

<Example title="One idea, four degrees of formality">

The council will approve the park, although it is expensive.

- Colloquial: *Van a aprobar el parque, aunque es carísimo, ¿eh?*
- Neutral: *El ayuntamiento aprobará el parque, aunque es caro.*
- Formal: *Si bien el proyecto resulta costoso, el ayuntamiento lo aprobará.*
- Formal, with an objection answered: *Por mucho que el proyecto resulte costoso, el ayuntamiento lo aprobará, puesto que sus beneficios superan su precio.*

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="architecture" kind="grammar" title="The architecture of an essay">

The *ensayo argumentativo* taught in Spanish-speaking schools has the same skeleton as its English cousin, but the expected phrases differ. Four moves:

1. **Planteamiento** (introduction): set the context in two or three sentences, then state the thesis plainly. *Este ensayo sostiene que…*, *En las páginas que siguen defenderé que…*, or a question: *Cabe preguntarse si…*
2. **Desarrollo** (body): one paragraph per argument, each opened by a connector of order (*En primer lugar*, *En segundo lugar*, *Por último*) or addition (*Asimismo*, *Además*, *Es más*), and each supported by an example (*Así, por ejemplo,…*; *Baste como ejemplo…*).
3. **Objeción y refutación**: anticipate the strongest objection and answer it. *Podría objetarse que…*, *Se dirá que…*, *Es cierto que…* — then *Ahora bien,…*, *Sin embargo, este argumento pasa por alto que…*
4. **Conclusión**: return to the thesis in new words, widen it, and end on a strong sentence. *En definitiva,…*, *Así pues,…*, *En suma,…*

| Move | Useful phrases |
| --- | --- |
| Stating the thesis | *Este ensayo sostiene que…* · *Me propongo demostrar que…* |
| Adding | *Asimismo,…* · *A ello se suma…* · *Es más,…* |
| Exemplifying | *Así, por ejemplo,…* · *Pensemos en…* · *Un caso ilustrativo es…* |
| Conceding | *Si bien es cierto que…* · *Hay que reconocer que…* |
| Objecting | *Podría objetarse que…* · *Cabe la objeción de que…* |
| Refuting | *Ahora bien,…* · *Este argumento, sin embargo, olvida que…* |
| Concluding | *En definitiva,…* · *Así pues,…* · *Todo ello lleva a concluir que…* |

Spanish essays tolerate longer sentences than English ones, but modern style guides ask for the same virtues: one idea per paragraph, connectors that are *true* (do not write *por consiguiente* unless the second idea really follows), and variety — a paragraph with *sin embargo* three times reads as nervously as one with *however* three times.

</Section>

<Section id="model-essay" kind="reading" title="A model essay: «La calle es de quien la camina»">

Read this short essay, written for this lesson. The connectors are in bold. Notice where the writer concedes, where the objection appears, and how the last sentence echoes the title.

> **La calle es de quien la camina**
>
> En las últimas décadas, muchas ciudades del mundo hispanohablante han cerrado al tráfico sus centros históricos. Pontevedra, en Galicia, es uno de los casos más citados, pero no el único. **Sin embargo,** cada nueva propuesta despierta la misma polémica: comerciantes que temen perder clientes, vecinos que no saben dónde aparcar, conductores que se sienten expulsados. Este ensayo sostiene que, **siempre y cuando** se garantice el acceso de los servicios esenciales y del transporte público, peatonalizar el centro beneficia a la mayoría, incluidos muchos de quienes hoy se oponen.
>
> **En primer lugar,** está la salud. **Dado que** el tráfico es una de las principales fuentes de ruido y de contaminación en las ciudades, reducirlo mejora de inmediato la vida de quienes viven allí. **A ello se suma** la seguridad: una calle sin coches es una calle donde un niño puede soltar la mano de su madre sin que nadie contenga la respiración. **De ahí que** en tantos centros peatonales hayan vuelto a verse niños jugando en la calle.
>
> **En segundo lugar,** el argumento económico, que suele esgrimirse en contra, puede volverse a favor. **Por mucho que** los comerciantes teman lo contrario, el peatón no es un cliente perdido, sino un cliente lento: pasea, mira escaparates, entra, se sienta en una terraza. Quien conduce, **en cambio,** solo se detiene si encuentra dónde aparcar. **Ahora bien,** sería ingenuo afirmar que todos ganan: las tiendas que reciben mercancía voluminosa o que viven de clientes que llegan de lejos necesitan soluciones propias, como horarios de reparto o aparcamientos en las afueras.
>
> **Podría objetarse que** cerrar el centro a los coches perjudica a las personas mayores o con movilidad reducida. Es una objeción seria y, **por ende,** merece una respuesta seria. **Si bien es cierto que** para algunas personas el coche es imprescindible, **no lo es menos que** la mayoría de ellas tampoco encuentra hoy aparcamiento en el centro, **de modo que** el problema no lo crea la peatonalización. **Aun cuando** desaparezca el coche privado, nada impide que circulen taxis adaptados, ambulancias y autobuses de barrio.
>
> **En definitiva,** la pregunta no es si el centro debe pertenecer a los peatones o a los coches, sino cómo repartir con justicia un espacio escaso. **Así pues,** la peatonalización no debería imponerse de un día para otro, sino negociarse con quienes viven y trabajan en el barrio. **Con todo,** la experiencia de muchas ciudades sugiere que, una vez superado el miedo inicial, pocos vecinos querrían volver atrás. La calle, **al fin y al cabo,** es de quien la camina.

| Word or phrase | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *peatonalizar* | to pedestrianise (*el peatón*, pedestrian) |
| *despertar una polémica* | to stir up controversy |
| *contener la respiración* | to hold one's breath |
| *esgrimir un argumento* | to wield, put forward an argument |
| *un escaparate* | a shop window (*una vitrina* in much of Latin America) |
| *imprescindible* | indispensable |
| *de un día para otro* | overnight, all at once |

**How it is built.** Paragraph 1 gives context and a thesis that already contains a condition (*siempre y cuando…*), which makes the claim precise and harder to attack. Paragraphs 2 and 3 each carry one argument, and paragraph 3 concedes a weakness (*Ahora bien…*) before the reader can raise it. Paragraph 4 states the strongest objection in its best form and answers it with the *si bien es cierto que… no lo es menos que…* formula. Paragraph 5 reframes the question, makes a practical recommendation and closes by echoing the title.

Notice the moods: *se garantice* and *teman* (subjunctive after *siempre y cuando* and a non-asserted *por mucho que*), *hayan vuelto* (subjunctive after *de ahí que*), *es* (indicative after *dado que* and *si bien*), *desaparezca* (subjunctive: *aun cuando* with a hypothesis).

</Section>

<Section id="essay-tradition" kind="culture" title="The essay in the Spanish-speaking world">

The essay has a long and proud history in Spanish. In the eighteenth century the Benedictine monk Benito Jerónimo Feijoo filled the volumes of his *Teatro crítico universal* with short pieces attacking superstition and common errors. In the nineteenth, Mariano José de Larra wrote sharp newspaper articles about Spanish society, and in Latin America the essay became the form in which writers argued about identity and independence: José Martí's *Nuestra América* (1891) and José Enrique Rodó's *Ariel* (1900) were read across the continent. Twentieth-century thinkers on both sides of the Atlantic kept the tradition alive, often in newspapers rather than in books.

That history explains two things you will notice. First, Spanish argumentative prose often appears in the **press**: the *columna* and the *tribuna* (see <LessonLink slug="media-and-rhetoric">Media and rhetoric</LessonLink>) are essays in miniature. Second, schoolchildren are trained in it: in Spain, the language paper of the university entrance exam typically asks for a text commentary and a short argumentative piece, and many Latin American curricula ask for an *ensayo argumentativo* with exactly the four moves above. Your reader will recognise the skeleton — and notice if you skip the objection.

<Reflection title="Arguing in good faith">

The best essays state the opposing view so fairly that its defenders would sign it, and only then answer it. That is more than technique: it is a way of treating the other person as someone who reasons, not an obstacle. The Letter of James asks us to be "swift to hear, slow to speak" (James 1:19). In an essay, listening is the paragraph that begins *Podría objetarse que…* — and writing it honestly is a small act of charity.

</Reflection>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common slips">

<Misconception>

**«Aunque…, sin embargo…»** Learners sometimes mark one contrast twice: *Aunque el plan es caro, sin embargo lo aprobaron*. Some languages pair "although" with "but" as a rule; careful Spanish, like English, uses one connector per contrast: *Aunque el plan es caro, lo aprobaron* or *El plan es caro; sin embargo, lo aprobaron.*

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**«Si bien» with the subjunctive.** *Si bien* always concedes a fact, so it takes the indicative: *Si bien **es** caro…*, never *si bien sea caro*. If you want a hypothesis, use *aunque* or *aun cuando* + subjunctive.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**Formal connectors in speech.** *Por ende* and *no obstante* sound stiff in conversation, a little like "hence" and "notwithstanding" at a barbecue. In speech use *así que*, *por eso*, *pero*, *aun así*. Register is part of correctness.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**«En base a».** It is everywhere, but careful writers and style guides advise against it. Prefer *basándose en*, *sobre la base de*, *a partir de* or *según*: *Decidimos **a partir de** los datos.*

</Misconception>

</Section>
