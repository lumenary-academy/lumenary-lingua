<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="The verbs that tell a journey">

In <LessonLink slug="what-happened">the last lesson</LessonLink> you learned the preterite of regular verbs. Try to tell the story of any trip, though, and you'll immediately need *I went*, *it was*, *we had to*, *they said*, *she came*: all irregular. The good news is that the irregular preterites fall into a few families with shared endings, so ten verbs are less work than they look. We'll pair them with the vocabulary of travel and with the small pronouns that save you from repeating "the passport, the passport, the passport".

</Section>

<Section id="ser-ir" kind="grammar" title="Ser and ir: the same past">

The two most irregular verbs share one set of preterite forms:

| | ser / ir |
| --- | --- |
| yo | <Say lang="es">fui</Say> |
| tú | <Say lang="es">fuiste</Say> |
| él, ella, usted | <Say lang="es">fue</Say> |
| nosotros, nosotras | <Say lang="es">fuimos</Say> |
| vosotros, vosotras | <Say lang="es">fuisteis</Say> |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | <Say lang="es">fueron</Say> |

Context always tells you which verb it is. If a place follows with **a** (or *adónde* asks the question), it's *ir*; if a description or a noun follows, it's *ser*:

- <Say lang="es">Fuimos a Cartagena en marzo.</Say> We **went** to Cartagena in March. (*ir*)
- <Say lang="es">El viaje fue muy largo.</Say> The journey **was** very long. (*ser*)
- <Say lang="es">¿Cómo fue el concierto?</Say> How **was** the concert? (*ser*)

Note: *fui* and *fue* have no accents (one syllable).

</Section>

<Section id="strong" kind="grammar" title="The strong preterites">

A group of very common verbs has a new stem in the preterite and a special set of endings, **without any written accents**:

| Infinitive | Preterite stem | yo | él, ella, usted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| tener | **tuv-** | <Say lang="es">tuve</Say> | <Say lang="es">tuvo</Say> |
| estar | **estuv-** | <Say lang="es">estuve</Say> | <Say lang="es">estuvo</Say> |
| poder | **pud-** | <Say lang="es">pude</Say> | <Say lang="es">pudo</Say> |
| poner | **pus-** | <Say lang="es">puse</Say> | <Say lang="es">puso</Say> |
| hacer | **hic-** | <Say lang="es">hice</Say> | <Say lang="es">hizo</Say> |
| venir | **vin-** | <Say lang="es">vine</Say> | <Say lang="es">vino</Say> |
| decir | **dij-** | <Say lang="es">dije</Say> | <Say lang="es">dijo</Say> |

The endings are the same for all of them:

| | ending | tener | decir |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | -e | tuve | dije |
| tú | -iste | tuviste | dijiste |
| él, ella, usted | -o | tuvo | dijo |
| nosotros, nosotras | -imos | tuvimos | dijimos |
| vosotros, vosotras | -isteis | tuvisteis | dijisteis |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | -ieron (-eron after j) | tuvieron | **dijeron** |

Two details: **hizo** is spelled with *z* to keep the sound (*hico* would say "EE-ko"), and stems ending in **j** drop the *i* in the *ellos* form: *dijeron*, not *dijieron*.

Some examples: <Say lang="es">Tuvimos un problema con el vuelo.</Say> <Say lang="es">Estuve tres días en Quito.</Say> <Say lang="es">No pude dormir en el avión.</Say> <Say lang="es">¿Dónde pusiste las llaves?</Say> <Say lang="es">¿Qué hicisteis el sábado?</Say> <Say lang="es">Mis amigos vinieron al aeropuerto.</Say>

**Tener que.** *Tener que* + infinitive means "to have to": <Say lang="es">Tengo que facturar la maleta.</Say> (I have to check my suitcase.) In the preterite it tells what you were obliged to do: <Say lang="es">Tuve que esperar cinco horas.</Say> (I had to wait five hours.)

### Dar

*Dar* (to give) is short and irregular: it uses the *-er/-ir* endings, without accents: <Say lang="es">di</Say>, <Say lang="es">diste</Say>, <Say lang="es">dio</Say>, <Say lang="es">dimos</Say>, <Say lang="es">disteis</Say>, <Say lang="es">dieron</Say>. *Dimos una vuelta por la playa.* (We went for a walk along the beach.)

</Section>

<Section id="pronouns" kind="grammar" title="Lo, la, los, las: it and them">

A **direct object** is the person or thing that receives the action: *I have **the passport***, *I saw **Laura***. To avoid repeating it, Spanish replaces it with a pronoun that agrees with the noun in gender and number:

| Replaces | Pronoun | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a masculine singular noun | <Say lang="es">lo</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿El pasaporte? Lo tengo yo.</Say> The passport? I've got it. |
| a feminine singular noun | <Say lang="es">la</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿La maleta? No la encuentro.</Say> The suitcase? I can't find it. |
| a masculine plural noun | <Say lang="es">los</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Los billetes? Los compré ayer.</Say> The tickets? I bought them yesterday. |
| a feminine plural noun | <Say lang="es">las</Say> | <Say lang="es">¿Las llaves? Las puse en la mesa.</Say> The keys? I put them on the table. |

They also replace people: <Say lang="es">¿Viste a Laura? Sí, la vi ayer.</Say> (Did you see Laura? Yes, I saw her yesterday.) For "me, you, us", the direct object pronouns are the same as the reflexive ones you know: **me, te, nos, os**. <Say lang="es">¿Me ves?</Say> (Can you see me?)

**Position.** The pronoun goes:

1. **directly before a conjugated verb**, after *no* if there is one: <Say lang="es">Lo tengo.</Say> <Say lang="es">No lo tengo.</Say>
2. **or attached to an infinitive**, when there is one: <Say lang="es">Quiero comprarlo.</Say> = <Say lang="es">Lo quiero comprar.</Say> (I want to buy it.) Both orders are equally correct.

It never goes after a conjugated verb: *~~Tengo lo~~*.

**Lo for ideas.** *Lo* also stands for a whole idea, as in two phrases worth learning now: <Say lang="es">Lo sé.</Say> (I know.) <Say lang="es">No lo sé.</Say> (I don't know.)

<Note>

**Leísmo.** In much of Spain, people use *le* instead of *lo* when the direct object is a man: *A Juan le vi ayer* (I saw Juan yesterday). This use is accepted in standard Spanish for masculine singular people. In Latin America, *lo vi* is the norm. Both are understood everywhere; this course uses *lo*.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="A long way to Cartagena">

Inés, from Barcelona, has just arrived in Cartagena, on the Caribbean coast of Colombia, for a holiday. The morning after, she has a video call with her brother Marc.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marc | <Say lang="es">¡Inés! ¿Qué tal el viaje?</Say> | Inés! How was the trip? |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Uf, fue muy largo. El vuelo de Madrid salió con tres horas de retraso.</Say> | Phew, it was very long. The flight from Madrid left three hours late. |
| Marc | <Say lang="es">¡No! ¿Y qué hiciste en el aeropuerto?</Say> | No! And what did you do at the airport? |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Nada especial. Leí, comí algo y di una vuelta por las tiendas.</Say> | Nothing special. I read, ate something and wandered round the shops. |
| Marc | <Say lang="es">¿Y pudiste tomar el vuelo a Cartagena?</Say> | And were you able to catch the flight to Cartagena? |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">No, lo perdí. Tuve que dormir en Bogotá. La aerolínea puso a todos los pasajeros en un hotel.</Say> | No, I missed it. I had to sleep in Bogotá. The airline put all the passengers in a hotel. |
| Marc | <Say lang="es">¿Y la maleta?</Say> | And your suitcase? |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Ese es el problema: no la tengo. La facturé en Madrid, pero no llegó.</Say> | That's the problem: I haven't got it. I checked it in Madrid, but it didn't arrive. |
| Marc | <Say lang="es">¿Y el pasaporte? ¿Lo tienes?</Say> | And your passport? Have you got it? |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Sí, sí, lo tengo. Fui a la oficina de equipajes y me dijeron que la maleta viene hoy.</Say> | Yes, yes, I've got it. I went to the baggage office and they told me the suitcase is coming today. |
| Marc | <Say lang="es">Bueno. ¿Y Cartagena?</Say> | Fine. And Cartagena? |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">¡Preciosa! Ayer vino mi amiga Laura e hicimos un tour por la ciudad amurallada.</Say> | Gorgeous! Yesterday my friend Laura came and we did a tour of the walled city. |
| Marc | <Say lang="es">¿Hiciste fotos?</Say> | Did you take photos? |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">¡Muchísimas! ¿Quieres verlas?</Say> | Loads! Do you want to see them? |

**Notes.** *Salió* is regular (*salir* is irregular only in the present *yo* form, *salgo*). *Pudiste* (*poder*) in the preterite means "managed to, were able to". *E hicimos*: *y* (and) becomes *e* before a word beginning with an *i* sound, including *hi-*. *Me dijeron* ("they told me") contains *me* as an indirect object, which you'll study in <LessonLink slug="plans-and-future">lesson 10</LessonLink>. *Muchísimas* means "very many"; the ending *-ísimo* comes in lesson 12. And *verlas*: the pronoun *las* (the photos) is attached to the infinitive.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Where the stress falls in the past">

Regular preterites are stressed on the ending, which is why they carry accents: *hablé, comí, llegó*. Strong preterites are stressed on the **stem**, so they need no accent: <Say lang="es">tuve</Say> (TU-ve), <Say lang="es">hizo</Say> (I-zo), <Say lang="es">pude</Say> (PU-de), <Say lang="es">vino</Say> (VI-no). Compare <Say lang="es">comió</Say> (co-MIÓ) with <Say lang="es">vino</Say> (VI-no): the different stress is a good clue to which kind of verb you're hearing.

*Vino* is also the word for wine, with the same pronunciation. Context makes it easy: *Vino Laura* (Laura came), *un vino tinto* (a red wine).

Watch the silent *h* in *hice, hizo, hicimos*: "I-se", "I-so". And in *dije*, *dijo*, *dijeron*, the *j* is the breathy throat sound.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Travelling the Spanish-speaking world">

Travel vocabulary changes from country to country more than almost any other area: the same bus is an *autobús* in Madrid, a *camión* in Mexico City, a *colectivo* in Buenos Aires and a *guagua* in Havana or Las Palmas. Don't worry about learning them all; people understand the general words, and you'll pick up the local ones quickly.

The ways of travelling differ too. Spain has a large high-speed rail network linking its main cities. In much of Latin America, long-distance buses are the backbone of travel, and on overnight routes in countries such as Argentina, Chile and Peru you can often choose seats that recline almost flat, called *semicama* and *cama*.

One of the oldest journeys in the Spanish-speaking world is made on foot. For over a thousand years, pilgrims have walked the Camino de Santiago to the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela, in north-west Spain, where tradition places the tomb of the apostle James. Today people of many faiths and none walk it, for prayer, for reflection, for the landscape or for the company, greeting each other along the way with *¡Buen camino!*

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"*Tuvé*, *hizó*: preterites always have an accent." Only the regular ones (*hablé, comió*). Strong preterites are stressed on the stem and have no accent: **tuve, hizo, estuve, dijo**. *Fui, fue, di, dio, vi, vio* have no accent either.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Fui* always means 'I went'." It can also mean "I was": *Fui profesora diez años* (I was a teacher for ten years). Look at what follows.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Dijieron*, *trajieron*." After the *j* of a strong stem, the *ellos* ending is *-eron*: **dijeron**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Tengo lo*, like English 'I have it'." Object pronouns go before a conjugated verb: **Lo tengo**. They can only follow a verb when it's an infinitive (or, as you'll see in lesson 11, a command): *Quiero tenerlo*.

</Misconception>

</Section>
