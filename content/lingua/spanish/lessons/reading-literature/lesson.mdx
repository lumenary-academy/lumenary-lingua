<Section id="figures" kind="grammar" title="Figures of speech">

Spanish rhetoric inherited its terms from Latin and Greek, so most figures have English cousins. What changes is how often you meet them: Spanish prose, not only poetry, uses them freely.

| Figure | What it does | Example (written for this lesson) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *metáfora* | identifies one thing with another | *La luna era una moneda olvidada.* |
| *símil* | compares with *como*, *cual*, *igual que* | *Terco como un viejo.* |
| *personificación* | gives human traits to things | *La ciudad dormía con un ojo abierto.* |
| *hipérbole* | exaggerates | *Mil ventanas apagadas.* |
| *antítesis* | sets opposites side by side | *Mil ventanas apagadas; en una sola, luz.* |
| *paradoja* | an apparent contradiction that holds a truth | *Lo había perdido todo y se sentía rica.* |
| *anáfora* | repeats a word at the start of units | *Silencio de piedra, silencio de agua, silencio de espera.* |
| *hipérbaton* | alters the normal word order | *De la noche en el bolsillo.* |
| *epíteto* | adds an adjective that is already implied | *La fría nieve, el verde prado.* |
| *aliteración* | repeats sounds | *El susurro sordo de las sábanas.* |
| *apóstrofe* | addresses someone or something absent | *¡Oh noche, no te vayas todavía!* |

Read this short passage, written for this lesson, and see how many figures you can find before reading the notes below it.

> La ciudad dormía con un ojo abierto. Sobre los tejados, la luna era una moneda olvidada en el bolsillo de la noche, y el río, terco como un viejo, repetía su única palabra. En la plaza, mil ventanas apagadas; en una sola, la luz amarilla de quien no puede dormir. Silencio de piedra, silencio de agua, silencio de espera. Y Lucía, que lo había perdido todo, se sintió aquella noche, por primera vez, rica.

<Example title="The figures in the passage">

- *La ciudad dormía con un ojo abierto*: personification (and an idiom: *dormir con un ojo abierto*, to sleep lightly).
- *la luna era una moneda olvidada en el bolsillo de la noche*: a metaphor inside a metaphor — the moon is a coin, the night has a pocket.
- *terco como un viejo*: simile; *repetía su única palabra*: personification of the river's sound.
- *mil ventanas apagadas; en una sola…*: hyperbole and antithesis (a thousand dark, one lit).
- *Silencio de piedra, silencio de agua, silencio de espera*: anaphora in three parts.
- *lo había perdido todo… rica*: paradox — the last word overturns the sentence.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="metre" kind="pronunciation" title="Counting syllables: metre and rhyme">

Spanish verse is measured by **syllables**, not by stresses as in English. To count, read the line aloud and apply three rules.

1. **Count spoken syllables**, not written ones. *Hombre* has two (*hom-bre*); *poesía* four (*po-e-sí-a*).
2. **Sinalefa.** When one word ends in a vowel and the next begins with a vowel (or *h* + vowel), the two vowels usually merge into one syllable: *la princesa está* is *la-prin-ce-sa es-tá*, with *sa es* counting as one.
3. **The ending.** The count is adjusted by the stress of the last word: if it is *llana* (stressed on the penultimate: *sincero*), the count stands; if it is *aguda* (stressed on the last: *volver*), add one; if it is *esdrújula* (stressed on the antepenultimate: *pálido*), subtract one.

Rule 3 exists so that lines with different endings "sound" the same length: every line, in effect, is measured up to its last stressed syllable plus one.

### The commonest metres

- **Octosílabo** (8): the metre of the *romance* (ballad), of folk songs and of popular poetry — Spanish falls into eight syllables almost by itself.
- **Endecasílabo** (11): brought from Italy in the sixteenth century; the line of the sonnet and of the grandest poetry (see <LessonLink slug="golden-age-spanish">The language of the Golden Age</LessonLink>).
- **Alejandrino** (14): two halves of seven, with a pause (*cesura*) in the middle; revived by the Modernists at the end of the nineteenth century.

### Rhyme

- **Rima consonante**: every sound matches from the last stressed vowel: *sincero / quiero*, *palma / alma*.
- **Rima asonante**: only the vowels match from the last stressed vowel: *casa / alma* (á-a), *colgar / llamarán* (á). Assonance is everywhere in Spanish ballads and songs, and it is the rhyme an English ear most easily misses.

<Example title="Counting a line step by step">

*la-prin-ce-sa es-tá-tris-te* → sinalefa *sa es* → 7 syllables; *triste* is *llana*, so the count stays at **7**.

*ya-te-vas-pa-ra-no-vol-ver* → 8 spoken syllables; *volver* is *aguda*, so add one → **9**.

</Example>

</Section>

<Section id="three-poets" kind="reading" title="Three poets, a line or two each">

What follows are short quotations, a line or two from each work, from three nineteenth-century poets whose work is in the public domain. Read each one aloud, then read the gloss. The full texts are free at the [Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes](https://www.cervantesvirtual.com/) and [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/).

### Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer (Seville, 1836–1870): *Rimas*

Bécquer's *Rimas*, published after his early death, are short, intimate poems of love, loss and the mystery of poetry itself. Their language is simple; their music is in assonance and in the half-said. The best known opens:

> *Volverán las oscuras golondrinas*
> *en tu balcón sus nidos a colgar*
>
> — Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer, *Rimas*, LIII

**Gloss.** In everyday order: *Las oscuras golondrinas volverán a colgar sus nidos en tu balcón* — "The dark swallows will come back to hang their nests on your balcony." Notice:

- **Hipérbaton**: the verb *volverán* is separated from its *a colgar* by a whole line, and the object *sus nidos* comes before the infinitive. Restoring the order reveals a periphrasis you know from <LessonLink slug="nuances-of-mood-and-aspect">Nuances of mood and aspect</LessonLink>: *volver a* + infinitive, "to do again".
- **Epíteto**: *oscuras* golondrinas — swallows *are* dark; the adjective before the noun adds atmosphere, not information.
- **Metre**: *vol-ve-rán-las-os-cu-ras-go-lon-dri-nas* = 11 (*llana*). *en-tu-bal-cón-sus-ni-dos-a-col-gar* = 10 spoken, *aguda*, so 11. Two hendecasyllables.

The poem goes on to say that the swallows will return, but not *those* swallows — not the ones that saw the lovers' happiness. Another Rima ends with a line that every Spanish schoolchild knows, the poet's answer to someone asking him what poetry is:

> *Poesía... eres tú.*
>
> — Bécquer, *Rimas*, XXI

### José Martí (Havana, 1853–1895): *Versos sencillos* (1891)

Martí was a poet, journalist and the leading figure of Cuba's struggle for independence; he died in battle. His *Versos sencillos* are, as the title says, "simple verses": octosyllables in the rhythm of popular song. The first poem begins:

> *Yo soy un hombre sincero*
> *de donde crece la palma*
>
> — José Martí, *Versos sencillos*, I

**Gloss.** "I am a sincere man from where the palm tree grows" — a land named by its emblematic tree rather than by its name, which most readers take to be Cuba (naming something by a description is a *circunlocución*). Count: *yo-soy-un-hom-bre-sin-ce-ro* = 8; *de-don-de-cre-ce-la-pal-ma* = 8. Both lines are *llanas*, both octosyllables. *Sincero* rhymes in full with a later *quiero*, and *palma* with *alma*. Lines from this poem were later adapted into one of the most famous songs in Spanish — proof of how naturally the octosyllable sings.

### Rubén Darío (Nicaragua, 1867–1916): *Prosas profanas* (1896) and *Cantos de vida y esperanza* (1905)

Darío led **Modernismo**, the movement that renewed poetry in Spanish with music, colour and exotic imagery — swans, princesses, blue and gold. His poem "Sonatina" begins:

> *La princesa está triste... ¿qué tendrá la princesa?*
>
> — Rubén Darío, "Sonatina", *Prosas profanas*

**Gloss.** "The princess is sad… what can be the matter with the princess?" *¿Qué tendrá?* is the **future of probability**: not "what will she have?" but a guess about the present. Count: *la-prin-ce-sa es-tá-tris-te* (7) + *qué-ten-drá-la-prin-ce-sa* (7) = an **alejandrino** of 14, split by a pause. The line begins and ends with the same words, *la princesa*, framing her sadness.

And from "Canción de otoño en primavera", a poem on lost youth:

> *Juventud, divino tesoro,*
> *¡ya te vas para no volver!*
>
> — Rubén Darío, "Canción de otoño en primavera", *Cantos de vida y esperanza*

**Gloss.** "Youth, divine treasure, you are leaving, never to return!" The speaker addresses youth directly (**apóstrofe**) and calls it a treasure (**metáfora**). Both lines have nine syllables: the first is *llana* (9), the second has eight spoken syllables and ends in the *aguda* *volver*, so it counts as 9 too.

</Section>

<Section id="literary-grammar" kind="grammar" title="The grammar of literary Spanish">

Most literary Spanish uses the grammar you know. A few features, though, are rare in conversation and common on the page.

- **Free word order.** Poets and many prose writers move objects, adverbs and infinitives around (*hipérbaton*). Your first move with a difficult sentence is to find the verb and its subject, then put everything back in neutral order.
- **The adjective before the noun.** In everyday speech most adjectives follow the noun; in literary style many go before it, adding colour or emotion: *la callada noche*, *un pálido reflejo*.
- **The *-ra* form as a past tense.** In literary and journalistic prose, *-ra* forms can replace the pluperfect indicative: *la casa que **fuera** de su abuelo* = *que **había sido** de su abuelo*. This is the original Latin meaning of the form, kept alive in writing.
- **Pronouns after the verb.** Older texts, and some modern writers imitating them, attach object pronouns to conjugated verbs: ***díjole*** (*le dijo*), ***sentose*** (*se sentó*). You will meet many more in the Golden Age lesson.
- **The future and conditional of probability**, as in Darío's *¿qué tendrá?*: a guess, not a prediction.
- **Literary vocabulary.** Poetry keeps words that speech has dropped or never used: *el céfiro* (breeze), *el ósculo* (kiss), *la faz* (face), *do* (where). The DLE labels them *poét.* or *lit.*

</Section>

<Section id="map" kind="culture" title="A reader's map of Spanish literature">

You do not need to read in order, but it helps to know where a book sits.

- **The Middle Ages and the Golden Age** (to the late seventeenth century): the epic *Cantar de mio Cid*, then Garcilaso, Cervantes, Lope de Vega, Quevedo and, in New Spain, Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz. Their Spanish needs some help; see <LessonLink slug="golden-age-spanish">The language of the Golden Age</LessonLink>.
- **Romanticism** (mid-nineteenth century): Bécquer, and Rosalía de Castro, who wrote in Galician (*Cantares gallegos*, 1863) and in Spanish (*En las orillas del Sar*, 1884).
- **Realism**: Benito Pérez Galdós, whose novels such as *Fortunata y Jacinta* (1887) paint Madrid in every social layer — long, but in modern, readable Spanish.
- **Modernismo** (from the 1880s): Martí, Darío and many others across Latin America — the first literary movement born in the Americas that changed the literature of Spain.
- **The twentieth century and after**: the Generation of '27 in Spain, the great Latin American poets and the novelists of the "Boom". Much of this work is still under copyright: read it in published editions or from your library.

### How to read a literary text in Spanish

1. **Read it once without a dictionary**, for the shape and the mood.
2. **Read it aloud.** Poetry is sound; the metre and rhyme will often show you where a phrase begins and ends.
3. **Restore the word order** of any sentence that confuses you: find the verb, then the subject.
4. **Ask who is speaking to whom**: the *yo lírico* and the *tú* are often the key.
5. **Look up only the words that block meaning**, in the DLE, and note the labels.
6. **Use annotated editions** (*ediciones anotadas*) when you can: Spanish school editions gloss archaic and regional words at the foot of the page.

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common misconceptions">

<Misconception>

**"Spanish metre counts written syllables."** It counts *spoken* syllables, with sinalefa merging vowels between words and an adjustment for the stress of the last word. *Volver* at the end of a line is worth three, not two.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A poem that doesn't rhyme fully doesn't rhyme."** Much Spanish poetry rhymes in assonance — only the vowels — and native readers hear it at once. *Colgar* and *llamarán* rhyme for a Spanish ear.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"*Verso* means a verse."** *Un verso* is one line. A group of lines is *una estrofa*. "The second verse of the song" is *la segunda estrofa*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Literary Spanish is old Spanish."** Darío and Martí wrote modern Spanish, and so did Galdós. What makes their language harder is not age but density: figures, inversions and a richer vocabulary — exactly the things this lesson trains you to see.

</Misconception>

</Section>
