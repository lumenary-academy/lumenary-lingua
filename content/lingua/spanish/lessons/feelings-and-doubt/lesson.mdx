<Section id="emotions" kind="grammar" title="The subjunctive after emotions">

When the main clause expresses a **feeling about** something, the verb after *que* goes into the subjunctive, even when the thing is a plain fact. The feeling, not the fact, is the point of the sentence.

- <Say lang="es">Me alegra que estés aquí.</Say> — I'm glad you're here. (You *are* here; the subjunctive marks my reaction.)
- <Say lang="es">Siento que no puedas venir.</Say> — I'm sorry you can't come.
- <Say lang="es">Me molesta que la gente hable por teléfono en el cine.</Say> — It bothers me when people talk on the phone at the cinema.
- <Say lang="es">A mis abuelos les preocupa que viajemos solos.</Say> — My grandparents are worried about us travelling alone.
- <Say lang="es">¡Qué pena que se vaya Camila!</Say> — What a shame Camila is leaving!

The emotional expressions come in four shapes, all followed by *que* + subjunctive:

| Shape | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Verbs like *gustar* | *me alegra, me molesta, me preocupa, me sorprende, me encanta, me da miedo, me da pena* |
| Reflexive verb + *de que* | *me alegro de que, me quejo de que* |
| *estar* + adjective + *de que* | *estoy contento de que, estoy harta de que* |
| *ser* / *qué* + noun or adjective | *es una pena que, es raro que, es normal que, qué bien que, qué lástima que* |

As in the last lesson, if the person who feels and the person who acts are the same, use the infinitive:

| Same person: infinitive | Different person: que + subjunctive |
| --- | --- |
| *Siento llegar tarde.* I'm sorry I'm late. | *Siento que llegues tarde.* I'm sorry you're late. |
| *Me alegra estar aquí.* I'm glad to be here. | *Me alegra que estés aquí.* I'm glad you're here. |

Notice the small difference between **me alegra que** (like *gustar*, no *de*) and **me alegro de que** (reflexive *alegrarse de*). Both are correct; mixing them (*me alegro que*) is common in speech but avoided in careful Spanish.

</Section>

<Section id="doubt" kind="grammar" title="Believing, doubting and denying">

Here the subjunctive follows a simple logic. If you **assert** something as true (you believe it, you are sure of it), use the **indicative**. If you **doubt** or **deny** it, use the **subjunctive**.

| Indicative: asserted | Subjunctive: doubted or denied |
| --- | --- |
| *Creo que Ana tiene razón.* | *No creo que Ana tenga razón.* |
| *Pienso que es buena idea.* | *No pienso que sea buena idea.* |
| *Estoy seguro de que vienen.* | *No estoy seguro de que vengan.* |
| *Es verdad que hace frío.* | *No es verdad que haga frío.* |
| *Es evidente que está cansado.* | *Dudo que esté cansado.* |
| *Me parece que llueve.* | *No me parece que llueva.* |

<Example title="From belief to doubt in three steps">

Start with a belief: *Creo que el museo abre los lunes.* (indicative: I'm asserting it)

1. Negate the main verb: *No creo que…*
2. Keep *que*, and put the verb after it in the subjunctive. *Abrir* is an *-ir* verb, so *abre* becomes *abra*.
3. Result: <Say lang="es">No creo que el museo abra los lunes.</Say>

The same steps turn *Es verdad que tiene coche* into *No es verdad que **tenga** coche*, and *Estoy segura de que lo sabe* into *No estoy segura de que lo **sepa***. Going the other way, *Dudo que venga* becomes *Estoy seguro de que **viene***.

</Example>

Possibility and probability count as "not asserted" too: <Say lang="es">Es posible que lleguemos tarde.</Say> <Say lang="es">Es probable que llueva.</Say> <Say lang="es">Puede que tengas razón.</Say>

<Note>

In questions, *¿Crees que…?* usually takes the indicative (*¿Crees que va a llover?*), because the speaker is just asking. You may also hear the subjunctive (*¿Crees que llueva?*) when the speaker leans towards doubt; this is frequent in parts of Latin America. *No dudo que* asserts, so it normally takes the indicative: *No dudo que es verdad.*

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="ojala-and-perhaps" kind="grammar" title="Ojalá, quizás, tal vez and a lo mejor">

### Ojalá

**Ojalá** means "I hope" or "let's hope", and it is always followed by the subjunctive. The *que* after it is optional:

- <Say lang="es">Ojalá haga sol mañana.</Say> — I hope it's sunny tomorrow.
- <Say lang="es">Ojalá que no llueva en la boda.</Say> — Let's hope it doesn't rain at the wedding.
- <Say lang="es">¿Vienes el sábado? —¡Ojalá!</Say> — Are you coming on Saturday? —I hope so!

With the present subjunctive, *ojalá* wishes for something that is still possible. In <LessonLink slug="if-i-were">If I were…</LessonLink> you will use it with the imperfect subjunctive for wishes that are unlikely or impossible (*ojalá tuviera más tiempo*).

### Perhaps: four words, three behaviours

| Word | Mood | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *quizás, quizá, tal vez* | Either. Subjunctive adds doubt; indicative sounds more likely. | *Quizás viene mañana.* / *Quizás venga mañana.* |
| *a lo mejor* | Indicative | *A lo mejor viene mañana.* |
| *puede que, es posible que* | Subjunctive | *Puede que venga mañana.* |

With *quizás* and *tal vez*, both moods are correct and the difference is a matter of degree. *Tal vez está en casa* sounds like a reasonable guess; *tal vez esté en casa* leaves the question more open. If the word comes **after** the verb, use the indicative: *Está en casa, tal vez.*

*A lo mejor* is the most common in relaxed conversation, and it takes the indicative: *A lo mejor nos vemos en la fiesta.*

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="News from Camila">

**In a shared flat in Bogotá, Colombia.** Inés and Diego have just heard that their friend Camila is moving to Canada. They use *tú*.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">¿Ya sabes lo de Camila? Se va a vivir a Montreal en marzo.</Say> | Have you heard about Camila? She's moving to Montreal in March. |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">Sí, me lo contó ayer. Me alegra mucho que tenga esa oportunidad… pero qué pena que se vaya tan lejos.</Say> | Yes, she told me yesterday. I'm really glad she's got that opportunity… but what a shame she's going so far away. |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">A mí me preocupa que no conozca a nadie allá.</Say> | I'm worried she doesn't know anyone there. |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">No creo que eso sea un problema. Camila hace amigos en todas partes.</Say> | I don't think that'll be a problem. Camila makes friends everywhere. |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Es verdad. Pero dicen que los inviernos son durísimos. Dudo que esté preparada para veinte grados bajo cero.</Say> | True. But they say the winters are really tough. I doubt she's ready for twenty below zero. |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">Ella dice que ya se compró un abrigo enorme. Creo que está más emocionada que nerviosa.</Say> | She says she's already bought a huge coat. I think she's more excited than nervous. |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Ojalá que le vaya muy bien. ¿Le hacemos una despedida?</Say> | I really hope it goes well for her. Shall we throw her a farewell party? |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">¡Claro! A lo mejor la podemos hacer aquí, el último sábado de febrero.</Say> | Of course! Maybe we can have it here, on the last Saturday in February. |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Quizás sea mejor en casa de sus papás. Aquí no cabemos todos.</Say> | Perhaps it would be better at her parents' house. We won't all fit in here. |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">Tienes razón. Es posible que vengan cuarenta personas.</Say> | You're right. Forty people might come. |
| Inés | <Say lang="es">Me encanta que la gente la quiera tanto. Y me molesta un poco ser la última en saberlo.</Say> | I love that people care about her so much. And it annoys me a bit to be the last to know. |
| Diego | <Say lang="es">¡Ja! Siento que te enteres así.</Say> | Ha! Sorry you're finding out like this. |

Count the triggers: emotions (*me alegra que, qué pena que, me preocupa que, me encanta que, siento que*), doubt and denial (*no creo que, dudo que, es posible que*), *ojalá*, and *quizás* with the subjunctive. Against them, the asserted beliefs take the indicative: *creo que está, dicen que son*. And *a lo mejor la podemos hacer* keeps the indicative, as *a lo mejor* always does.

<Note>

Inés says *me molesta ser la última en saberlo* with an infinitive, because the person annoyed and the person who is last are the same: her. *Allá* (over there) is preferred to *allí* in much of Latin America, and *papás* is the usual word for "parents" in Colombia and many other countries, where Spain says *padres*.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Linking words together">

Spanish speakers don't pause between words that end and begin with a vowel; they glide them into one syllable. This linking, called **sinalefa**, is why fast Spanish can sound like one long word, and why phrases from this lesson may be hard to catch at first.

| Written | Heard roughly as |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">me alegra que estés aquí</Say> | *mea-le-gra-quees-té-sa-quí* |
| <Say lang="es">no creo que esté en casa</Say> | *no-cre-o-quees-téen-ca-sa* |
| <Say lang="es">ojalá haga sol</Say> | *o-ja-lá-ga-sol* (the *h* is silent) |
| <Say lang="es">tal vez esté allí</Say> | *tal-ve-ses-téa-llí* |

Notice two more things in the last example. A consonant at the end of a word attaches itself to the vowel of the next (*vez esté → ve-ses-té*), and *tal vez* is pronounced as one unit, *talbés* or *talbéz*. In most of Spain the *z* sounds like English *th*; elsewhere it is an *s*.

Two stress points: **ojalá** and **quizás** are stressed on the last syllable (*o-ja-LÁ, qui-ZÁS*), as their written accents show. Practise *ojalá* slowly, then in a phrase: <Say lang="es">¡Ojalá!</Say> <Say lang="es">Ojalá que sí.</Say>

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Ojalá: a word with a long history">

*Ojalá* is one of the many words Spanish owes to Arabic, spoken in much of the Iberian Peninsula for centuries during the Middle Ages. The Spanish academies' dictionary traces it to an Andalusi Arabic expression meaning, roughly, "if God wills it". Over time it lost its religious sense for most speakers and became an ordinary way to say "I hope", used by believers and non-believers alike. Other everyday words from Arabic include *aceite* (oil), *azúcar* (sugar), *almohada* (pillow), *alcalde* (mayor) and *hasta* (until).

Feelings also travel differently between countries. *Me da pena* means "it makes me sad" or "I feel sorry" in Spain and much of South America, but in Mexico and several other countries it often means "I'm embarrassed": *Me da pena hablar en público*. Similarly, *¡Qué pena!* can be "What a shame!" or, in those countries, "How embarrassing!" Context usually makes it clear, and asking *¿En qué sentido?* (In what sense?) is always fine.

Finally, Spanish speakers often express sympathy with a set phrase built on *sentir*: *Lo siento mucho* (I'm very sorry), or, at a funeral, *Te acompaño en el sentimiento* or *Mi más sentido pésame* (my deepest condolences).

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mistakes">

<Misconception>

**"Creo que sea…"** *Creer que* asserts what you believe, so it takes the indicative: **Creo que es** una buena idea. Only the negative, *no creo que*, takes the subjunctive.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"The subjunctive is for things that aren't true."** After emotions, the subjunctive is used even for facts: *Me alegra que **estés** aquí* is said to someone who is clearly here. What triggers it is the reaction, not doubt about reality.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"A lo mejor venga."** *A lo mejor* takes the indicative: **A lo mejor viene.** If you want the subjunctive, use *quizás*, *tal vez* or *puede que*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Ojalá + indicative."** *Ojalá* never takes the indicative: not *ojalá vienes* but **ojalá vengas**.

</Misconception>

</Section>
