<Section id="vocabulary" kind="vocabulary" title="Words for science and the environment">

Scientific and technical Spanish is full of sentences in which the agent doesn't matter or isn't known: *the samples were analysed*, *Spanish is spoken here*, *they say it'll rain*. English often reaches for the passive with *to be*. Spanish has that too, but uses it much less, and prefers a set of structures with **se**. This lesson sorts them out, using the vocabulary of science and the environment.

<Section id="ser-passive" kind="grammar" title="The passive with ser">

The Spanish passive is built like the English one: **ser** + **participle**, with the agent introduced by **por**. The participle agrees with the subject in gender and number.

- <Say lang="es">La penicilina fue descubierta por Alexander Fleming.</Say> — Penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming.
- <Say lang="es">Las muestras serán analizadas en Bogotá.</Say> — The samples will be analysed in Bogotá.
- <Say lang="es">El puente fue construido en el siglo XIX.</Say> — The bridge was built in the 19th century.

It appears mostly in **formal writing**: news, history, science, official texts. In conversation it sounds stiff, and Spanish speakers use an active sentence or *se* instead. It's also used mostly in the preterite and the future; *la puerta es abierta* in the present sounds odd.

Don't confuse it with **estar** + participle, which describes the resulting **state**:

- *La tienda **fue cerrada** por la policía.* — The shop was closed by the police (an action).
- *La tienda **estaba cerrada**.* — The shop was closed (its state).

One English passive Spanish can't copy: an **indirect object** can't become the subject. *I was given a prize* is **not** *fui dado un premio*. Say ***Me dieron un premio*** (they gave me a prize), or formally *Se me concedió un premio*.

</Section>

<Section id="se" kind="grammar" title="Se: the pasiva refleja and impersonal se">

### The pasiva refleja

The most common Spanish equivalent of the English passive is **se** + a verb in the third person, **agreeing with the thing** affected:

- <Say lang="es">Se vende piso.</Say> — Flat for sale.
- <Say lang="es">Se venden casas.</Say> — Houses for sale.
- <Say lang="es">Se analizaron las muestras en el laboratorio.</Say> — The samples were analysed in the laboratory.
- <Say lang="es">Se necesitan voluntarios.</Say> — Volunteers needed.
- <Say lang="es">En la reunión se tomaron tres decisiones.</Say> — Three decisions were taken at the meeting.

The thing affected works as the grammatical subject, so a plural noun needs a plural verb: *se venden casas*, *se observaron cambios*. It's the backbone of scientific writing in Spanish: *Se tomaron muestras de agua de diez ríos. Se midió la temperatura cada hora. Se observó un aumento de…*

Normally the pasiva refleja doesn't mention an agent with *por*. If you need to say who did it, use the *ser* passive or an active sentence.

### Impersonal se

Spanish also uses **se** + a verb in the **third person singular** to talk about people in general, with no subject at all. This happens with verbs that have no direct object:

- <Say lang="es">En este barrio se vive muy bien.</Say> — People live well in this neighbourhood.
- <Say lang="es">Aquí se come muy bien.</Say> — The food's very good here (one eats well).
- <Say lang="es">¿Por dónde se va a la estación?</Say> — How do you get to the station?

And with people introduced by the personal ***a***. The *a* makes the person an object, so the verb stays **singular**:

- <Say lang="es">Se entrevistó a doscientas personas.</Say> — Two hundred people were interviewed.
- <Say lang="es">Se busca a los responsables.</Say> — Those responsible are being sought.

<Note>

On signs you will often see *Se vende pisos* or *Se alquila habitaciones*, with a singular verb and a plural noun. The academies consider the plural, ***se venden pisos***, the correct form, and that's what careful writing uses. The reverse mistake, *se entrevistaron a doscientas personas*, is also common; with personal *a*, keep the verb singular.

</Note>

</Section>

<Section id="other-impersonals" kind="grammar" title="Uno, they and you: other impersonal structures">

### Uno, una

**Uno** (or **una**, for a woman speaking about herself) means "one" or "you" in general, and usually includes the speaker. It is essential with **reflexive verbs**, where *se* + *se* is impossible:

- <Say lang="es">Uno se acostumbra a todo.</Say> — You get used to anything.
- <Say lang="es">Cuando una tiene hijos, duerme menos.</Say> — When you have children, you sleep less.

### The third person plural

A verb in the **third person plural**, with no subject, refers to unknown or unspecified people, and never includes the speaker:

- <Say lang="es">Dicen que va a llover.</Say> — They say it's going to rain.
- <Say lang="es">Me robaron la bici.</Say> — My bike was stolen (someone stole it).
- <Say lang="es">Llaman a la puerta.</Say> — Someone's at the door.

### Generic tú

In conversation, as in English, *tú* is often used for anyone: *Cuando estudias de noche, te cansas más* (When you study at night, you get more tired).

### Choosing the right one

| Structure | Feel | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *ser* + participle | Formal, written; agent can be named | *El edificio fue diseñado por una arquitecta mexicana.* |
| Pasiva refleja | Neutral, very common; no agent | *Se diseñó un edificio nuevo.* |
| Impersonal *se* | People in general | *Se trabaja mejor por la mañana.* |
| *uno / una* | General, includes the speaker; works with reflexive verbs | *Uno se cansa de esperar.* |
| Third person plural | Someone, unknown people | *Han abierto un museo nuevo.* |
| Generic *tú* | Conversational | *Si comes bien, te sientes mejor.* |

</Section>

<Section id="reading" kind="reading" title="Reading: Agua que viene de la niebla">

**A popular-science text, written for this lesson.**

> <Say lang="es">En la costa del norte de Chile y del sur de Perú casi nunca llueve: es una de las regiones más secas del planeta. Sin embargo, muchas mañanas el paisaje desaparece bajo una niebla espesa que llega desde el océano Pacífico. En Chile se la conoce como camanchaca; en Lima, la humedad gris del invierno se llama garúa.</Say>
>
> <Say lang="es">Durante siglos, esa niebla fue vista como parte del paisaje y poco más. Las plantas, en cambio, la aprovechaban. En las lomas, colinas cercanas al mar, crece cada invierno una vegetación sorprendente que vive casi solo de las gotas que se quedan atrapadas en sus hojas.</Say>
>
> <Say lang="es">A finales del siglo XX, varios equipos de investigadores se preguntaron si se podría imitar a esas plantas. Así nacieron los atrapanieblas: grandes redes de malla fina que se colocan en vertical, en lo alto de los cerros y de cara al viento. Cuando la niebla atraviesa la malla, las gotas microscópicas chocan con los hilos, se unen y forman gotas más grandes, que resbalan hasta una canaleta. Desde allí, el agua se conduce por tubos hasta un depósito.</Say>
>
> <Say lang="es">Cuando se instalaron las primeras redes, en algunos pueblos las miraban con desconfianza. Hoy se usan atrapanieblas en comunidades de Chile, de Perú y de otros países con costas desérticas. Con esa agua se riegan huertos, se plantan árboles y, en algunos lugares, se abastece a familias que antes dependían de camiones cisterna.</Say>
>
> <Say lang="es">Pero uno no debe imaginar una solución mágica. La cantidad de agua depende de la estación, del viento y de la altura, y las redes tienen que ser reparadas con frecuencia. La niebla no sustituye a otras fuentes de agua: las complementa. Aun así, la idea tiene algo de poético. En uno de los lugares más secos del mundo, el agua se recoge del aire.</Say>

| Word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *espeso, espesa* | thick |
| *la camanchaca* | Chilean name for the thick coastal fog |
| *la garúa* | fine mist or drizzle, typical of Lima's winter |
| *aprovechar* | to make use of |
| *las lomas* | coastal hills that turn green in the foggy season |
| *la gota* | drop |
| *atrapado* | trapped, caught |
| *el atrapanieblas* | fog catcher (same form in the plural) |
| *la malla* | mesh |
| *de cara al viento* | facing the wind |
| *atravesar* | to pass through |
| *resbalar* | to slide, to run down |
| *la canaleta* | gutter, channel |
| *el depósito* | tank |
| *la desconfianza* | distrust |
| *regar* | to water (plants) |
| *el huerto* | vegetable garden |
| *abastecer* | to supply |
| *el camión cisterna* | water tanker (truck) |

**Things to notice.** The *ser* passive appears twice, in a formal register: *fue vista*, *tienen que ser reparadas*. The pasiva refleja is everywhere, always agreeing with its noun: *se colocan* (redes), *se conduce* (el agua), *se usan* (atrapanieblas), *se riegan huertos, se plantan árboles*. *Se abastece a familias* is impersonal *se* with personal *a*, so it stays singular. *En algunos pueblos las miraban* is the third person plural for unspecified people, and *uno no debe imaginar* is impersonal *uno*.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Scientific words that sound different">

Many scientific words look almost like English, which makes them easy to read and easy to mispronounce. Three things to watch:

**1. The extra *e*.** Spanish words never begin with *s* + consonant, so they gain an *e*: <Say lang="es">especie</Say>, <Say lang="es">estudio</Say>, <Say lang="es">espacio</Say>, <Say lang="es">estrella</Say>, <Say lang="es">estructura</Say>. Pronounce that *e* clearly: *es-PE-cie*, not *spe-cie*. When English speakers drop it, it is one of the most noticeable features of their accent.

**2. Stress in a different place.** The written accent tells you where it falls: <Say lang="es">océano</Say> (*o-CÉ-a-no*), <Say lang="es">análisis</Say> (*a-NÁ-li-sis*), <Say lang="es">hipótesis</Say> (*hi-PÓ-te-sis*), <Say lang="es">química</Say> (*QUÍ-mi-ca*), <Say lang="es">biología</Say> (*bio-lo-GÍ-a*), <Say lang="es">fenómeno</Say> (*fe-NÓ-me-no*).

**3. Ciencia.** There's no *s* before the *c*: <Say lang="es">ciencia</Say>. In most of Spain both *c*s sound like English *th* in *think*; in Latin America, the Canary Islands and parts of Andalusia, they sound like *s*.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Science in Spanish">

Spanish has a long scientific tradition. The Spanish neuroscientist **Santiago Ramón y Cajal** shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1906 for his work on the structure of the nervous system; his drawings of neurons are still admired today. Since then, scientists from Spanish-speaking countries have received Nobel Prizes for, among other things, the physiology of hormones (Bernardo Houssay, Argentina, 1947), the chemistry of sugars (Luis Federico Leloir, Argentina, 1970), antibodies (César Milstein, Argentina, 1984), and the chemistry of the ozone layer (Mario Molina, Mexico, 1995).

Much of today's research is published in English, but Spanish remains an important language of science, especially in medicine, agriculture, ecology and the social sciences. **SciELO**, a network of open-access journals that began in Brazil, publishes thousands of articles in Spanish from Latin America and Spain. And **divulgación científica**, popular-science writing and broadcasting, is a lively field across the Spanish-speaking world, from museums and radio programmes to podcasts and video channels.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mistakes">

<Misconception>

**"Fui dado un libro."** Only a direct object can become the subject of a Spanish passive. Say ***Me dieron un libro***.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Every English passive needs ser."** Spanish prefers the pasiva refleja or an active sentence: *Spanish is spoken here* is ***Aquí se habla español***, not *aquí es hablado español*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Se vende casas."** In the pasiva refleja, the verb agrees with the noun: ***Se venden casas***.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Se se acostumbra."** A reflexive verb can't take impersonal *se* on top of its own *se*. Use *uno*: ***Uno se acostumbra***.

</Misconception>

</Section>
