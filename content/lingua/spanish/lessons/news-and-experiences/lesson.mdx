<Section id="vocabulary" kind="vocabulary" title="Words for the news">

*¿Has visto la noticia?* is how countless conversations begin, and it uses the tense of this lesson, the **present perfect** (*he visto, has visto*). It is the tense of recent news, of things done "today" or "this year", and of experiences in your life so far. It is also the tense where Spain and most of Latin America differ most, so you will learn both habits.

<Section id="present-perfect" kind="grammar" title="The present perfect">

### Formation

The present perfect is the present of *haber* plus a past participle, just as the pluperfect from <LessonLink slug="telling-a-story">Telling a story</LessonLink> was the imperfect of *haber* plus a participle.

| | haber (present) | + participle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yo | he | trabajado |
| tú | has | comido |
| él, ella, usted | ha | vivido |
| nosotros, nosotras | hemos | visto |
| vosotros, vosotras | habéis | hecho |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | han | dicho |

The same three rules apply as for the pluperfect: the participle never changes (*ellas han llegado*), pronouns go before *haber* (*me lo ha dicho*), and nothing goes between the two parts (*ya he comido*, not *he ya comido*).

### Irregular participles

| Infinitive | Participle | Infinitive | Participle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| abrir | abierto | poner | puesto |
| cubrir | cubierto | resolver | resuelto |
| decir | dicho | romper | roto |
| escribir | escrito | ver | visto |
| hacer | hecho | volver | vuelto |
| morir | muerto | | |

Verbs built on these behave the same way: *descubrir → descubierto, describir → descrito, devolver → devuelto, deshacer → deshecho, componer → compuesto, prever → previsto*.

One spelling detail: when the stem ends in *a, e* or *o*, the *-ído* takes an accent to keep the *i* stressed: *leer → leído, oír → oído, traer → traído, creer → creído, caer → caído*. Verbs in *-uir* don't need it: *construido, incluido*.

### Uses

The present perfect connects the past with **now**. It is used for:

- **Life experiences** up to now, often with *alguna vez, nunca, varias veces*: <Say lang="es">¿Alguna vez has comido mole?</Say> <Say lang="es">Nunca he estado en Guatemala.</Say>
- **Things not yet done**, or already done, with *ya, todavía no, aún no*: <Say lang="es">Todavía no he terminado el informe.</Say>
- **Recent events still relevant now**: <Say lang="es">Ha habido un accidente en la autopista.</Say> (There's been an accident on the motorway.)
- **Actions within a period that includes now**: *hoy, esta semana, este año, este mes, últimamente*: <Say lang="es">Este año he leído doce novelas.</Say>

To say you've *been to* a place, Spanish normally uses *estar*: *He estado en Japón* (I've been to Japan). *He ido a Japón* is also correct.

</Section>

<Section id="perfect-or-preterite" kind="grammar" title="Present perfect or preterite? Spain and Latin America">

Both tenses talk about completed actions; the difference is how the speaker frames the time. And here Spanish speakers genuinely differ.

**In most of Spain**, the dividing line is whether the time period is finished:

- Period still open (*hoy, esta mañana, este año*), or very recent → **present perfect**: *Hoy **he desayunado** tarde.*
- Period closed (*ayer, el lunes pasado, en 2019*) → **preterite**: *Ayer **desayuné** tarde.*

**In most of Latin America** (and in the Canary Islands and parts of north-western Spain), the preterite covers far more ground, including *today*: *Hoy **desayuné** tarde.* *¿Viste la noticia?* The present perfect is kept mostly for experiences and for situations that continue up to now, especially with *nunca, todavía no, siempre* or *últimamente*: *Nunca **he estado** en Cuba. Todavía no **ha llegado**.*

| Situation | Typical in Spain | Typical in most of Latin America |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Something done today | *Esta mañana he ido al banco.* | *Esta mañana fui al banco.* |
| Have you seen…? (just now) | *¿Has visto el correo?* | *¿Viste el correo?* |
| Something done yesterday | *Ayer fui al banco.* | *Ayer fui al banco.* |
| Life experience | *¿Has estado en Perú?* | *¿Has estado en Perú?* (also *¿Estuviste…?*) |
| Not yet | *Todavía no ha llegado.* | *Todavía no llega* or *todavía no ha llegado* |

<Example title="Let the time marker decide (the Spanish habit)">

- *Esta semana **he trabajado** mucho.* — *Esta semana* isn't over yet.
- *La semana pasada **trabajé** mucho.* — *La semana pasada* is over.
- *¿**Has estado** alguna vez en Lima?* — Your whole life up to now.
- *¿Cuándo **estuviste** en Lima?* — *¿Cuándo?* asks for a specific point in the past, so even in Spain the preterite is the natural choice.

</Example>

Both systems are standard, and speakers from each side understand each other without trouble. A sensible strategy as a learner is to pick the habit of the variety you hear most, and to recognise the other.

### Ya, todavía, aún and acabar de

- **ya** = already: <Say lang="es">Ya he comprado las entradas.</Say> In questions it means "yet": *¿Ya has comido?*
- **todavía no / aún no** = not yet: <Say lang="es">Aún no he visto la película.</Say>
- **ya no** = no longer: *Ya no trabajo allí.*
- **todavía / aún** (without *no*) = still: *Todavía vive con sus padres.*
- **acabar de** + infinitive = to have just done something, used everywhere: <Say lang="es">Acabo de leer la noticia.</Say> (I've just read the news.)

Note the accent: **aún** (still, yet) has one; **aun** without an accent means "even" (*aun así* = even so).

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="Sixty years late">

**In an office in Madrid.** Carmen, from Madrid, and Rodrigo, from Monterrey in Mexico, are colleagues. Carmen has just read an (invented) news story on her phone. Notice how each speaks.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Rodrigo, ¿has visto la noticia de la biblioteca de Valparaíso?</Say> | Rodrigo, have you seen the story about the library in Valparaíso? |
| Rodrigo | <Say lang="es">No, todavía no. Hoy no leí nada; me levanté tardísimo. ¿Qué pasó?</Say> | No, not yet. I haven't read anything today; I got up really late. What happened? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Pues esta mañana una señora ha devuelto un libro que su padre sacó hace más de sesenta años.</Say> | Well, this morning a woman returned a book her father borrowed more than sixty years ago. |
| Rodrigo | <Say lang="es">¡No me digas! ¿Y le cobraron la multa?</Say> | No way! And did they charge her the fine? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">No. La directora ha dicho que la biblioteca ya ha perdonado todas las multas antiguas. Y han puesto el libro en una vitrina en la entrada.</Say> | No. The director has said the library has already forgiven all old fines. And they've put the book in a display case at the entrance. |
| Rodrigo | <Say lang="es">Qué bonito. ¿Y qué libro era?</Say> | How lovely. And what book was it? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Un libro de poemas. Parece que el padre lo había guardado en una caja y la hija lo encontró cuando se mudó.</Say> | A book of poems. It seems the father had kept it in a box and his daughter found it when she moved house. |
| Rodrigo | <Say lang="es">Yo también he encontrado cosas raras en mudanzas. Una vez encontré una carta de mi abuelo dentro de un libro de cocina.</Say> | I've found strange things when moving too. Once I found a letter from my grandfather inside a cookbook. |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">¿Ah, sí? ¿Y la leíste?</Say> | Oh really? And did you read it? |
| Rodrigo | <Say lang="es">Claro, ese mismo día. Era una carta de amor para mi abuela.</Say> | Of course, that same day. It was a love letter to my grandmother. |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">¡Qué romántico! Oye, ¿tú has estado alguna vez en Valparaíso?</Say> | How romantic! Hey, have you ever been to Valparaíso? |
| Rodrigo | <Say lang="es">No, nunca he ido a Chile. ¿Y tú?</Say> | No, I've never been to Chile. Have you? |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Sí, he estado dos veces. La última vez fue hace cinco años. Es una ciudad preciosa, llena de cerros y de colores.</Say> | Yes, I've been twice. The last time was five years ago. It's a beautiful city, full of hills and colours. |
| Rodrigo | <Say lang="es">Pues ya tengo plan para las próximas vacaciones.</Say> | Well, now I've got a plan for my next holiday. |
| Carmen | <Say lang="es">Y ya sabes qué visitar primero.</Say> | And you already know what to visit first. |

Carmen speaks like most people in Spain: *esta mañana ha devuelto, ha dicho, han puesto* are today's events, so she uses the present perfect; *sacó hace más de sesenta años* and *la última vez fue* are closed periods, so the preterite. Rodrigo speaks like most Mexicans: *hoy no leí, me levanté, ¿qué pasó?* are all today, but he uses the preterite. Both use the present perfect for life experiences: *he encontrado cosas raras, nunca he ido, he estado dos veces*. Carmen's *¿Y la leíste?* is preterite because the reading happened at a specific past time, *una vez*.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="The silent h of haber">

The *h* of *haber* is never pronounced, so its forms sound just like other words:

| Written | Sounds like | Meaning of the other word |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">he</Say> | *e* | "and" before *i-* (*padre e hijo*) |
| <Say lang="es">ha</Say> | *a* | to, at |
| <Say lang="es">has</Say> | *as* | ace |
| <Say lang="es">hecho</Say> | *echo* | I throw (*echar*) |
| <Say lang="es">haber</Say> | *a ver* | let's see |

Context always settles it in speech, but spelling is another matter: even native speakers sometimes write *a ver* when they mean *haber* and the other way round. If it is a verb (*puede haber problemas*, *tiene que haber un error*), it's *haber*; if it means "let's see", it's *a ver*.

In fast speech, *ha* and *he* also link with the following vowel: <Say lang="es">ha empezado</Say> sounds like *aem-pe-za-do*, and <Say lang="es">lo he oído</Say> like *loeo-í-do*. The accent on *oído* keeps the *í* as its own syllable: *o-Í-do*.

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Reading the news in Spanish">

Reading news is one of the best ways to build B1 and B2 vocabulary, because stories repeat the same words for days. Major Spanish-language outlets include public broadcasters such as Spain's RTVE, international services such as BBC News Mundo, and the leading newspapers of each country. Try reading the same story in two outlets from different countries: you will notice vocabulary differences (*el sismo* and *el terremoto*, *el noticiero* and *el telediario*) and, often, the two habits with the present perfect.

Headlines in Spanish, as in English, are usually written in the present tense, even for past events: *El Gobierno aprueba la nueva ley*, *Un terremoto sacude el sur del país*. The article underneath then switches to the past: in Spain usually the present perfect for that day's events (*ha aprobado*), in Latin America usually the preterite (*aprobó*).

Spanish-language journalists have their own help desk. **FundéuRAE**, a foundation linked to the Real Academia Española and the EFE news agency, publishes short daily recommendations about new words and doubtful usages that appear in the news. Its pages are a good, quick read at B1–B2.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mistakes">

<Misconception>

**"The present perfect is wrong in Latin America" or "the preterite for today is wrong in Spain".** Neither is wrong. They are two standard habits. What matters is consistency and understanding both.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"Las cartas han llegadas."** After *haber* the participle never agrees: *Las cartas han **llegado**.* (It agrees only when used as an adjective: *las cartas están abiertas*.)

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"He ya terminado."** *Haber* and its participle stay together. Put *ya* before or after: ***Ya he terminado*** or *He terminado ya*.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

**"I have lived here for five years" = "He vivido aquí cinco años".** That sentence exists, but for an action that is still going on, Spanish usually prefers the present: ***Vivo aquí desde hace cinco años*** or ***Llevo cinco años viviendo aquí***.

</Misconception>

</Section>
