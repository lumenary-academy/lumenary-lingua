<Section id="intro" kind="motivation" title="From being to doing">

So far your Spanish verbs have been *ser*, *estar* and *tener*: you can say who people are, how they are and what they have. This lesson opens the door to everything people *do*. Spanish verbs come in three families, named after the ending of the infinitive: **-ar** (*hablar*, to speak), **-er** (*comer*, to eat) and **-ir** (*vivir*, to live). Learn one model of each and you can use thousands of regular verbs. Then we'll add two twists, reflexive verbs and stem changes, which are exactly what you need to talk about a day from waking up to going to bed.

</Section>

<Section id="regular" kind="grammar" title="Regular verbs in the present">

To conjugate a regular verb, remove **-ar, -er** or **-ir** to find the stem (*habl-, com-, viv-*), then add the ending for the person.

| | hablar (to speak) | comer (to eat) | vivir (to live) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | habl**o** | com**o** | viv**o** |
| tú | habl**as** | com**es** | viv**es** |
| él, ella, usted | habl**a** | com**e** | viv**e** |
| nosotros, nosotras | habl**amos** | com**emos** | viv**imos** |
| vosotros, vosotras | habl**áis** | com**éis** | viv**ís** |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | habl**an** | com**en** | viv**en** |

Notice that **-er** and **-ir** verbs share all endings except *nosotros* and *vosotros*. The *yo* form always ends in **-o**. (With *vos*, the forms are *hablás, comés, vivís*.)

Some examples: <Say lang="es">Hablo inglés y un poco de español.</Say> <Say lang="es">¿Dónde vives?</Say> <Say lang="es">Mis padres comen a las dos.</Say> <Say lang="es">Leemos mucho.</Say> <Say lang="es">Mi hermana escribe en un periódico.</Say> (My sister writes for a newspaper.)

**One present, three English meanings.** *Trabajo* can mean "I work", "I'm working" or "I do work"; there is no extra word like *do* in questions and negatives: <Say lang="es">¿Trabajas hoy? No, no trabajo hoy.</Say> (Are you working today? No, I'm not working today.) The present can even refer to the near future: <Say lang="es">Mañana estudio.</Say> (Tomorrow I'm studying.)

</Section>

<Section id="reflexive" kind="grammar" title="Reflexive verbs: doing things to yourself">

Many routine verbs are **reflexive**: the action falls back on the person doing it. In the dictionary they end in **-se** (*levantarse*, to get up, literally "to raise oneself"). When you conjugate them, the *-se* becomes a pronoun that matches the subject and goes **before** the verb:

| | levantarse | ducharse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yo | <Say lang="es">me levanto</Say> | <Say lang="es">me ducho</Say> |
| tú | <Say lang="es">te levantas</Say> | <Say lang="es">te duchas</Say> |
| él, ella, usted | <Say lang="es">se levanta</Say> | <Say lang="es">se ducha</Say> |
| nosotros, nosotras | <Say lang="es">nos levantamos</Say> | <Say lang="es">nos duchamos</Say> |
| vosotros, vosotras | <Say lang="es">os levantáis</Say> | <Say lang="es">os ducháis</Say> |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | <Say lang="es">se levantan</Say> | <Say lang="es">se duchan</Say> |

You have used one since lesson 1: <Say lang="es">me llamo</Say> is the verb *llamarse*, "to call oneself". Now you can see why the formal question is *¿Cómo se llama usted?*: *usted* takes *se*, like *él* and *ella*.

The pronoun matters. *Levanto* alone means "I lift" (something else): <Say lang="es">Levanto la mesa.</Say> (I lift the table.) <Say lang="es">Me levanto.</Say> means I get (myself) up.

With parts of the body, Spanish uses the reflexive and **the**, not *my*: <Say lang="es">Me lavo los dientes.</Say> (I brush my teeth.) The *me* already says whose teeth they are.

When a reflexive verb follows another verb in the infinitive, the pronoun can go before the first verb or be attached to the infinitive: <Say lang="es">Quiero levantarme temprano.</Say> = <Say lang="es">Me quiero levantar temprano.</Say> (I want to get up early.)

</Section>

<Section id="stem-changes" kind="grammar" title="Stem-changing verbs">

In some verbs, the vowel of the stem changes when it is stressed. You saw this with *tener* (*tienes*). There are three common patterns:

| | querer (e → ie) | poder (o → ue) | dormir (o → ue) | pedir (e → i) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| yo | qu**ie**ro | p**ue**do | d**ue**rmo | p**i**do |
| tú | qu**ie**res | p**ue**des | d**ue**rmes | p**i**des |
| él, ella, usted | qu**ie**re | p**ue**de | d**ue**rme | p**i**de |
| nosotros, nosotras | queremos | podemos | dormimos | pedimos |
| vosotros, vosotras | queréis | podéis | dormís | pedís |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | qu**ie**ren | p**ue**den | d**ue**rmen | p**i**den |

The change happens in four forms and not in *nosotros* and *vosotros*, where the stress moves to the ending. If you draw a line around the changed forms in the table, it looks like a boot, so teachers often call these "boot verbs". The endings themselves are regular.

Learn each verb with its change, as dictionaries and this course mark it:

- **e → ie:** *querer, empezar* (*empiezo*), *despertarse* (*me despierto*), *preferir* (*prefiero*), *pensar* (*pienso*)
- **o → ue:** *poder, dormir, volver* (*vuelvo*), *almorzar* (*almuerzo*), *acostarse* (*me acuesto*)
- **e → i** (only -ir verbs): *pedir, vestirse* (*me visto*), *servir* (*sirvo*)

*Querer* and *poder* are often followed by an infinitive: <Say lang="es">Quiero dormir.</Say> (I want to sleep.) <Say lang="es">¿Puedes cocinar hoy?</Say> (Can you cook today?) *Empezar* takes *a* before an infinitive: <Say lang="es">Empiezo a trabajar a las ocho.</Say>

</Section>

<Section id="frequency" kind="grammar" title="How often?">

| Spanish | English |
| --- | --- |
| <Say lang="es">siempre</Say> | always |
| <Say lang="es">normalmente</Say>, <Say lang="es">generalmente</Say> | usually |
| <Say lang="es">todos los días</Say>, <Say lang="es">cada día</Say> | every day |
| <Say lang="es">a menudo</Say> | often (Mexico also *seguido*) |
| <Say lang="es">a veces</Say> | sometimes |
| <Say lang="es">una vez a la semana</Say> | once a week (also *una vez por semana*) |
| <Say lang="es">dos veces al día</Say> | twice a day (*al* = *a* + *el*; more in lesson 6) |
| <Say lang="es">casi nunca</Say> | hardly ever |
| <Say lang="es">nunca</Say> | never |

Most of these can go at the start or the end of the sentence, or right before the verb: <Say lang="es">Normalmente desayuno a las siete.</Say> <Say lang="es">Corro dos veces a la semana.</Say>

**Nunca** has two positions. Before the verb, it stands alone: <Say lang="es">Nunca como carne.</Say> After the verb, Spanish needs *no* before the verb as well: <Say lang="es">No como carne nunca.</Say> Both mean "I never eat meat". This "double negative" is correct and normal in Spanish.

</Section>

<Section id="dialogue" kind="dialogue" title="Two flatmates, two clocks">

In Santiago de Chile, Aisha, a student from Egypt, shares a flat with Daniel, a nurse who sometimes works nights. It's Sunday evening.

| | Spanish | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aisha | <Say lang="es">Daniel, ¿a qué hora te levantas mañana?</Say> | Daniel, what time are you getting up tomorrow? |
| Daniel | <Say lang="es">Mañana no me levanto temprano. Esta semana trabajo por la noche.</Say> | I'm not getting up early tomorrow. This week I'm working nights. |
| Aisha | <Say lang="es">¿Y cuándo duermes?</Say> | And when do you sleep? |
| Daniel | <Say lang="es">Duermo por la mañana, de ocho a tres. Luego almuerzo y descanso.</Say> | I sleep in the morning, from eight to three. Then I have lunch and rest. |
| Aisha | <Say lang="es">Yo me despierto a las seis y media. Me ducho, desayuno y tomo el metro a las siete y media.</Say> | I wake up at half past six. I shower, have breakfast and take the metro at half past seven. |
| Daniel | <Say lang="es">¡Qué temprano! ¿A qué hora empiezan las clases?</Say> | So early! What time do your classes start? |
| Aisha | <Say lang="es">A las ocho y media. Siempre llego un poco tarde.</Say> | At half past eight. I always arrive a bit late. |
| Daniel | <Say lang="es">¿Y por la tarde?</Say> | And in the afternoon? |
| Aisha | <Say lang="es">Estudio en la biblioteca y a veces corro en el parque. Nunca miro la tele.</Say> | I study in the library and sometimes I run in the park. I never watch TV. |
| Daniel | <Say lang="es">Yo sí, cuando no puedo dormir. ¿Cenamos juntos hoy?</Say> | I do, when I can't sleep. Shall we have dinner together tonight? |
| Aisha | <Say lang="es">¡Sí! ¿Cocinas tú? Yo lavo los platos.</Say> | Yes! Will you cook? I'll wash the dishes. |
| Daniel | <Say lang="es">Trato hecho.</Say> | Deal. |

**Notes.** *Esta semana* = this week. *Juntos* = together. *¿Cenamos juntos?* uses the present to suggest something, like English "Shall we…?". *Yo sí* means "I do" (contrasting with Aisha). *Trato hecho* (literally "deal done") is a fixed phrase. Notice *lavo los platos* (I wash the dishes) without *me*: Aisha is washing something else, not herself.

</Section>

<Section id="pronunciation" kind="pronunciation" title="Stress in verb forms">

Verb forms follow the stress rules from lesson 1, and getting the stress right matters, because later in the course you'll meet past-tense forms that differ only in stress.

- *hablo, hablas, habla, hablan* end in a vowel, -s or -n, so the stress falls on the second-to-last syllable: <Say lang="es">hablo</Say> (HA-blo), <Say lang="es">hablan</Say> (HA-blan). Don't stress the ending.
- *hablamos, comemos, vivimos*: also second-to-last: <Say lang="es">hablamos</Say> (ha-BLA-mos), <Say lang="es">vivimos</Say> (vi-VI-mos).
- the *vosotros* forms carry an accent: <Say lang="es">habláis</Say>, <Say lang="es">coméis</Say>, <Say lang="es">vivís</Say>.
- in stem-changing verbs, the new diphthong carries the stress: <Say lang="es">quiero</Say> (KYEH-ro), <Say lang="es">puedo</Say> (PWEH-do), <Say lang="es">duermo</Say> (DWER-mo). Say each as two syllables, not three.

Reflexive pronouns run into the verb with no break: <Say lang="es">me levanto</Say> sounds like one word, "melevanto".

</Section>

<Section id="culture" kind="culture" title="Siesta, sobremesa and merienda">

The *siesta*, a nap after lunch, is famous, but in big cities today most people with regular jobs don't take one on weekdays; it survives more on weekends, in the summer heat, in small towns, and among people who can organise their own time. What does shape the day in many places is the long midday meal. In Spain and Mexico *la comida* is often the main meal of the day, and the time spent talking at the table afterwards has its own name: *la sobremesa*. Rushing away from the table can seem a little rude.

Because dinner is late in Spain and in Argentina, many people have *la merienda* in the late afternoon: a snack of bread, fruit, a pastry or a sandwich, often with coffee. In Argentina, Uruguay and Paraguay you'll also see people carrying a thermos and a gourd for *mate*, a herbal infusion that is passed around and shared throughout the day.

</Section>

<Section id="misconceptions" kind="misconceptions" title="Common mix-ups">

<Misconception>

"*Levanto a las siete* means I get up at seven." Without the pronoun it means "I lift (something) at seven". Reflexive verbs need their pronoun: **Me levanto a las siete**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"*Querer* changes everywhere: *quieremos*." The stem changes only when it is stressed, so *nosotros* and *vosotros* keep the original vowel: **queremos, queréis; podemos; dormimos; pedimos**.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"'I'm eating' needs a special form, as in English." Usually it doesn't: *Como* means both "I eat" and "I'm eating". Spanish does have a form like "I am eating", which you'll learn in lesson 12, but the simple present is used much more.

</Misconception>

<Misconception>

"Two negatives make a positive." Not in Spanish: *No como carne nunca* is perfectly correct, just like *Nunca como carne*, and both mean "I never eat meat".

</Misconception>

</Section>
